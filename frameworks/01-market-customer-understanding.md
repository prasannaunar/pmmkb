# Category 1: Market & Customer Understanding

## Segmentation–Targeting–Positioning (STP) Framework

**What it is:** A three-step strategic framework that first divides the overall market into distinct customer segments (Segmentation), then selects the most attractive segments to pursue (Targeting), and finally defines how your product will be perceived within those chosen segments (Positioning). STP is foundational—it underpins all downstream messaging, pricing, and go-to-market decisions.

**When to use it:** Use STP at the start of any product marketing effort, when entering a new market or segment, or when sales plateaus suggest your current positioning no longer resonates. It answers: "Who is our customer? Why them? What do we stand for in their eyes?" Apply it quarterly during competitive reviews or when product changes shift your value proposition.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Segmentation** – Divide your addressable market by attributes: industry, company size, job title, buying behavior, budget, or problem urgency. Create 4–6 distinct segments with clear characteristics.
2. **Targeting** – Evaluate each segment on three criteria: Size (TAM/SAM), Growth rate, and Competitive intensity. Rank segments and select your primary target(s). Choose 1–2 primary segments (not 5—focus wins).
3. **Positioning** – For each target segment, define: What problem do they face? How does your solution uniquely solve it? What proof validates that claim? Craft one clear positioning statement per segment.
4. **Validate & Refine** – Test positioning with 10–15 target customer interviews. Does it resonate? Does it differentiate from competitors? Refine based on feedback.
5. **Align Operations** – Ensure product roadmap, pricing, messaging, and sales process all reflect your segment choice and positioning.

**Example:** A B2B SaaS expense management tool initially targeted "all companies." Segmentation revealed three distinct segments: (1) Mid-market finance teams (1000–5000 employees), (2) Startups (10–50 employees), (3) Enterprise (5000+). Targeting analysis showed startups had the lowest CAC and fastest sales cycle. Positioning pivoted from "enterprise-grade compliance" to "spend visibility for fast-growing teams." Sales cycle dropped 30%, CAC payback improved to 9 months, and NPS climbed from 32 to 52 in the startup segment.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Chasing too many segments.** "We'll target mid-market and enterprise" dilutes messaging and spreads sales effort thin. Pick one primary segment and own it before expanding.
- **Positioning that doesn't differentiate.** "We're an expense management tool" describes the category, not your position. State what makes you unique: "Built for startup founders who want real-time spend visibility without finance team overhead."

**See also:** Geoffrey Moore's Positioning Statement Framework (articulate your positioning statement for each segment); Value Proposition Canvas (map your offering to what each segment needs); Jobs-to-be-Done Positioning Framework (understand what job each segment is trying to accomplish).

## Complete Product Experience Framework

**What it is:** A seven-touchpoint model that ensures your positioning is reinforced consistently across every customer interaction: Marketing, Sales, Product/Features, Technology, Supporting Systems, Third-party Integrations, and Support. The framework recognizes that positioning isn't just messaging—it's the sum of how customers experience your company at every stage.

**When to use it:** Use this when you notice misalignment between what you claim and what customers experience. Common triggers: sales says "we're easy to implement" but onboarding takes 12 weeks; marketing says "enterprise-grade" but support is slow; product roadmap ignores customer pain points from win/loss interviews. Apply it quarterly as a coherence check.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Define your positioning** – Start with your one-sentence positioning statement and three core benefits.
2. **Map touchpoints** – For each of the seven areas, document the current experience:
   - **Marketing:** Website copy, case studies, webinars—do they convey the positioning?
   - **Sales:** Discovery calls, demos, proposals—do they reinforce the same message?
   - **Product/Features:** Does the UI/UX reflect the positioning? Are feature priorities aligned?
   - **Technology:** Is your stack reliable enough to support the promises you make?
   - **Supporting Systems:** Do onboarding, training, documentation reinforce positioning?
   - **Integrations:** Do your native integrations and partnership ecosystem support the positioning?
   - **Support:** Do support response times, quality, and problem-solving match customer expectations set by positioning?
3. **Identify gaps** – Where does reality diverge from positioning? Flag mismatches.
4. **Prioritize fixes** – Fix the gaps that hurt most: usually Sales (if demo doesn't match claims), Product (if features don't deliver on promises), or Support (if issue resolution is slow).
5. **Iterate** – Refresh quarterly. As you improve one area, new gaps may emerge elsewhere.

**Example:** A project management SaaS claimed "designed for remote teams" but had three gaps: (1) Marketing showed desktop-only features; Product roadmap had no mobile app; Support lacked timezone coverage. Customer feedback confirmed frustration: "You say remote-first, but your app doesn't work on my phone." Fix priority: (1) Build mobile, (2) Hire 24/7 support, (3) Update marketing. Six months later, NPS climbed 18 points and churn dropped 5 percentage points.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Assuming marketing drives the experience.** Positioning isn't what you say; it's what customers experience. A great website can't overcome a bad product or slow support.
- **Fixing marketing without fixing operations.** "We'll just update the website" doesn't solve an 8-week onboarding or missing features. Fix the core experience first.

**See also:** Geoffrey Moore's Positioning Statement Framework (define your positioning statement clearly before mapping touchpoints); Value Proposition Canvas (ensure touchpoints reflect your value proposition); PMM Lifecycle Management Framework (applies positioning consistently across customer lifecycle stages).

## Product Development Stage Framework

**What it is:** A process-oriented framework that guides product marketers through key activities during different phases of product development: ideation, prototype, beta, launch, and maturity. It defines PMM's role at each stage (research, validation, messaging, enablement) and ensures market insights inform product decisions before launch.

**When to use it:** Use this as a playbook when launching new products, major features, or entering new markets. It answers: "What should product marketing be doing right now?" Apply it from day one of a new initiative—too many companies start PMM work after product launch, when it's too late to influence the most important decisions.

**How to apply it:**

**Phase 1: Ideation & Research (Weeks 1–4)**
- Conduct customer discovery: What problem does this solve? Who has it? How urgent is it?
- Analyze competitive landscape: Who else solves this? How will we differ?
- Estimate TAM and pricing sensitivity
- Output: Customer research doc, problem validation, rough positioning hypothesis

**Phase 2: Prototype & Validation (Weeks 5–12)**
- Share prototype with 15–20 target customers; validate problem-solution fit
- Refine positioning based on feedback
- Identify go-to-market risks (e.g., "No one will pay for this" or "Sales can't explain it")
- Output: Validated positioning, refined ICP, early case study customer identified

**Phase 3: Beta & Messaging (Weeks 13–20)**
- Recruit 5–10 beta customers for deep feedback
- Draft complete positioning statement, value proposition, and key messages
- Create 3–5 early case study scenarios
- Build initial sales enablement: demo script, objection handling, one-pager
- Output: Final positioning, messaging playbook, draft collateral

**Phase 4: Launch (Week 21+)**
- Coordinated go-to-market: simultaneous messaging across website, sales, PR, events
- Full collateral suite: landing page, case studies, webinar, email campaign
- Sales enablement training and kickoff
- Output: Go-to-market execution, customer wins, initial demand

**Phase 5: Maturity & Optimization (Months 4+)**
- Monitor win rates, sales feedback, customer questions
- Iterate messaging based on win/loss analysis
- Expand positioning to adjacent segments
- Output: Refined positioning, segment-specific messaging, upsell strategy

**Example:** A data infrastructure startup followed this framework for a new "real-time analytics" product. Phase 1 interviews revealed customers spent 40% of their day waiting for reports. Phase 2 prototype testing confirmed willingness to pay. Phase 3 beta customers (Uber, Stripe) provided names for case studies. Phase 4 launch message: "See your data in seconds, not hours." First month: 120 trials, 8 paid customers. By month 6, product had 200 customers and $150K ARR.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Skipping phases to move faster.** Launching without Phase 2 validation (problem-solution fit) often means building for a market that doesn't exist or at a price no one will pay.
- **PMM joining too late (Phase 4).** If marketing joins only at launch, the product may already be un-positionable. Involvement from Phase 1 prevents costly misalignment.

**See also:** Complete GTM Workflow Stages (sequences phases from Foundation through Expand with go-to-market focus); STP Framework (segment and position before Phase 3 to ensure product roadmap aligns with target market); Jobs-to-be-Done Positioning Framework (identify the core job customers are hiring your product to do during Phase 1 research).
