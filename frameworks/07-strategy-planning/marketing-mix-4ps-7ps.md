# Marketing Mix (4Ps and 7Ps)

**What it is:** A descriptive map of the decisions a company controls when it takes one offer to one target segment. Neil Borden coined the term "marketing mix" for the set of ingredients a marketing manager blends, and E. Jerome McCarthy grouped those ingredients into four headings in *Basic Marketing* (1960): **product** (what you sell, including features, quality, packaging and support), **price** (what the buyer pays and how: list price, discounts, terms, the unit you charge for), **place** (how the offer reaches the buyer: channels, coverage, partners) and **promotion** (how you tell the market: messaging, advertising, content, sales conversations, PR). In 1981 Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner argued that services need three more: **people** (everyone who delivers the service and shapes the buyer's experience of it), **process** (the steps, flow and time it takes to receive the service) and **physical evidence** (the tangible cues a buyer uses to judge something they cannot touch before buying). Together these are the 7Ps. The mix does not tell you what to decide on any single lever; it shows you every lever at once, so you can see whether the decisions agree with each other and with the segment you chose. In this knowledge base it is the overview page: each P routes to a deeper entry that does the detailed work.

**When to use it:**
- **When a new product, tier or segment needs a commercial plan** and you need one page that shows leadership every lever before the detailed work starts.
- **When results lag and nobody can say why.** A mismatch between levers (a premium price sold through a discount channel, enterprise messaging on a self-serve funnel) is a common cause that a single-lever review misses.
- **When entering a new segment or geography** with an existing product, to list which Ps change and which carry over.
- **When a SaaS or services offer wins demos but loses on onboarding or trust.** That is a signal to extend from the 4Ps to the 7Ps and look at people, process and physical evidence.
- **When onboarding a new PMM or agency** who needs a fast, shared picture of how an offer goes to market.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company, leadership signs off the mix, but no single function owns it end to end: product management owns product decisions, a pricing lead or finance owns price, the CRO owns channel and sales coverage, and marketing owns promotion. PMM typically assembles the mix document, supplies the segment and positioning inputs, and flags where levers conflict. At a solo or founding-PMM stage, the PMM often owns the whole mix outright with the founder, because the same person is writing the pricing page, choosing the first channels and drafting the launch messaging. As the company grows, PMM keeps the assembly and consistency role while individual Ps move to specialist owners.

**How to read it:** Read the mix in two directions. **Down each P**, ask whether the decision is made, evidenced and owned; an empty or "to be confirmed" cell is a gap, not a neutral state. **Across the Ps**, ask whether they tell one story to one segment. A consistent mix is one where a buyer meeting any lever would guess the others: a $40,000 annual contract implies a sales-led channel, proof-heavy promotion and a named onboarding contact. An inconsistent mix is one where one lever contradicts the rest. Use the 4Ps for physical or simple self-serve products. Use the 7Ps for services and for most B2B SaaS, because the buyer cannot judge the product before purchase, so implementation people, onboarding process and proof such as security reports and reviews do real selling work.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Fix the offer and segment first.** The mix is set for one offer and one target segment at a time. If the segment is unclear, run the Segmentation–Targeting–Positioning (STP) Framework before filling any P.
2. **Product.** Write down the core offer, the "whole product" around it and the features that matter to this segment. Use the Complete Product Experience Framework to define what the buyer actually receives and the Kano Model to separate expected features from differentiators.
3. **Price.** Record the price point, the value metric and the packaging. Route the detailed work to the Value Metric / Willingness-to-Pay Framework for the charging unit, the Good-Better-Best (GBB) Packaging Framework for tiers, and the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM) for an acceptable price range.
4. **Place.** Record how buyers find, evaluate and buy. Use the GTM Motion Model to choose between self-serve, product-led, inside sales, field sales and partner channel, and the Bullseye Framework (Traction Channel Selection) to test which acquisition channels to prioritise.
5. **Promotion.** Record the core message, proof points and launch weight. Use Message Architecture (Messaging House) for the message, the Buyer's Journey Content Map for content by stage, and the Launch Tier Framework (Tier 1/2/3) to size the launch.
6. **People, process and physical evidence (7Ps).** For services and SaaS, record who the buyer meets after signature, the onboarding steps and time to first value, and the proof a buyer sees before buying. Use the Time to Value Framework and the Customer Onboarding Maturity Framework for process, and the Forrester Customer Advocacy Model to build reference proof.
7. **Run the consistency check.** Put all seven on one page and test each pair against the segment. Name every conflict, assign an owner and a date to resolve it, and get leadership sign-off on the full page, not lever by lever.
8. **Set one metric per P and review quarterly.** For example: activation rate (product), win rate at list price (price), pipeline by channel (place), message recall or reply rate (promotion), time to first value (process). A lever that misses its metric for two quarters is the first one to reopen.

**Example:** Fernhill, a fictional accounts-payable automation SaaS company, sold a $9,000-per-year plan to mid-market finance teams through a three-person inside sales team. In 2025 it decided to target enterprise finance teams. The first plan changed only two Ps: a new $60,000 enterprise tier (price) and a new "built for the global finance team" campaign (promotion). After two quarters, enterprise pipeline reached $2.1 million but win rate was 9%, against 27% in mid-market. The PMM laid out the full 7Ps and found three conflicts. Place: enterprise buyers expected a field rep and a procurement process, but deals still ran through inside sales on 30-day cycles. Process: onboarding was the mid-market self-guided checklist, with a median 41 days to first paid invoice. Physical evidence: there was no SOC 2 report and no enterprise reference customer, and security reviews stalled 6 of 11 late-stage deals. Leadership signed off a revised mix: two field reps and a partner with a large audit firm (place), a named implementation lead and a 30-day onboarding plan (people and process), and a SOC 2 Type II report plus two referenceable customers (physical evidence). Over the next three quarters, enterprise win rate rose to 22% and median time to first paid invoice fell to 24 days.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Treating the mix as a checklist of separate decisions.** Teams fill each P in a different meeting with a different owner and never read across the page, so a premium price ships with a discount-led channel. The cost is a launch where every lever looks reasonable and the whole still fails. Recovery: make step 7 a required sign-off, with every pair of Ps checked against the segment before anything ships.
- **Stopping at the 4Ps for a service or SaaS offer.** A team that sets product, price, place and promotion well can still lose deals in security review or churn in onboarding, because people, process and physical evidence were never planned. Recovery: default to the 7Ps for any offer the buyer cannot fully judge before purchase, and track time to first value and proof coverage alongside pipeline.
- **Using the mix in place of the deeper work.** Writing "price: $60,000" in the price cell is a placeholder, not a pricing decision. A mix filled from opinion gives leadership false confidence that every lever has been tested. Recovery: mark each cell as evidenced or assumed, and route every assumed cell to its deeper entry (step 2 to step 6) before sign-off.
