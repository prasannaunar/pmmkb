# Category Quiz: Competitive Strategy

1. A board asks "where do we sit versus the competition?" for the second time after a detailed feature-comparison spreadsheet failed to land in the first briefing. The team needs a fast, visual way to show the market's structure on a dimension buyers actually care about. Which tool should the PMM reach for?
   - **A.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **B.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **C.** CI Program Maturity Model
   - **D.** A Category Design bet

   **Correct answer: A.** The Perceptual Map is purpose-built for exactly this situation: a fast, visual read on where genuine open space exists, built from axes buyers actually care about, when a checklist of feature comparisons has already failed to communicate the finding.

   *Why not B:* Bowman's Strategic Clock is the closest neighbour to this tool, but it always uses the same two fixed axes, price and perceived value; it answers a price-to-value defensibility question, not a general "where do we sit" briefing built on whichever buyer attributes matter most here.

   *Why not C:* The CI Program Maturity Model diagnoses the health of the competitive intelligence function itself, not the company's market position; it would not produce the visual comparison the board is asking for.

   *Why not D:* A Category Design bet is a multi-year strategic commitment to reframe the market entirely, far larger than what a board's positioning question calls for here.

2. A B2B SaaS company has just lost three competitive deals in a row to a new entrant. Sales reports that prospects keep citing the competitor's messaging around speed of deployment, and internal reviews confirm the product is not meaningfully behind on this dimension, it has simply never been positioned around it. Which framework should the PMM prioritise?
   - **A.** Differentiation Strategy
   - **B.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **C.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **D.** CI Program Maturity Model

   **Correct answer: A.** This is a differentiation gap: a real strength exists but has not been identified, scored for meaningfulness, uniqueness, and defensibility, and embedded into messaging and sales conversations. The framework exists precisely to turn an unclaimed strength into an operational differentiator across product, pricing, and messaging.

   *Why not B:* Nothing in this scenario describes a price-to-value mismatch; the product's price has not changed and value has not visibly dropped. The issue is an unclaimed differentiator, not a defensibility problem on the price-value axis.

   *Why not C:* A Perceptual Map could visualise this gap on the right axes, but it is a diagnostic snapshot, not the framework that builds the operational commitment, roadmap alignment, and sales enablement needed to actually close it.

   *Why not D:* The CI Program Maturity Model would help if the underlying problem were stale or poorly distributed competitive intelligence; here the intelligence is already known internally, the gap is in positioning and alignment, not the CI function.

3. A company already wins the majority of its head-to-head competitive deals and has a well-regarded differentiation story. Despite this, analysts and investors keep asking "so who do you actually take share from?", and revenue growth has plateaued because the existing category's overall ceiling is capped, regardless of the company's share within it. Which is the appropriate next step?
   - **A.** A Category Design bet
   - **B.** Differentiation Strategy
   - **C.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **D.** Bowman's Strategic Clock

   **Correct answer: A.** This is exactly the trigger the methodology names: out-executing competitors inside the existing category is not the constraint, since the company is already winning; the category itself has a capped ceiling. That calls for the multi-year, executive-sponsored bet of defining a new category, not further optimisation within the old one.

   *Why not B:* The company already has a strong, well-regarded differentiation story; sharpening it further stays inside a category whose ceiling is the actual constraint here. This is the right kind of strengthening work aimed at the wrong scope: a capped category, not an unclaimed strength, is the problem.

   *Why not C:* A Perceptual Map would show where the company sits within the existing category's attributes; it cannot address a ceiling created by the category definition itself, which requires reframing the buyer's problem entirely.

   *Why not D:* Bowman's Clock addresses whether a price-to-value position is defensible; the scenario describes a market-size and framing ceiling, not a pricing or value-perception problem.

4. Sales reps say competitive intelligence is stale, hard to find, and sometimes contradicts what they hear live in deals. Leadership assumes the underlying research must be poor quality. Which tool should the PMM use to properly diagnose this?
   - **A.** CI Program Maturity Model
   - **B.** A Category Design bet
   - **C.** Differentiation Strategy
   - **D.** Bowman's Strategic Clock

   **Correct answer: A.** This is a textbook symptom the model is designed to diagnose: stale, hard-to-find, contradictory intel almost always points to a function stuck at Ad Hoc or Adopting on repository, process, and distribution, not a research-quality problem. Scoring the four dimensions reveals exactly where the gap sits.

   *Why not B:* A Category Design bet addresses market and category framing, an entirely different problem from whether the CI function's internal processes and distribution are mature enough to serve sales reliably.

   *Why not C:* Differentiation Strategy builds and embeds a competitive advantage into the product and messaging; it does not diagnose whether the underlying competitive intelligence operation itself is functioning well.

   *Why not D:* Bowman's Strategic Clock is a natural downstream beneficiary of a mature CI function, since fresher, more frequent data feeds it, but it diagnoses price-to-value positioning, not whether the CI process producing that data is itself mature and well distributed.

5. During a round of enterprise renewals, several long-standing customers say for the first time that the price no longer feels justified, even though nothing in the contract terms has changed. Which framework directly addresses this?
   - **A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **B.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **C.** A Category Design bet
   - **D.** CI Program Maturity Model

   **Correct answer: A.** A shift in perceived value with price held constant is precisely the price-to-value mismatch Bowman's Strategic Clock is built to diagnose; pricing renegotiations that reveal customers no longer see the value they once did are one of its named triggers.

   *Why not B:* A Perceptual Map uses buyer-chosen axes picked fresh each time; it is the right family of visual, buyer-grounded tool, but not the fixed price-and-value diagnostic this specific renewal-pricing problem calls for.

   *Why not C:* A Category Design bet addresses whether the market frame itself is limiting growth; a shift in perceived value during renewals is a narrower, more immediate pricing and positioning question, not a category-reframing one.

   *Why not D:* The CI Program Maturity Model diagnoses the competitive intelligence function's own maturity; it has no direct bearing on a customer's changing perception of value during a renewal conversation.

6. A team needs to determine whether their current price is defensible against what customers perceive their product to be worth, using a fixed, repeatable diagnostic they can re-run every two quarters. Which tool is purpose-built for this, rather than one that would require choosing new axes each time?
   - **A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **B.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **C.** Differentiation Strategy
   - **D.** A Category Design bet

   **Correct answer: A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock always uses the same two axes, price and perceived value, which is exactly what makes it repeatable and directly comparable across re-plotting cycles; the Perceptual Map, by contrast, is deliberately axis-agnostic and its dimensions are chosen fresh each time.

   *Why not B:* The Perceptual Map's strength is flexibility: choosing whichever two buyer-relevant attributes matter most for a given question. That flexibility makes it less suited to a fixed, repeatable price-versus-value tracking exercise than the clock's stable axes.

   *Why not C:* Differentiation Strategy identifies and embeds a competitive advantage; it does not test price-to-value defensibility directly or provide a repeatable two-axis diagnostic.

   *Why not D:* A Category Design bet operates at the level of market framing, not a repeatable price-value tracking exercise; it is a multi-year strategic bet, not a quarterly diagnostic.

7. A team has just validated a white-space finding with a Perceptual Map: buyers place them in an unoccupied quadrant they genuinely want. What should happen next?
   - **A.** Move to Differentiation Strategy to embed the validated attributes operationally.
   - **B.** Treat the map itself as the finished positioning deliverable and move to the next project.
   - **C.** Re-run Bowman's Strategic Clock using the map's two axes instead of price and value.
   - **D.** Immediately launch a category design programme built around the white-space quadrant.

   **Correct answer: A.** A validated map is explicitly an input to further work, not a finished positioning statement; the natural next step is operationalising the finding, embedding it into the roadmap, pricing, messaging, and sales process the way Differentiation Strategy's later steps describe.

   *Why not B:* The entry is explicit that the map is a diagnostic to run in an afternoon, not a replacement for the deeper positioning or differentiation work that must follow a validated finding.

   *Why not C:* Bowman's Clock's axes are fixed by design, price and perceived value, specifically to test whether a competitive strategy is defensible; substituting the map's chosen axes changes what the clock is meant to test and is not how the two tools are meant to work together.

   *Why not D:* A single validated white-space finding does not by itself meet the bar for a multi-year, executive-sponsored category bet; that requires the additional evidence and executive sponsorship Category Design's own first step calls for.

8. A company has strong, well-embedded differentiation, confirmed through win-loss data, sales alignment, and consistent execution across every touchpoint. Growth has still plateaued, and analysts keep framing the company as a challenger inside a category it has already proven it can win consistently. What does this combination of facts suggest?
   - **A.** The differentiation work has succeeded, but the ceiling is now the category itself.
   - **B.** The differentiation strategy has failed and should be rebuilt from the MUD scoring stage.
   - **C.** A Perceptual Map should be run to find a new differentiator to replace the current one.
   - **D.** The CI Program Maturity Model should be applied to find why analysts still call it a challenger.

   **Correct answer: A.** Strong, validated differentiation combined with a plateau caused by the category's own ceiling, not competitive losses, is precisely the pattern that separates a differentiation problem from a category-framing problem; it is the trigger for considering the larger, multi-year Category Design bet.

   *Why not B:* The facts describe differentiation that is working, confirmed by win-loss data and consistent execution; there is no evidence the MUD scoring or execution has failed, which rules out rebuilding it from scratch.

   *Why not C:* This misreads the evidence: win-loss data and consistent execution show the current differentiator is still working. A new one would still operate inside the same category ceiling, since the constraint here is structural to the category, not a sign differentiation has stopped working.

   *Why not D:* Analyst framing here reflects the category's definition, not the competitive intelligence function's maturity; CI process quality is not what is causing analysts to view the company as a challenger.

9. A newly appointed CI lead scores the function as Impacting because the team recently produced one excellent, thorough battlecard for the company's top-named competitor. Four other competitors that come up regularly in live deals have no current materials at all. What is the flaw in this self-assessment?
   - **A.** The function should be scored on typical coverage, not its single strongest deliverable.
   - **B.** The score is correct; reaching Impacting only requires one high-quality example.
   - **C.** The Impacting score can stand, as long as the four gaps are logged as a follow-up.
   - **D.** The scoring exercise cannot be trusted unless it is conducted by an outside consultant.

   **Correct answer: A.** Scoring on the best deliverable rather than typical performance is a named pitfall of the model: a single excellent battlecard does not make the whole function Impacting if several regularly relevant competitors have nothing current at all. An honest score reflects coverage across the competitors that actually come up in live deals.

   *Why not B:* One strong example is explicitly insufficient evidence under the model; the four uncovered competitors are exactly where the real gap, and the real risk in live deals, will show up.

   *Why not C:* Logging the gap is a good habit, but it does not fix the score itself: a function with four regularly relevant competitors uncovered is not yet Impacting, whatever follow-up gets logged alongside it. The score has to reflect typical coverage now, not a plan to close the gap later.

   *Why not D:* The model does not require external validation; the fix here is applying the model's own scoring guidance honestly against typical coverage, which the internal team is capable of doing itself.

10. A company runs a successful category design launch, complete with a published Point of View, analyst briefings, and two flagship customer references. Six months later, the team is fully reassigned to other projects. A competitor then begins using the same category term without having done any of the underlying research. What was the failure?
    - **A.** The company fully resourced Define and Develop but abandoned Dominate entirely.
    - **B.** The team should have kept publishing new research even after moving to other projects.
    - **C.** The category name should have been trademarked before launch to prevent competitor use.
    - **D.** The analyst briefings should have been replaced with a larger paid advertising budget.

    **Correct answer: A.** Dominate is meant to run for years, not one launch cycle, through sustained evangelism, defending the term as imitators appear, and refreshing the point of view as the market evolves. Disbanding the team after a successful launch is exactly how a well-resourced competitor can adopt the vocabulary with none of the original research or credibility behind it.

    *Why not B:* More research would have helped, but Dominate is broader than continued publishing: it also means active evangelism and defending the term as competitors try to use it. Publishing alone, without a team sustaining the wider effort, still leaves the vocabulary open to being co-opted.

    *Why not C:* Category names describing a market problem are generally not defensible through trademark in the way this situation would require; the real defence is sustained, ongoing evangelism and vocabulary ownership, not a legal filing.

    *Why not D:* Advertising does not build the credibility that ongoing analyst relationships and flagship customer proof provide; the entry favours sustained relationship-based seeding over advertising, and the failure here is stopping that work entirely, not the choice of channel.
