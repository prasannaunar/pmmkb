# Quiz: Category Design

1. A Head of Product Marketing wants to pursue category design because a competitor recently coined a catchy new term for its market. Win-loss interviews, however, show the company already wins the majority of head-to-head deals inside its existing category. What should the PMM recommend?
   - **A.** Test whether the category ceiling is genuinely capped before considering category design at all.
   - **B.** Draft a Point of View and a brand-new category name right away, to match the competitor's new move.
   - **C.** Take no action on the competitor's new term and leave strategy and messaging as they are.
   - **D.** Ask engineering to build a genuinely new product category before any positioning decisions.

   **Correct answer: A.** Category design is a multi-year, executive-sponsored bet reserved for situations where out-executing competitors inside the current category would still leave a capped ceiling. A company already winning most head-to-head deals should check that test first, since the evidence here points to a differentiation opportunity, not a category-reframing one.

   *Why not B:* This jumps straight to Define before running the actual test the methodology calls for first, whether the existing category's ceiling is genuinely capped. Skipping that check risks committing years of budget to a bet the underlying win-loss data does not yet support.

   *Why not C:* Ignoring the move entirely skips the diagnostic step; the right response is to check the evidence first, not to default to inaction any more than to overreaction.

   *Why not D:* Category design does not require a new product first; it requires a genuine reframing of an already-real problem. Building new features on the assumption a new category is warranted skips the evidence-gathering step entirely.

2. A PMM is drafting a Point of View for a proposed new category and writes the "Legendary Problem" based on what the leadership team believes the market should care about, without checking it against recent win-loss interviews. What risk does this create?
   - **A.** The category claim will likely read as a marketing wrapper the first time a sceptical buyer tests it.
   - **B.** This carries little risk; executive conviction is what mainly earns a new category its credibility here.
   - **C.** The main risk is that the category name will be difficult for sales to pronounce in a live pitch.
   - **D.** The bigger risk shows up later, when a well-resourced competitor tries to co-opt the term.

   **Correct answer: A.** The Legendary Problem must come from evidence: win-loss interviews, Voice of the Customer signal, and discovery calls, not from what the team decides buyers should care about. A category invented from internal ambition rather than evidenced pain collapses the first time a sceptical buyer or analyst tests it against their own experience.

   *Why not B:* Executive sponsorship is necessary for credibility and follow-through, but it does not substitute for evidence that buyers actually experience the named problem; a category with strong internal conviction and no buyer evidence still fails externally.

   *Why not C:* Pronounceability is a minor packaging concern; the structural risk here is that the entire premise of the category rests on an unvalidated assumption about buyer pain.

   *Why not D:* Competitor co-option is a real risk, but it belongs to the Dominate phase, years after launch. The immediate problem in this scenario is upstream of that: an unvalidated Legendary Problem that will not survive a sceptical buyer's first test, well before a competitor ever gets the chance to copy it.

3. A company successfully launches a new category with strong analyst briefings and two flagship customer references, then redirects the team to other projects six months later. A competitor starts using the same category term without doing any of the underlying research. What went wrong?
   - **A.** The company fully resourced Define and Develop but abandoned Dominate entirely.
   - **B.** The team should have kept publishing new research even after moving to other projects.
   - **C.** The analyst briefings should have been replaced with a larger paid advertising budget.
   - **D.** The category name should have been trademarked before launch to prevent competitor use.

   **Correct answer: A.** Dominate is meant to run for years, not one launch cycle: sustained evangelism, defending the term as imitators appear, and refreshing the point of view as the market evolves. Stopping after a successful launch is exactly how a well-resourced competitor can adopt the vocabulary with none of the original research behind it.

   *Why not B:* More research would have helped, but Dominate is broader than continued publishing: it also means active evangelism and defending the term as competitors try to use it. Publishing alone, without a team sustaining the wider effort, still leaves the vocabulary open to being co-opted.

   *Why not C:* Advertising does not build the credibility that analyst relationships and flagship customer proof provide; the entry specifically favours those over advertising for seeding a new category. The failure here is stopping the work, not the choice of channel.

   *Why not D:* Category names describing a market problem are generally not defensible through a trademark or patent in the way a competitor's co-opting of common vocabulary would require; the real defence is sustained, ongoing evangelism, not a legal filing.

4. A team updates its homepage headline to use a newly invented category name, without publishing any supporting research or securing a flagship customer reference. What is the most likely outcome?
   - **A.** A sceptical buyer who tests the claim will find nothing behind it.
   - **B.** The category claim will succeed regardless; a strong headline is what mainly drives adoption.
   - **C.** The claim will fail only if a competitor immediately contests the new term publicly.
   - **D.** This is a faster, equally valid alternative to the full Define, Develop, Dominate process.

   **Correct answer: A.** Swapping a headline to a new category name without the underlying evidence base, published Point of View, and at least one flagship reference is a named pitfall: it fools no one and can damage credibility once a sceptical buyer or analyst tests the claim and finds nothing substantive behind it.

   *Why not B:* A headline alone cannot manufacture the credibility a real category claim requires; without proof and flagship validation, the claim collapses under any real scrutiny regardless of how compelling the headline reads.

   *Why not C:* The claim is vulnerable the moment any buyer, analyst, or prospect tests it directly, not only if a competitor happens to contest it publicly. Narrowing the risk to a competitor's public challenge misses that the absence of underlying substance is the problem in every one of those encounters.

   *Why not D:* This is explicitly the pitfall the entry warns against, not a legitimate shortcut; skipping Develop's proof-building step is what separates a real category claim from an unsupported rebrand.

5. Twelve months into a category design programme, leadership asks how to measure whether it is actually working, beyond a subjective sense that the messaging feels stronger. What should the team track?
   - **A.** How often prospects, press, and analysts use the new category term unprompted.
   - **B.** Total marketing spend allocated to the category launch campaign.
   - **C.** The number of internal employees who can recite the new Point of View from memory.
   - **D.** Website traffic to the page where the new category name first appeared.

   **Correct answer: A.** The methodology specifically calls for measuring unprompted category-term usage, in inbound enquiries, analyst reports, or even a competitor's own marketing, as the real evidence a category is taking hold, since a category becomes real to a buyer once a peer they trust uses the term unprompted.

   *Why not B:* Spend is an input measure, not an outcome measure; a large budget with no unprompted term usage to show for it does not indicate the category claim has actually landed with buyers.

   *Why not C:* Internal recall confirms the team has aligned on the vocabulary, which matters for consistent execution, but it measures the company, not the market; it says nothing about whether buyers, press, or analysts outside the company have adopted the term.

   *Why not D:* Traffic to a specific page reflects visits, not whether visitors internalised and started using the category language themselves; unprompted usage elsewhere is the stronger signal the methodology asks teams to track.
