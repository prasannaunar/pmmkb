# Quiz: Perceptual Map (2x2)

1. A board asks a PMM "where do we sit versus the competition?" and a detailed feature-comparison spreadsheet has failed to land in two prior briefings. What is the strongest reason to reach for a Perceptual Map here instead of another round of the same spreadsheet?
   - **A.** A map makes the market's structure visible at a glance on axes buyers actually care about.
   - **B.** A map is a more rigorous, purely quantitative alternative to the spreadsheet.
   - **C.** A map replaces the need for any deeper positioning work on its own, without further research.
   - **D.** A map takes less time to prepare than updating an existing spreadsheet.

   **Correct answer: A.** The map's value is visual and buyer-grounded: two axes chosen from what buyers actually say drives their choice let a board see the competitive structure immediately, which is exactly the kind of question a feature-by-feature spreadsheet struggles to answer clearly.

   *Why not B:* The map is not inherently more quantitative; its two axes are often built from qualitative buyer language just as much as any numeric source. Its real advantage is visual clarity for a non-specialist audience, not measurement precision.

   *Why not C:* The entry is explicit that a validated map is an input to a full positioning statement or canvas, not a replacement for either; it is a fast diagnostic, not the complete positioning exercise.

   *Why not D:* Speed of preparation is not the reason to choose this tool; the reason is that it communicates the finding in a format the audience can actually absorb, which the spreadsheet approach had already failed to do twice.

2. A team picks "number of integrations" and "years in business" as the two axes for its perceptual map, because those are the two stats that make its own product look strongest. What is the most likely problem with this choice?
   - **A.** The axes were chosen to flatter the company's own product rather than sourced from buyer evidence.
   - **B.** Two axes are never enough to represent a competitive market accurately.
   - **C.** "Years in business" is not a valid metric for any competitive analysis.
   - **D.** The map needed at least eight named competitors plotted before it could be considered statistically valid enough to trust.

   **Correct answer: A.** This is the flattering-axes pitfall: choosing dimensions the product already wins on, rather than sourcing axis choice from unprompted buyer language in win/loss interviews or review sites, produces an internally reassuring chart that does not reflect how buyers actually decide.

   *Why not B:* Two axes is the intended design of this tool; the issue here is which two axes were chosen, not the number of dimensions used.

   *Why not C:* Years in business can be a legitimate axis if buyers genuinely weigh it when choosing a vendor; the problem is that it was picked to flatter the company, not that the metric itself is invalid in every context.

   *Why not D:* Five to eight is guidance for keeping the chart readable, not a validity threshold, and eight sits inside that range rather than below a minimum; the flaw here is the axis selection, not the competitor count.

3. While building a map, a PMM scores every competitor's position using each rival's own website copy, since it is the fastest source available before a deadline. What is the likely consequence?
   - **A.** The map ends up reflecting marketing claims rather than genuine buyer perception.
   - **B.** The map will be highly accurate; a company's website is the most authoritative source on its own positioning.
   - **C.** This approach is fine as long as the same website-copy method is used for every competitor.
   - **D.** The deadline pressure is the real problem, and the map should simply be delayed.

   **Correct answer: A.** Competitor websites reliably describe themselves in the best possible light on every dimension at once; a map built from that copy is really a collection of marketing claims arranged on a grid, not a picture of how buyers actually perceive the competitive set.

   *Why not B:* A company's own website is the least reliable source for how buyers actually perceive it; it reflects aspiration and positioning intent rather than buyer experience.

   *Why not C:* Consistency of a flawed method does not fix the underlying problem; using the same biased source for every competitor still produces a map built on marketing claims rather than buyer evidence.

   *Why not D:* The core issue is the evidence source, not the timeline; even with more time, sourcing scores from website copy instead of win/loss interviews or review-site text would still produce the same distorted picture.

4. A team plots its map and finds an entirely empty quadrant with no competitors in it. Before building a positioning claim around that space, what should happen next?
   - **A.** Validate that a meaningful number of target buyers actually want what sits in that quadrant.
   - **B.** Launch messaging claiming ownership of the empty quadrant immediately, ahead of any further checking.
   - **C.** Redraw the map with different axes until a competitor appears in that quadrant.
   - **D.** Treat the finding as valid already, on the strength of the map's buyer-sourced axes.

   **Correct answer: A.** An empty quadrant is not automatically valuable; the framework calls for validating demand directly with five to eight target buyers before committing a positioning claim, since silence or confusion in that validation is a signal the space is empty for a reason, not a green light.

   *Why not B:* Rushing to claim unvalidated white space risks building a positioning story around a gap buyers do not actually care about, which the pitfalls section specifically warns against.

   *Why not C:* Changing the axes just to make the finding look different defeats the purpose of using buyer-sourced axes in the first place; the map should reflect real buyer priorities, not be adjusted to manufacture a preferred result.

   *Why not D:* Buyer-sourced axes make the map more credible than one built on internal opinion, but sourcing the axes is a different check from validating demand in this specific quadrant; the map can be well-built and the white space still empty for a reason.

5. A company has already run Bowman's Strategic Clock and confirmed its price-to-value position is defensible. A board member still asks how the product compares on ease of implementation, a dimension the clock does not cover. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Build a Perceptual Map using implementation ease and a second buyer-relevant attribute as axes.
   - **B.** Explain that Bowman's Strategic Clock already covers this question fully.
   - **C.** Re-run Bowman's Strategic Clock with implementation ease substituted for price.
   - **D.** Decline to answer, treating ease of implementation as not a genuinely valid competitive dimension here.

   **Correct answer: A.** The Perceptual Map exists precisely because the clock always uses the same two axes; when a different buyer-relevant attribute needs examining, the map's axis-agnostic design is the right tool to check the price-value picture against a second dimension.

   *Why not B:* The clock's fixed price-and-value axes do not cover implementation ease at all; treating it as already answered ignores a real, separate buyer consideration the board has raised.

   *Why not C:* The clock's two axes are fixed by design, price and perceived value, specifically to test whether a competitive strategy is defensible; substituting a different axis changes what the tool is testing and is not how the two tools are meant to work together.

   *Why not D:* Implementation ease is exactly the kind of concrete, buyer-recognisable attribute the map is built to use as an axis; dismissing it ignores a legitimate and commonly cited buyer consideration.
