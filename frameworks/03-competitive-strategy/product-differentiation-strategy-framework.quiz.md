# Quiz: Product Differentiation Strategy Framework

1. A sales leader tells the PMM team that reps are "winning on price alone" in nearly every deal this quarter, and asks for a bigger discount tier to close more of them. What should the PMM recognise about this pattern?
   - **A.** It is a differentiation failure that discounting harder will not actually solve.
   - **B.** It confirms the product is priced too high and should be repriced immediately.
   - **C.** It is a normal, temporary condition that will resolve once the next release ships.
   - **D.** It means reps need better objection-handling training for price conversations.

   **Correct answer: A.** Deals decided on price alone almost always signal that buyers see no meaningful difference between the options in front of them, which is a differentiation problem even if no one has framed it that way. Discounting harder wins individual deals while leaving the underlying perception gap untouched.

   *Why not B:* Repricing addresses the symptom, not the cause; if buyers genuinely valued something distinct about the product, price would matter less in the decision, regardless of what the number is.

   *Why not C:* Treating a pattern across an entire quarter of deals as temporary risks letting a real differentiation gap persist uncorrected until win rate has already eroded further.

   *Why not D:* Better objection handling can help reps communicate a difference once one exists, but it cannot manufacture a difference that is not currently there in the product, pricing, or messaging; training fixes delivery, not the missing differentiator.

2. A mid-market SaaS company picks "24/7 customer support" as its key differentiator, based on an internal belief that its support team is excellent. Six months later, three competitors have matched the offering. What did the team most likely get wrong in its MUD scoring?
   - **A.** The differentiator was never realistically assessed for defensibility against being copied.
   - **B.** The team should have chosen a lower-cost differentiator to invest in instead.
   - **C.** MUD scoring does not apply to service-based differentiators, only product features and pricing.
   - **D.** The team failed to survey enough customers before selecting the differentiator.

   **Correct answer: A.** A support commitment is often meaningful to buyers and can feel unique at first, but 24/7 coverage is comparatively easy for a well-resourced competitor to match within a release cycle or two. Skipping a rigorous defensibility check is exactly how a company ends up with a differentiator that collapses the moment a rival responds.

   *Why not B:* The problem is not the cost of the investment; a defensible, well-resourced support offering can still work. The issue is that defensibility was never seriously stress-tested before committing to it as the core differentiator.

   *Why not C:* MUD scoring applies to any lever, including service and support, ecosystem, or pricing model, not just product features; the framework's usefulness does not depend on the type of differentiator.

   *Why not D:* Survey sample size is not the failure here; the team could have surveyed extensively and still confirmed customers valued support. The gap was in testing whether competitors could copy it quickly, not in whether customers cared.

3. A PMM is building a differentiation strategy and finds two candidate levers: one is a proprietary integration that took eighteen months to build and touches core architecture, the other is a slightly more generous free trial period than competitors offer. Which should be prioritised as the primary differentiator?
   - **A.** The proprietary integration, the harder of the two for a competitor to replicate quickly.
   - **B.** The free trial length; it is easier for marketing to communicate to prospects.
   - **C.** Both should be dropped in favour of a price-based differentiator instead.
   - **D.** The free trial length, which ships and can be fully tested much faster than the proprietary integration.

   **Correct answer: A.** Speed of implementation and ease of messaging matter, but defensibility, whether a competitor can copy the lever quickly, is one of the three criteria a differentiator must clear. A trial-length change can be matched in days; deep architectural work cannot be replicated nearly as fast, making it the stronger long-term bet.

   *Why not B:* Ease of communication is a messaging consideration, not a strategic one; a differentiator that is simple to explain but trivially copyable will not hold up once a competitor matches it.

   *Why not C:* Competing primarily on price abandons differentiation altogether and pushes the company toward the price-sensitive dynamics the framework is meant to help escape.

   *Why not D:* Shipping fast is a real advantage, but it is only part of the picture: a differentiator's value comes from how long it holds up under competitive pressure, and the trial-length change is also the more easily copied of the two once it ships.

4. A company's sales team continues to lead every pitch with "we're 20% cheaper" even after PMM has spent two quarters building a differentiation strategy around integration depth and compliance certifications. What is the most likely reason the strategy is failing to show results?
   - **A.** Teams were never aligned around the new strategy, so sales kept executing the old price-led pitch.
   - **B.** The differentiators chosen were not meaningful enough to this segment's buyers.
   - **C.** The MUD scoring process was skipped entirely during the strategy's development.
   - **D.** The company should abandon differentiation entirely and formally adopt a price-led strategy going forward.

   **Correct answer: A.** Inconsistent execution is a named pitfall: even a strong, well-researched differentiation strategy fails if sales, product, and marketing are not genuinely aligned around it. A sales team still opening with price undermines two quarters of roadmap and messaging work built around a different story entirely.

   *Why not B:* This would be a real concern if buyers were pushing back on the differentiators themselves, but the scenario describes a sales team that never changed its pitch at all, which points to an alignment gap rather than customers rejecting the value of integration depth or compliance.

   *Why not C:* The problem described is behavioural and cross-functional, about what sales says in the room, not a scoring methodology issue from when the strategy was originally built.

   *Why not D:* Reverting to price leadership abandons the investment already made in integration depth and compliance work; the fix is aligning the sales team to the existing strategy, not discarding it.

5. Twelve months after launching a differentiator built around a single third-party integration, a competitor announces the identical integration. The PMM team is caught off guard. What should have happened earlier in the process to prevent this?
   - **A.** A stress test at selection time, asking how a competitor could copy it within twelve months.
   - **B.** Choosing a more defensible integration to build in the first place, instead of this one.
   - **C.** Keeping the integration secret from customers until closer to the competitor's likely launch.
   - **D.** Taking legal action to prevent the competitor from building a similar integration.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework explicitly calls for stress-testing any differentiator against imitation before committing significant investment, and pairing an imitable one with a second, harder-to-copy lever such as proprietary data or an accumulating brand asset. Skipping that step is exactly how a team gets blindsided when a competitor matches the feature.

   *Why not B:* Picking a seemingly stronger integration helps, but any single lever eventually gets matched; the real fix is the ongoing habit of stress-testing whatever is chosen and having a second, harder-to-copy differentiator ready, not just a better initial pick.

   *Why not C:* Hiding a differentiator from customers defeats its purpose; a differentiator only creates value once buyers know about it and factor it into their decision.

   *Why not D:* Building a similar integration is rarely something legal action can prevent, since integrations built on public APIs are generally not proprietary in a legally defensible way; the real defence is a stronger, harder-to-copy strategy.
