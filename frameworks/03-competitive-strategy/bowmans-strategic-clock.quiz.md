# Quiz: Bowman's Strategic Clock

1. A hardware company's flagship product has crept from a defensible hybrid position (good value, competitive price) to high price with low perceived value over three product cycles, purely through incremental feature additions and cost creep, with no single decision behind the shift. What is the most likely underlying failure?
   - **A.** No one owned a deliberate target position, so changes drifted unchecked across three cycles.
   - **B.** The clock should be re-plotted now to confirm exactly where the product currently sits.
   - **C.** Sales never escalated discounting requests to leadership quickly enough during that quarter.
   - **D.** The pricing team set the list price too high compared with competitors from the very outset.

   **Correct answer: A.** This is the classic drift pitfall: positions shift gradually when no one owns an intentional target and checks decisions against it. The fix is deciding on a position once, committing to it, and re-plotting on a fixed cadence to catch drift early.

   *Why not B:* Re-plotting confirms where the product sits today, but does not explain how it got there. This is correct as far as it goes; it stops short of naming the missing ownership that let three cycles of small decisions go unchecked.

   *Why not C:* Escalating discounts might slow the symptom in one deal, but it does not address why the product ended up in an unstable position in the first place. Discounting pressure is a downstream effect of poor price-to-value fit, not the root cause.

   *Why not D:* An initial pricing error would show up immediately, not build gradually over three cycles. This scenario describes drift over time, which points to an absence of ongoing ownership rather than a single upfront mistake.

2. A SaaS vendor just renegotiated three enterprise renewals and, for the first time, heard customers say the price no longer feels justified, even though nothing in the contract terms changed. Which use case does this best match for applying Bowman's Strategic Clock?
   - **A.** A trigger to re-plot the price-to-value ratio for this segment.
   - **B.** A cue to switch to a Perceptual Map for this pricing conversation instead.
   - **C.** A sign the sales team needs better negotiation training before the next renewal cycle.
   - **D.** An isolated account issue, best solved by discounting the three renewals to retain them.

   **Correct answer: A.** Pricing renegotiations that reveal customers no longer see the value they once did are one of the framework's stated triggers. When perceived value drops while price holds steady, that is precisely the price-to-value mismatch the clock exists to surface and correct.

   *Why not B:* The Perceptual Map is useful for buyer-chosen attribute comparisons on a fresh pair of axes; this scenario is specifically about the price-value relationship shifting, which is the clock's own diagnostic, not a reason to swap tools.

   *Why not C:* Better negotiation tactics might buy time in this renewal cycle, but they do not fix a genuine value perception gap; the same objection will resurface with the next customer and the one after that.

   *Why not D:* Treating this as isolated to three accounts ignores that a price-value mismatch discovered in a handful of renewals is usually present across the whole segment, not just the accounts where it happened to surface first.

3. A premium-priced enterprise software vendor tells its team it occupies position 5 (focused differentiation) because its product has more features than any competitor. Win-loss interviews, however, show buyers routinely describe the product as "expensive but not obviously better." What does this reveal?
   - **A.** The vendor is confusing perceived value with price rather than customer-validated value.
   - **B.** The win-loss sample size was too small to draw any reliable conclusion about positioning.
   - **C.** The vendor should cut price to match competitors and drop the premium strategy.
   - **D.** The product roadmap simply needs more features to close the perception gap.

   **Correct answer: A.** Perceived value is what customers actually experience and can articulate, such as outcomes, certifications, or testimonials, not what a team internally believes its feature count earns it. Without that customer-side proof, a high price with unproven value sits in the unstable zone, not a defensible premium position.

   *Why not B:* The issue here is not sample size; it is that the feedback consistently describes a price-value mismatch. More interviews would likely reinforce the same finding rather than overturn it.

   *Why not C:* Cutting price reactively, without first diagnosing whether the value case is fixable through proof points and positioning, skips the diagnostic step the clock is meant to force before any pricing action.

   *Why not D:* This treats the gap as a feature gap, but the finding is a perception gap: customers already see plenty of features and still do not feel the price is justified. More features without proof of impact will not close that gap.

4. After plotting a product into an unstable zone (high price, standard or lower perceived value), what should a team do first, according to the framework?
   - **A.** Choose one direction: raise perceived value, lower price, or reposition to fit the actual cost structure.
   - **B.** Launch a marketing campaign that emphasises existing features more heavily to shift buyer perception.
   - **C.** Wait for the next win-loss cycle to confirm the finding before taking any action.
   - **D.** Cut price immediately to match the nearest low-price competitor's list price.

   **Correct answer: A.** Landing in an unstable zone calls for a deliberate choice among the framework's defensible options: raise real value, lower real cost and price, or reposition entirely. Committing to one direction, rather than drifting further, is the point of diagnosing the gap in the first place.

   *Why not B:* A campaign that only emphasises existing features does not change the underlying value customers experience; if the gap is real, better messaging alone will not close it and may even erode trust once buyers see through it.

   *Why not C:* This is the right instinct, gathering more evidence, at the wrong point: the clock exercise has already produced the diagnosis. Waiting for another cycle just extends the time spent in a position the framework has already shown is unsustainable.

   *Why not D:* Matching a low-price competitor's price without addressing the underlying cost structure risks eroding margin without actually resolving the value perception issue, and abandons any differentiation strategy the company might otherwise pursue.

5. A team ran Bowman's Strategic Clock once, ahead of last year's annual planning cycle, set a target position, and has not looked at it since. What is the most likely consequence, and what should they do about it?
   - **A.** The team cannot confirm the repositioning happened, and should build re-plotting into a fixed cadence.
   - **B.** Nothing significant; competitive positions rarely shift enough within a single year to matter.
   - **C.** They should drop the clock in favour of a different framework for tracking position over time.
   - **D.** They should re-run the exercise only if a major new competitor enters the market.

   **Correct answer: A.** Running the exercise once and never revisiting it is a named pitfall: without a recurring cadence, teams cannot verify whether a strategic move actually happened or just intended to. Re-plotting every two quarters, using the same data sources, is what turns intent into a tracked, accountable outcome.

   *Why not B:* Perceived value and competitive dynamics shift continuously as competitors reposition, add features, or change pricing; a full year without re-checking is exactly the gap that lets drift back into an unstable position go unnoticed.

   *Why not C:* The clock remains useful precisely because it can be re-run to track movement over time, not just as a one-off diagnostic; abandoning it discards the ongoing tracking value the framework is designed to provide.

   *Why not D:* Re-running only when a new competitor appears misses that positions can also drift from internal decisions alone, such as feature creep or cost changes, entirely independent of competitor moves. The fixed two-quarter cadence catches both; an event trigger catches only one.
