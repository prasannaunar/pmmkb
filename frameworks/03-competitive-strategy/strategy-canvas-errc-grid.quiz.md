# Quiz: Strategy Canvas and ERRC Grid

1. Win/loss interviews at a mid-market HR software company show buyers calling it and three rivals "basically the same", and most deals end in a discount round. The product team's answer is another quarter of catch-up features. What should the PMM do first?
   - **A.** Chart every rival across the factors buyers weigh, then redesign the offer with an ERRC grid.
   - **B.** Plot the company and its rivals on a two-axis map built from the two attributes buyers mention most.
   - **C.** Approve the catch-up features so the product stops losing feature-by-feature comparisons with rivals.
   - **D.** Lower list price by 15% so the company wins the discount round before it starts in each deal.

   **Correct answer: A.** Interchangeable offers and discount-driven deals are the convergence the strategy canvas exposes: rival curves running in parallel across the same factors. The ERRC grid then turns that diagnosis into a redesign, deciding what to stop, cut, raise and add rather than adding more of the same.

   *Why not B:* A Perceptual Map is the neighbouring diagnostic, but it holds only two axes. When buyers see four offers as identical across a whole list of factors, two axes cannot show where every curve converges or which factors to eliminate and reduce; the canvas holds eight to twelve at once.

   *Why not C:* Catch-up features push the curve further into parallel with rivals, which is the convergence causing the discounting in the first place. The framework asks which factors buyers would accept less of, not only what to add.

   *Why not D:* A price cut competes on one factor the whole market already fights over and leaves the converged curve unchanged. It also erodes margin without giving buyers any new reason to choose the company.

2. A PMM builds a strategy canvas using the factor list from the product team's feature matrix. The result shows the company leading on 7 of 10 factors, yet win rate in the segment has fallen for three quarters. What is the most likely flaw?
   - **A.** The factors came from the internal feature view instead of what buyers weigh in their decisions.
   - **B.** The rivals were scored from their own marketing claims instead of from buyer evidence about them.
   - **C.** The canvas needed at least fifteen factors before its curves could show a reliable pattern.
   - **D.** The company's curve should have been drawn last, after every rival's curve was already scored.

   **Correct answer: A.** A canvas built from the feature matrix shows the company competing hard on factors it has invested in, which may not be the factors buyers decide on. Leading on 7 of 10 while win rate falls is the signal: the factor list itself needs rebuilding from coded win/loss, VoC and review evidence.

   *Why not B:* Scoring rivals from their marketing is a real evidence problem, but it is the wrong stage here. Even perfectly scored curves mislead if the factors on the horizontal axis are ones buyers barely weigh; the evidence fix has to start with which factors are on the canvas.

   *Why not C:* More factors does not fix factors chosen from the wrong source. The entry keeps the list to the 8 to 12 factors buyers actually weigh, and adding internal factors would only deepen the problem.

   *Why not D:* The order in which curves are drawn does not change what they show. The issue is which factors were chosen, not the sequence of plotting.

3. A PMM's draft ERRC grid lists three factors to raise and two to create, with the Eliminate and Reduce boxes empty. Every entry traces to buyer interviews. The grid is due at the leadership meeting next week. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Return to the canvas and find factors buyers weigh less, to fill Eliminate and Reduce before presenting.
   - **B.** Present the grid as drafted and agree to add the Eliminate and Reduce entries after leadership signs off.
   - **C.** Present the grid as drafted, since every Raise and Create entry already traces back to buyer evidence.
   - **D.** Move the two Create entries into Raise, so the grid stays within factors the industry already offers.

   **Correct answer: A.** A grid with only Raise and Create entries is a feature roadmap, not a redesign: it raises cost and leaves the converged curve in place. The canvas already shows where everyone over-delivers relative to buyer importance, which is where the Eliminate and Reduce entries should come from.

   *Why not B:* This includes the missing work but puts it at the wrong point. Leadership would be approving a cost-raising grid without the reductions that pay for it, and reductions added afterwards arrive without the trade-off being decided on.

   *Why not C:* Buyer evidence for the raises is necessary but not sufficient. Without subtraction, the offer becomes a costlier version of the same curve, which the framework exists to avoid.

   *Why not D:* Relabelling Create as Raise does not address the empty boxes and drops the factors that make the curve diverge from rivals. Creating what no one offers is one of the four actions, not something to avoid.

4. At a 400-person SaaS company, a PMM's canvas and ERRC grid recommend eliminating a reporting module from the core tier, a change that affects roadmap, pricing and two engineers' work. Buyer testing supports the trade. What should the PMM do next?
   - **A.** Take the grid, with its cost and revenue implications, to the CEO and product leadership to decide.
   - **B.** Write the new positioning and launch messaging now, then brief product leadership on the module change.
   - **C.** Ask the sales team to vote on which reductions they are comfortable selling before taking it further.
   - **D.** Publish the canvas on the website as a comparison page showing where the company now leads.

   **Correct answer: A.** At scale the PMM builds the evidence and drafts the grid, but eliminating a module commits roadmap, pricing and headcount, so the decision sits with the CEO and product leadership. The PMM then executes the positioning and messaging once the change is approved.

   *Why not B:* Messaging is the PMM's work, but it comes after the decision, not before it. Writing launch copy for a change product leadership has not approved risks positioning an offer the company never builds.

   *Why not C:* Sales input is useful evidence, but a vote is not how a roadmap and pricing decision gets made. The buyer test has already checked the trade; the decision belongs to the people accountable for the offer.

   *Why not D:* An internal diagnostic is not a public comparison page, and the redesigned curve does not exist yet. Publishing it before approval or delivery would promise buyers an offer that may change.

5. Six months after a relaunch built on an ERRC grid, the exec deck says the company has "made competition irrelevant". A rival then copies its five-day onboarding within two releases, and win rate starts to slip. What should the PMM have done differently?
   - **A.** Framed the result as a differentiated curve, planned for imitation and kept re-plotting rivals.
   - **B.** Run a Category Design programme to name the new space, so the rival could not claim the same ground.
   - **C.** Raised three more factors straight after launch, to stay ahead of any rival before it could respond.
   - **D.** Kept the onboarding change confidential until the rival's next release cycle had already finished.

   **Correct answer: A.** A redesigned value curve is a differentiated offer, not proof that competition has gone away, and rivals can copy a raise or reduction within a few release cycles. A commonly raised criticism is that the originators' evidence rests largely on retrospective cases of successful moves, so planning for imitation and re-plotting on a fixed cadence is the safer stance.

   *Why not B:* Category Design is the neighbouring entry for naming a new market, but a category label does not stop a rival copying a factor. It answers a framing question; the failure here was over-claiming durability and not tracking rivals' curves.

   *Why not C:* Raising more factors without a plan adds cost and drifts back towards a crowded curve. The issue was the claim of lasting uniqueness, not too few raises.

   *Why not D:* A buyer-facing change cannot stay hidden from rivals, and hiding it would also stop buyers learning about it. The defence is planning the response to imitation, not secrecy.
