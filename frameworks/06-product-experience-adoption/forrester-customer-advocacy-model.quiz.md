# Quiz: Forrester Customer Advocacy Model

1. A company has a genuinely high NPS score, and marketing points to this as evidence it already has a strong advocacy programme. What does the model suggest about this claim?
   - **A.** A high NPS score is often mistaken for a strong advocacy programme.
   - **B.** The claim is correct; a high NPS score is functionally equivalent to Embedded stage.
   - **C.** NPS and advocacy maturity are entirely unrelated concepts to compare.
   - **D.** A high NPS score means the company should skip straight to an Embedded-stage programme.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly names this exact confusion: a high NPS score is often mistaken for an advocacy programme, when in reality most such companies are still Reactive, chasing references just in time with no systematic tracking, segmentation, or lifecycle instrumentation in place.

   *Why not B:* NPS reflects sentiment; the model's stages reflect whether a systematic process exists to identify, track, and act on that sentiment. A high score alone says nothing about whether that system has been built.

   *Why not C:* NPS is explicitly named as the initial source of Promoters this model segments into a managed advocate pool; the two are connected, just not equivalent to full advocacy maturity.

   *Why not D:* This gets the connection right but the sequence wrong: skipping stages ignores that each one builds on systems, tracking, ask-menu design, fatigue management, that the earlier stages establish. The model is a ladder to climb deliberately, not a shortcut to take.

2. An audit finds that four long-tenured customer accounts have absorbed 90% of all advocacy asks over two years, while a separate NPS analysis shows 62 other Promoters have never been approached. What stage does this pattern most likely indicate, and what is the fix?
   - **A.** Reactive stage; cross-reference NPS Promoters and cap asks per account.
   - **B.** Embedded stage; the programme is mature and needs more of the same four accounts.
   - **C.** Programmatic stage; add a bigger reward tier for the four heavily used accounts.
   - **D.** This pattern isn't diagnostic of any particular stage under this model.

   **Correct answer: A.** This is the model's own Reactive-stage symptom, advocacy chased from the same top-of-mind accounts with no tracking of who has already been asked. The fix is exactly what the model prescribes: cross-reference NPS Promoters against the audit, build a tracking system, and cap asks per account, routing new requests to the under-used pool.

   *Why not B:* Embedded stage requires lifecycle instrumentation and measured pipeline contribution; over-relying on the same four accounts with no tracking is the opposite of that stage's characteristics.

   *Why not C:* Programmatic stage already has a tiered ask menu and named owner in place; what's missing here is any tracking or distribution at all, the defining Reactive symptom, not a reward that needs resizing.

   *Why not D:* This exact pattern, a small pool absorbing nearly all asks while many Promoters go untouched, is the model's clearest named signal of Reactive-stage advocate fatigue risk.

3. A company builds a formal, tiered ask menu with rewards scaled by effort, and assigns a named owner who runs a quarterly nomination cycle. However, advocacy requests are still only ever generated when marketing has a specific need, never triggered by renewal or usage milestones. What stage does this represent?
   - **A.** Programmatic; it lacks the lifecycle instrumentation that would move it to Embedded.
   - **B.** Embedded; a tiered ask menu and named owner are the only requirements for this stage.
   - **C.** Reactive; a tiered ask menu doesn't count toward any stage above the baseline.
   - **D.** Identified; a named owner alone qualifies a programme for this stage.

   **Correct answer: A.** This matches the Programmatic stage definition precisely: a formal, tiered ask menu with a matched reward, run on a defined cadence by a named owner, but with advocacy still triggered reactively by marketing need rather than automatically at lifecycle moments, which is what would move it to Embedded.

   *Why not B:* Embedded specifically requires advocacy triggered automatically at lifecycle moments and its pipeline or win-rate contribution measured; the scenario explicitly describes the opposite, requests still driven by marketing need.

   *Why not C:* A formal tiered ask menu and named owner represent meaningful progress beyond Reactive; the model would misclassify real structural progress by calling this Reactive.

   *Why not D:* Identified is a much earlier stage, defined by using NPS and win/loss data to systematically find and segment Promoters with lightweight tracking; a full tiered ask menu and named owner go well beyond that stage's criteria.

4. A company has a well-regarded advocacy programme, but no one tracks whether references actually influence deal outcomes or contribute to pipeline. During a budget review, the programme's funding is cut. What does the model suggest could have prevented this?
   - **A.** Tracking reference-assisted win rate and pipeline contribution from the outset.
   - **B.** Nothing; advocacy programmes are inherently vulnerable to budget cuts.
   - **C.** Focusing exclusively on high-effort asks, since those are self-evidently valuable.
   - **D.** Moving ownership from a dedicated advocacy role to Customer Success.

   **Correct answer: A.** Running the programme with no measurement is a named pitfall: without a pipeline or win-rate number to point to, an advocacy programme looks like a nice-to-have the first time budgets tighten. The recovery is tracking reference-assisted win rate and advocate-sourced pipeline from the programme's first quarter, even informally.

   *Why not B:* The model treats this outcome as preventable specifically through measurement; a programme with evidence of pipeline and win-rate contribution has a defensible case during a budget review that an unmeasured one lacks.

   *Why not C:* Effort level of the asks is unrelated to whether the programme's business impact is measured and demonstrable; even a programme built entirely on high-effort asks would remain vulnerable without measurement.

   *Why not D:* Who runs the programme, PMM or Customer Success, is a separate ownership question from whether its impact is measured; the specific fix for surviving a budget review is demonstrable measurement, not a change in reporting line.

5. A company has an active, tiered advocacy programme that has been running successfully for eighteen months. The team has not re-audited it since it launched. What does the model recommend, and why?
   - **A.** Re-audit at least annually, to catch quiet regression toward Reactive early.
   - **B.** No further action; Programmatic and Embedded status is permanent once reached.
   - **C.** Rebuild the programme entirely every eighteen months, regardless of performance.
   - **D.** Re-audit only if advocacy participation numbers visibly decline first.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly calls for re-auditing at least annually, since a programme can regress toward Reactive quietly, for example if its named owner changes roles or the tracking system falls into disuse, and catching that slide early, rather than after a visible decline, is the model's specific value.

   *Why not B:* The model treats stage advancement as reversible; without ongoing maintenance and re-auditing, a mature programme can regress, so no stage is described as a permanent, self-sustaining state.

   *Why not C:* Rebuilding from scratch is unnecessary and wasteful if the programme is performing well; the model calls for a re-audit to check for quiet regression, not routine full reconstruction.

   *Why not D:* Waiting for a visible decline in participation is reactive; the model's stated value is catching regression early, before it shows up as a visible drop in outcomes.
