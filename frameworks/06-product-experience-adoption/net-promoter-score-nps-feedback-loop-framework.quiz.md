# Quiz: Net Promoter Score (NPS) & Feedback Loop Framework

1. A company's overall NPS is 45, and leadership treats this as a healthy, unified signal across the whole customer base. A closer look shows mid-market scores 60 while SMB scores 15. What should the team do?
   - **A.** Analyse and act on the two segments separately.
   - **B.** Keep reporting only the blended 45 score going forward.
   - **C.** Deprioritise the SMB segment going forward entirely.
   - **D.** Re-run the survey with different wording until scores match.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework explicitly calls for analysing NPS by segment; a wide divergence, mid-market at 60 versus SMB at 15, means the two segments need fundamentally different strategies, not a single blended number that obscures the real, actionable gap between them.

   *Why not B:* Reporting is still happening, and NPS is still being tracked; the problem is scope. A single blended number buries exactly the segment-level divergence that needs a distinct intervention, rather than surfacing it.

   *Why not C:* A low segment score is a signal to diagnose and address a specific problem within that segment, as the framework's own example does with a new Lite tier and live chat support, not an automatic reason to abandon the segment.

   *Why not D:* Changing survey wording to manufacture a different score defeats the purpose of NPS as an honest sentiment signal; the fix is diagnosing and acting on the real underlying issues the low score reflects.

2. A team is proud that its NPS rose from 45 to 47 this quarter and reports the improvement to leadership without mentioning any of the qualitative themes behind it. What pitfall does this reflect?
   - **A.** Obsessing over the number rather than the themes behind it.
   - **B.** No follow-up on the Detractor themes driving sentiment.
   - **C.** Assuming all Detractors will inevitably churn.
   - **D.** This is not a pitfall; the two-point gain is a complete report.

   **Correct answer: A.** Obsessing over the number is a named pitfall: a score like 45 or 47 is useful context, but teams that fixate on moving the number by a point or two without discussing the themes behind it miss the underlying issues. The fix is always reporting NPS alongside its top three qualitative themes.

   *Why not B:* No follow-up is a real pitfall, but it describes failing to act on a specific Detractor complaint; this scenario is about reporting a number without any qualitative context, a distinct problem from unresolved outreach.

   *Why not C:* This pitfall concerns writing off Detractors as certain churners; the scenario here does not involve Detractor treatment at all, just a number reported without themes.

   *Why not D:* The framework explicitly warns against treating the number alone as a sufficient report; leadership needs the qualitative themes to understand what is actually driving the change.

3. A long-tenured, heavy user of the product scores a 4 (a Detractor) on NPS, and customer success considers this account a near-certain churn risk and deprioritises any further engagement. What does the framework suggest about this assumption?
   - **A.** Not all Detractors are churn risks; segment by usage and tenure first.
   - **B.** Treat every Detractor as an imminent churn risk, regardless of tenure.
   - **C.** Exclude this account from all future NPS surveys.
   - **D.** Offer this account an immediate retention discount without further diagnosis.

   **Correct answer: A.** Assuming all Detractors will churn is a named pitfall: some Detractors are highly engaged but critical precisely because they care about the product succeeding. The recovery is segmenting Detractors by usage and tenure, since a heavy, long-tenured user scoring low is often the best source of specific, fixable feedback and can become a Promoter once heard.

   *Why not B:* Treating every Detractor identically ignores exactly the nuance the framework calls for; a heavily engaged critic is a different case from a disengaged one about to leave, and deserves different outreach.

   *Why not C:* Excluding this customer from future surveys discards valuable, specific feedback from someone who clearly still cares about the product; the framework's guidance is to engage this kind of Detractor, not exclude them.

   *Why not D:* Acting quickly is the right instinct, but jumping straight to a discount skips the step the framework calls for first: segmenting by usage and tenure to tell a genuine risk apart from an engaged critic who just needs to be heard.

4. A Detractor reports that support response times are too slow. Three survey cycles later, response times still have not improved, and the same complaint keeps recurring. What does the framework say should have happened?
   - **A.** An owner should have been assigned to the theme within one week.
   - **B.** Keep surveying the same customer each quarter and wait for sentiment to improve.
   - **C.** Reclassify the customer as a Promoter, given their repeat engagement.
   - **D.** Change the survey question to avoid triggering the same complaint again.

   **Correct answer: A.** No follow-up is a named pitfall: if Detractors report an issue and it is never fixed, NPS will not improve no matter how many surveys are sent. The recovery is assigning an owner to every major Detractor theme within one week of the survey closing, with a target date for a visible resolution.

   *Why not B:* Repeatedly surveying without acting on the finding is exactly the "no follow-up" pattern the framework warns against; passive monitoring will not improve sentiment if the underlying issue is never addressed.

   *Why not C:* Repeat feedback on the same unresolved complaint is not evidence of Promoter-level satisfaction; reclassifying the customer would mask the ongoing issue rather than address it.

   *Why not D:* Changing the survey question does not fix the underlying support response-time issue driving the complaint; it only avoids seeing the same feedback again; the actual fix is resolving the reported issue.

5. A team wants to report a rising NPS trend to leadership as evidence of a successful quarter but has no other business metric to pair it with. What does the framework suggest?
   - **A.** Pair the NPS trend with a leading business metric like renewal rate.
   - **B.** NPS alone is always sufficient evidence of business health.
   - **C.** Replace NPS entirely with a purely financial commercial metric instead.
   - **D.** Wait until next year to report NPS trends at all.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework explicitly recommends reporting NPS alongside a leading business metric, like renewal rate or expansion revenue, when presenting to leadership, since a rising score in isolation can be a hard sell without a visible connection to commercial outcomes.

   *Why not B:* The framework treats NPS as most persuasive when paired with a commercial metric; presenting it alone risks leadership discounting the finding as a soft, unlinked signal.

   *Why not C:* This takes the right instinct, connecting NPS to commercial reality, too far; the framework's guidance is to pair NPS with a financial metric, not to discard sentiment data in favour of financial metrics alone.

   *Why not D:* The framework recommends tracking and reporting NPS monthly or quarterly, not withholding a full year of data; the fix for this scenario is pairing the metric with a business outcome, not delaying its use.
