# Quiz: Customer Onboarding Maturity Framework

1. A team writes three different email sequences and adds an in-app tutorial to try to fix a milestone stuck at 20% completion. None of the three interventions move the number. What should the team conclude?
   - **A.** Escalate the issue as a likely product or UX problem.
   - **B.** Try one more, shorter intervention before escalating.
   - **C.** Lower the milestone's completion target and stop investigating.
   - **D.** Remove the milestone from the model entirely.

   **Correct answer: A.** Ignoring the product is a named pitfall: onboarding content cannot overcome bad product design. The framework's threshold is explicit: more than one failed content intervention at the same milestone is the signal to escalate a product or UX fix rather than write another tutorial.

   *Why not B:* This is the right eventual move, just delayed. Three interventions have already failed, well past the "more than one" threshold the framework sets for escalating; trying a fourth first ignores a signal that has already fired.

   *Why not C:* Lowering the target sidesteps the actual diagnosis; the framework treats repeated intervention failure as a signal to investigate the product itself, not to redefine success downward.

   *Why not D:* Removing the milestone discards a potentially important indicator of customer success; the fix is escalating the underlying product issue, not deleting the measurement.

2. A company applies the same onboarding email sequence to both its $20-a-month self-serve customers and its enterprise accounts. Enterprise customers report feeling undersupported, while SMB customers report the emails feel excessive. What pitfall does this illustrate?
   - **A.** One-size-fits-all onboarding, using one sequence for different segments.
   - **B.** Ignoring the product, requiring a UX redesign applied equally to both segments.
   - **C.** Measuring the wrong metrics, so the fix is dropping email as a channel.
   - **D.** Standardising content this way is intentional and needs no changes at all.

   **Correct answer: A.** This is the named "one-size-fits-all onboarding" pitfall: applying identical content to segments with very different needs wastes SMB customers' patience while undersupporting enterprise customers who expect hands-on help. The fix is mapping distinct onboarding paths, self-serve, guided, fully-supported, by segment before building content.

   *Why not B:* Ignoring the product is a real pitfall elsewhere in this framework, but it describes a product or UX defect that content can't fix; nothing here suggests a design flaw, only that identical content was sent to segments with very different needs.

   *Why not C:* Measuring the wrong metrics describes pairing milestone completion with the wrong outcome; the problem here is that a single content approach was applied across two segments with fundamentally different expectations, which segmentation, not a metric change, would fix.

   *Why not D:* Standardising for efficiency is exactly what produces the described complaints; the framework explicitly calls for segment-specific paths, not a single shared sequence.

3. A customer completes every defined onboarding milestone within the target timeframe, but churns after two months without renewing. What does the framework suggest the team check?
   - **A.** Whether milestone completion actually correlates with retention.
   - **B.** Whether the milestones were defined too generously in the first place.
   - **C.** Whether to exclude the customer from all future NPS surveys.
   - **D.** Whether sales oversold the product during the initial sales conversation.

   **Correct answer: A.** Measuring the wrong metrics is a named pitfall: a customer can tick every milestone box without ever finding real value. The recovery is pairing milestone completion with a retention or expansion outcome, and dropping any milestone that doesn't correlate with a downstream result after two quarters of data.

   *Why not B:* Making milestones harder to reach does not address whether they actually predict retention; the issue is validating correlation with outcomes, not adjusting difficulty.

   *Why not C:* Removing a churned customer from surveys is unrelated to the actual gap identified, that milestone completion may not be measuring what matters; it does not fix the underlying metric validation problem.

   *Why not D:* A sales oversell is a plausible contributing cause, but the framework's specific guidance for this pattern, milestones completed without retention, is to check whether the milestones themselves correlate with real outcomes; investigating the sales process first skips that recommended step.

4. Onboarding milestones were validated with customer interviews two years ago, and the product has since added several major features and moved upmarket into larger accounts. The team is still using the original milestone list unchanged. What does the framework recommend?
   - **A.** Re-validate the milestone list now, given how much has changed.
   - **B.** Continue using the original milestones without ever revisiting them.
   - **C.** Add new milestones on top of the old ones without pruning any.
   - **D.** Wait for a churn spike before revisiting the milestone list.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework explicitly calls for revisiting the maturity model annually, since as the product adds features and the customer base shifts, for example moving upmarket, the milestones and interventions that worked before may no longer reflect the fastest path to value.

   *Why not B:* The framework treats an annual re-validation as standard practice precisely because product and customer base changes, like a move upmarket, can invalidate an older milestone list.

   *Why not C:* This is a step toward the right idea, updating the list, but layering new milestones onto stale ones without pruning produces a bloated, unfocused model; the framework calls for re-validating the whole list, not just adding to it.

   *Why not D:* Waiting for a churn spike is reactive; the framework's guidance is a standing annual review, not a trigger that depends on damage having already occurred.

5. Sales promises a fast, simple setup to close a deal, but the actual onboarding process for that customer type is known internally to take significant hands-on configuration. The customer becomes frustrated during onboarding. What does the framework identify as the root cause?
   - **A.** Misaligned expectations set during the sales process.
   - **B.** A pure product design flaw that only engineering can resolve.
   - **C.** An execution issue isolated to the customer success team.
   - **D.** An unavoidable cost of onboarding that needs no process change.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework names this directly: misaligned expectations set during the sales process are one of the most common causes of poor onboarding scores. The fix is coordinating across teams, including sales, so accurate expectations are set before the deal closes, aligned with the same milestone definitions everyone else uses.

   *Why not B:* The issue described originates in what sales promised, not necessarily in the product's actual design; the framework's fix is cross-team alignment on expectations, not automatically an engineering escalation.

   *Why not C:* Customer success does have to manage the resulting frustration, but isolating the problem to their execution misses where it actually started, upstream, during the sales process; the fix requires cross-team alignment, not a customer-success-only correction.

   *Why not D:* The framework treats this kind of frustration as a preventable, named cause of poor onboarding scores, with a specific coordination fix, not an unavoidable cost of doing business.
