# Quiz: Win/Loss Analysis Framework

1. An interviewer asks a buyer, "Why didn't you choose us because of our great support?" The buyer gives a vague, agreeable answer. What is the problem with this question?
   - **A.** It is a leading question that biases the buyer's answer.
   - **B.** There is no problem; it confirms what the team already suspects.
   - **C.** The question should have been sent as a written survey instead.
   - **D.** The issue is that the question was too long, not that it was leading.

   **Correct answer: A.** Asking leading questions is a named pitfall: a question framed around a specific assumed reason biases the answer toward confirming it. The recovery is open-ended questions, ideally conducted by someone outside the deal team so the buyer feels safe being candid.

   *Why not B:* Leading questions produce biased, unreliable data, not efficient confirmation; the whole value of win/loss interviews comes from candid, unprompted reasoning, which a leading question undermines.

   *Why not C:* The framework does favour a semi-structured interview over a written survey, but for a different reason, it lets the interviewer follow up on interesting answers. That format choice is not what makes this specific question flawed; the problem is the assumption embedded in its wording.

   *Why not D:* Length is not the issue; a short leading question would bias the response just as much as a long one. The fix is removing the embedded assumption, not shortening the question.

2. A team runs win/loss analysis but only interviews recent losses, reasoning that losses are more urgent to understand than wins. What is the risk in this approach?
   - **A.** Analysing losses but not wins skews the strategy toward defence.
   - **B.** This is fine; losses are always more informative than wins for improving win rate.
   - **C.** The risk is a smaller sample size, not the strategic direction of the findings.
   - **D.** Wins should be analysed only once every two years, unlike losses.

   **Correct answer: A.** Analysing losses but not wins is a named pitfall: you learn as much from why customers buy as from why they don't, and a losses-only approach produces a defensive, gap-focused strategy. The fix is interviewing an equal or near-equal number of wins and losses in every cycle.

   *Why not B:* The framework treats wins and losses as equally valuable sources of insight; losses alone tell only half the story and risk over-indexing strategy on defence rather than reinforcing what is actually working.

   *Why not C:* There is a real risk here, but it isn't just about sample size; the deeper risk is that a losses-only view distorts the strategy's direction toward defence, missing what is actually driving wins, not merely trimming precision.

   *Why not D:* The framework calls for running the full cycle, wins and losses together, quarterly; there is no guidance suggesting wins need far less frequent attention than losses.

3. Win/loss interviews reveal that three recent losses mentioned "we didn't know you could do X," even though the product already had that capability. What does the framework's example suggest as the appropriate response, and how quickly?
   - **A.** Add the unsurfaced capability to the demo script within two weeks.
   - **B.** Treat this as unfixable, requiring a multi-quarter engineering change.
   - **C.** Set the finding aside until it reaches the five-loss pattern threshold.
   - **D.** Escalate it as a pricing issue, since unclear value always points to price.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's own example treats "didn't know you could do X" as a fixable messaging and demo-script gap, adding the previously unsurfaced examples to the standard demo script within two weeks, and confirms this closed the specific gap when a follow-up sample showed zero losses citing it afterward.

   *Why not B:* This is explicitly not a product gap; the capability already existed. The issue is that messaging and the demo never surfaced it, which is a fast, fixable communication problem, not an engineering one.

   *Why not C:* The framework's five-or-more guidance is a general rule of thumb for spotting a pattern, not a hard floor; its own example treats three losses citing the same specific, clearly fixable issue as worth acting on regardless, rather than dismissed as below-threshold.

   *Why not D:* Nothing in the finding relates to price; it is specifically about a capability existing but never being surfaced during the sales process, a messaging and enablement gap.

4. A win/loss analysis is completed with clear findings and recommendations, but three months later nothing has changed in messaging, pricing, or the sales playbook. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Not following up; commit publicly to updating strategy within two weeks.
   - **B.** Asking leading questions; re-run the interviews with a different interviewer.
   - **C.** Segmenting by decision-maker incorrectly; re-analyse by CFO, CTO, and CMO roles.
   - **D.** This is expected; win/loss findings typically take six months to act on.

   **Correct answer: A.** Not following up is a named pitfall: collecting data and not acting on it wastes the time of every customer interviewed and erodes their willingness to participate next time. The recovery is a public commitment to update strategy within two weeks of completing the analysis, with a report-back on what changed.

   *Why not B:* Asking leading questions is a real pitfall, but it describes bias introduced during the interviews themselves; the scenario describes findings that were never acted on afterward, a follow-through failure, not an interview-technique one.

   *Why not C:* Decision-maker segmentation is a step within the analysis itself; the scenario describes action never being taken after the analysis was already completed, a separate, later-stage failure.

   *Why not D:* The framework explicitly calls for action within two weeks of completing the analysis; treating a six-month delay as expected contradicts this guidance directly.

5. A win/loss analysis finds that 8 of 15 losses went to a competitor because "it's what our enterprise parent company mandates," a decision made above the buyer's authority. How does the framework's own example suggest this finding should be treated relative to a smaller pattern of losses tied to unclear messaging?
   - **A.** As a largely unavoidable pattern, unlike the smaller, fixable messaging losses.
   - **B.** As the single highest-priority fix, since it is the largest loss count in the sample.
   - **C.** As evidence the product itself is fundamentally uncompetitive.
   - **D.** As a reason to stop pursuing enterprise accounts with a parent company.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's own example draws exactly this distinction: losing deals to an enterprise mandate made above the buyer's authority is a normal, largely unavoidable pattern, while a smaller number of losses tied to messaging gaps ("didn't know you could do X") is genuinely fixable and worth prioritising action on.

   *Why not B:* Loss count alone doesn't determine priority; the example explicitly treats the larger, mandate-driven count as lower priority for action, since it is not something messaging or product changes can influence, unlike the smaller, actionable messaging-gap pattern.

   *Why not C:* An above-the-buyer's-authority mandate reflects a procurement decision outside the deal itself, not a signal about the product's competitiveness; the framework does not treat it as a product weakness.

   *Why not D:* Withdrawing from enterprise accounts entirely is a much larger strategic move than the finding supports; the framework treats this loss reason as unavoidable in specific cases, not as grounds to abandon a whole segment.
