# Concept Testing

**What it is:** A research method that shows target customers a short description of a product, feature or offer that does not yet exist (or exists only as a prototype) and measures how they react. A concept is usually one page or one screen: the problem, the proposed solution, the main benefits, and often a price. Respondents rate it on appeal, relevance to their needs, uniqueness, believability and purchase intent, then explain their ratings in their own words. The method has no single originator; it grew out of consumer goods research and is now standard practice in survey platforms. There are two main designs. In a **monadic** test, each respondent sees only one concept, which gives the cleanest score but needs a separate sample per concept. In a **sequential monadic** test, each respondent rates several concepts in random order, which needs fewer people but lets one concept colour the next. Concept testing differs from message testing: concept testing asks whether customers want the offer, while message testing asks which words best describe an offer that already exists.

**When to use it:**
- **Before committing build budget to a new product or major feature**, when product and PMM need evidence of demand, not just an internal belief.
- **Choosing between two or three product or packaging ideas** aimed at the same segment, where a monadic score per concept gives a fair comparison.
- **Entering a new segment** (a market development move), to check that the new buyers want the existing product framed for them before a full launch.
- **Testing a new bundle, tier or pricing model** with customers before it goes on the price page.
- **A product team is split** on which of several directions to pursue, and a structured customer read would settle the argument faster than more internal debate.

**How to run it:**
1. **Write the decision first.** State what you will decide with the result: build or not, which concept to pursue, which segment to lead with. Set the threshold that would change the decision, such as "at least 30% of respondents say they would definitely or probably buy".
2. **Write each concept on one page.** Include the problem, the solution in plain language, three main benefits, and a price if price is part of the decision. Keep every concept the same length and format so differences in score reflect the idea, not the writing.
3. **Choose the design.** Use monadic when concepts are similar, the stakes are high, or you need a score to benchmark against future tests. Use sequential monadic for early screening of three to five ideas on a tight budget, and randomise the order.
4. **Recruit the right respondents.** Screen for the target segment and buying role using your ICP. Aim for at least 100 to 150 qualified respondents per concept in a monadic design; fewer than that and differences between concepts are often noise.
5. **Ask the standard measures.** Rate appeal, relevance, uniqueness and believability on a five-point scale, then purchase intent ("definitely would buy" to "definitely would not"). Add one open question: "What, if anything, would stop you buying this?"
6. **Analyse top-two-box scores by segment.** Report the share of respondents choosing the top two points on each scale, overall and by segment. Read the open answers for the objections that recur most.
7. **Compare against a benchmark.** Compare scores with an existing product tested the same way, or with your own past tests. A purchase-intent score means little on its own: researchers widely agree that people overstate what they will buy.
8. **Hand the result to the decision owner.** Give product the scores, the main objections and a recommendation. Feed the winning concept's customer language into positioning and launch messaging.

**Cadence & ownership:** PMM commonly runs concept tests, especially at growth-stage companies without a dedicated research team, because it holds the segment definitions, the positioning and the launch plan the result feeds. The build decision itself belongs to product. At a scaled company, a consumer insights or user research team may run the fieldwork, with PMM writing the concepts and defining the target sample. Run a test whenever a significant new product, tier or segment entry is proposed; a typical online concept test takes two to three weeks from brief to result.

**Example:** Fictional accounting software company Ledgerline had two ideas for its next product: an automated cash-flow forecast and a client portal for accountants to share documents. Product leadership was split. The PMM wrote both as one-page concepts with the same structure and a price of £15 a month each, and ran a monadic test with 300 qualified respondents (150 per concept), all small-business owners or finance leads who matched the ICP. The cash-flow forecast scored 41% top-two-box purchase intent and the client portal scored 22%. The open answers showed the portal's problem: most respondents said their accountant already provided one. Product chose the forecast. The PMM reused respondents' phrases, such as "know if I can make payroll in six weeks", in the launch messaging. The forecast launched seven months later and was adopted by 18% of active customers in its first quarter, against a target of 12%.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Taking purchase intent at face value.** It is a widely held view among researchers that respondents say they would buy far more often than they actually do. Recovery: compare scores against a benchmark tested the same way, and treat the result as a ranking between concepts rather than a sales forecast.
- **Writing the favoured concept better.** When one concept gets clearer writing, a stronger headline or a lower price, the test measures the copywriting, not the idea. Recovery: use one template for every concept, have someone outside the project review them for balance, and test price separately if it differs.
- **Testing with the wrong people.** A cheap panel of general consumers will happily rate a B2B finance tool. Recovery: screen strictly on segment, role and buying involvement, and remove respondents who fail the screener or an attention check.
