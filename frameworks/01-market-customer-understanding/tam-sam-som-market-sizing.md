# TAM/SAM/SOM Market Sizing

**What it is:** A way of estimating how big a market is in three nested layers. **Total addressable market (TAM)** is the revenue available if every possible customer for this kind of solution bought it. **Serviceable addressable market (SAM)** is the part of TAM your product can actually serve today, given its features, geography, price point and channels. **Serviceable obtainable market (SOM)** is the share of SAM you can realistically win in a set period, usually three years, given your competition and go-to-market capacity. No single person invented the approach; it grew out of venture capital and start-up practice, and Steve Blank and Bob Dorf's *The Startup Owner's Manual* is one of the best-documented versions. The layers matter because each answers a different question: TAM tells leadership whether the opportunity is worth pursuing at all, SAM tells PMM which segments to target, and SOM sets a realistic revenue goal. The most reliable method builds the numbers from the bottom up (number of target customers multiplied by what each would pay) rather than taking a percentage of a large analyst figure.

**When to use it:**
- **Choosing between segments in STP targeting**, where each candidate segment needs a size estimate before you compare attractiveness.
- **Building an investment case** for a new product, a new vertical or international expansion, where leadership or investors will ask how big the prize is.
- **Setting annual or three-year revenue goals** for a product line, so targets rest on the number of reachable customers rather than last year's growth plus a percentage.
- **A board deck or fundraising round** quotes a top-down market figure that nobody in the business can explain or defend.
- **Comparing growth options** (for example, in an Ansoff Matrix or BCG Growth-Share Matrix review), where each option needs a sized market so risk and reward can be compared.

**How to run it:**
1. **Define the customer and the problem precisely.** Write one sentence naming who buys, what problem the product solves, and what counts as a sale. "Mid-sized UK accountancy firms buying practice management software" can be sized; "SMBs that need productivity" cannot.
2. **Build TAM from the bottom up.** Count every organisation (or person) that has the problem, using public business registers, industry associations, LinkedIn company counts or census data. Multiply by the annual contract value a typical customer would pay. Record every source.
3. **Cross-check with a top-down estimate.** Find an analyst or industry figure for the category and compare it with your bottom-up TAM. A gap of more than about two times means one of your assumptions is wrong; investigate before moving on.
4. **Narrow to SAM.** Remove customers you cannot serve today: the wrong geography, the wrong size, missing integrations, required compliance you lack, or a channel you do not have. Each cut should be a named, sourced filter.
5. **Estimate SOM.** Apply a realistic share of SAM for the next three years based on evidence: your current win rate, sales capacity, marketing reach, and how many competitors split the market. A new entrant claiming more than a low single-digit share of SAM in three years needs strong evidence.
6. **Document assumptions and ranges.** Present a low, central and high case, with the two or three assumptions that move the number most. Leadership needs to see which assumptions to test.
7. **Feed the numbers into decisions.** Use SAM by segment in STP targeting, SOM in revenue planning and goal setting, and TAM in investment cases.
8. **Refresh when inputs change.** Update the model when pricing changes, when the product opens a new segment, or at least once a year in planning.

**Cadence & ownership:** PMM commonly runs market sizing at early and growth-stage companies, because it holds the segmentation, pricing and competitive inputs. At a scaled company, a strategy, finance or market research team often owns the official model, with PMM supplying segment definitions and validating assumptions against customer evidence; finance usually signs off any figure used in an investor or board document. A full model takes one to three weeks the first time. Refresh it annually during planning, and whenever pricing, target segments or geography change.

**Example:** Fictional dental practice software company Smilebook wanted to expand from the UK into Germany. The board deck quoted a European dental software market of €2.1 billion from an analyst report. The PMM rebuilt the number from the bottom up. A count from public registers came to roughly 40,000 German dental practices (all figures in this example are illustrative); at Smilebook's average contract of €3,600 a year, that gave a German TAM of about €144 million. Removing practices with more than ten chairs (which needed features Smilebook lacked) and practices tied into long contracts with the two dominant local vendors left a SAM of about 14,000 practices, or €50 million. Based on Smilebook's UK win rate of 18% in competitive deals, a planned sales team of six, and a 30-month ramp, the PMM estimated a three-year SOM of 3% of SAM, or about €1.5 million in annual recurring revenue. Leadership cut the German revenue target from €6 million to €2 million, focused the launch on practices with two to eight chairs, and hit €1.7 million in year three.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Leading with a top-down figure.** Taking 1% of a huge analyst number produces a figure nobody can defend and that hides who the customers are. Recovery: build bottom-up first, then use the analyst figure only as a cross-check.
- **Anchoring TAM to today's spending.** Products that make a job cheaper or easier can grow the market well beyond current spend, a point Bill Gurley made in 2014 about early valuations of Uber that sized it against the existing taxi market. Recovery: size TAM on the number of customers with the problem and what they would pay, and show a separate case for market expansion if you expect one.
- **Treating SOM as a goal rather than an estimate.** Teams pick an ambitious share and work backwards, so SOM stops reflecting capacity or competition. Recovery: derive SOM from win rates, sales capacity and reach, and make any stretch goal a separate, labelled number.
