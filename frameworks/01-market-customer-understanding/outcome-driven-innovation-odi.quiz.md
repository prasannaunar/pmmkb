# Quiz: Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI)

1. A PMM is drafting outcome statements for an ODI survey. Several read "Have an AI clause library" and "Offer a CRM integration". What should the PMM do before fieldwork?
   - **A.** Rewrite each as a direction, metric, object and clarifier, naming no feature.
   - **B.** Keep them, and add more statements so that every planned feature is covered.
   - **C.** Survey them as written, then translate the top-rated ones into outcomes later.
   - **D.** Replace the survey with switch interviews that probe each feature in depth.

   **Correct answer: A.** Statements that name a feature only tell you whether people like that feature. A well-formed outcome has a direction, a metric, an object and a clarifier, and stays true whatever product the customer uses.

   *Why not B:* Adding more feature statements makes the list longer without fixing it. The survey would still measure appetite for solutions, not which outcomes are unmet.

   *Why not C:* This is the near miss: translating to outcomes is right, but doing it after fieldwork reverses the sequence. The ratings would already be tied to features, so the scores could not be reused for the outcomes.

   *Why not D:* Switch interviews find why people switched; they do not test features or quantify unmet outcomes. The problem is the statement wording, which the survey can still fix.

2. A team has budget for 90 survey respondents and expects to find about three outcome-based segments. Leadership wants named segments in the readout. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Raise the sample to at least 100 respondents per expected segment.
   - **B.** Run the 90 responses, cluster them, and note the small sample size.
   - **C.** Cut the outcome list in half so each respondent rates fewer items.
   - **D.** Group the 90 respondents by company size and industry instead.

   **Correct answer: A.** A sample this small can rank outcomes, but it cannot support a stable cluster analysis. Sizing the sample for the segments you expect, before fieldwork, is what makes the segmentation trustworthy.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: clustering is the right step, but 90 responses is the wrong evidence for it. A caveat does not stop leadership acting on segments that may be noise.

   *Why not C:* A shorter list reduces respondent fatigue but does nothing for segment stability. It also risks dropping the outcomes that separate one segment from another.

   *Why not D:* Firmographic groups are not outcome-based segments. ODI's value is finding buyers who share unmet needs, which company size and industry often hide.

3. An ODI study's full-sample results show no outcome scoring above 11. Leadership concludes the market is well served and proposes dropping the new product idea. What should the PMM do first?
   - **A.** Cluster respondents on opportunity scores and read each segment.
   - **B.** Accept the result and move the budget to the existing product's roadmap.
   - **C.** Re-run the survey with a larger sample to see if the top scores rise.
   - **D.** Re-read the average scores split by company size and by industry.

   **Correct answer: A.** Averages blend underserved and overserved customers, so a segment with a strong unmet need can vanish into a middling score. Clustering on opportunity scores shows whether any group has outcomes well above the average.

   *Why not B:* Acting on the average alone is the pitfall the method warns against. The idea may suit one segment even if the market as a whole looks served.

   *Why not C:* A larger sample makes the averages more precise, not different. The blending effect stays the same however many people respond.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: splitting the data is right, but firmographics are the wrong cut. Segments with distinct unmet needs often cut across company size and industry, so only clustering on the scores finds them.

4. A PMM needs to know, for the target segment, which outcomes are both important and poorly satisfied, so they can choose what to lead messaging with. Which method fits?
   - **A.** ODI, with every outcome rated for both importance and satisfaction.
   - **B.** MaxDiff, with respondents picking the most and least important outcomes.
   - **C.** Kano, with each planned feature classed as must-be or delighter.
   - **D.** Switch interviews, collecting the words recent buyers use for the job.

   **Correct answer: A.** The question needs the gap between importance and satisfaction, which is exactly what the opportunity score measures. The highest-scoring outcomes for the segment are the strongest candidates to lead messaging.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: MaxDiff ranks relative importance well, but it asks nothing about satisfaction. A very important outcome that customers already find well served would top its ranking and still be a weak message.

   *Why not C:* Kano classifies features by their effect on satisfaction. The question is about outcomes and the size of the gap, not feature categories.

   *Why not D:* Switch interviews give useful language and triggers, but they are qualitative. They cannot show which outcomes are underserved across a segment.

5. An ODI study finds a segment, 30% of respondents, where satisfaction is higher than importance on most outcomes. What is the strongest use of this finding?
   - **A.** Test a simpler, lower-priced offer aimed at that segment.
   - **B.** Offer that segment a discount on the existing full product.
   - **C.** Lead that segment's messaging with the most advanced features.
   - **D.** Make it the primary target segment for the next launch campaign.

   **Correct answer: A.** An overserved segment gets more than it values, so it is the natural opening for a simpler, cheaper offer. Testing one first means the company takes that opening before a low-cost entrant does.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: a lower price is right, but on the same full product it is incomplete. The segment does not value the extra capability, so a discount gives away margin without offering the simpler product it would choose.

   *Why not C:* Advanced features address outcomes this segment already rates as well served. More capability is the opposite of what it values.

   *Why not D:* An overserved segment has few unmet outcomes to win on. The primary target should be a segment with strong underserved outcomes.
