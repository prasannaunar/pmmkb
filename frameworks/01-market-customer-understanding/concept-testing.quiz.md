# Quiz: Concept Testing

1. A concept test shows 38% of respondents would "definitely or probably buy" a new product. The product manager wants to forecast first-year sales from this figure. What should the PMM advise?
   - **A.** Compare it with a benchmark tested the same way and treat it as a ranking.
   - **B.** Use it as the forecast, but apply a 50% discount to cover respondent optimism.
   - **C.** Re-run the test with a larger sample, so the purchase intent figure is precise.
   - **D.** Set the score aside and rely only on the open answers for the build decision.

   **Correct answer: A.** Respondents overstate what they will buy, so a purchase intent score means little on its own. Comparing it with a benchmark tested the same way, and using it to rank concepts, is the defensible reading.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it recognises overstatement, but corrects it with an arbitrary discount. Without a benchmark, any adjustment is a guess presented as a forecast.

   *Why not C:* A larger sample makes the figure more precise but not more realistic. Overstatement is a bias, not a sampling error.

   *Why not D:* The open answers explain the score, but discarding the score throws away the comparison between concepts. Both are needed.

2. A team tests two concepts. The favoured one has a sharp headline, customer-style language and a price of £9; the other has a plain description and a price of £15. The favoured concept wins clearly. What is wrong with the test?
   - **A.** The concepts differed in writing and price, so it measured copy, not ideas.
   - **B.** The sample was probably too small, so the result is likely to be random noise.
   - **C.** A sequential monadic design should have been used to compare the two directly.
   - **D.** The test should have included a third concept to act as a neutral control option.

   **Correct answer: A.** When one concept gets better writing or a lower price, the test measures the copywriting and the price, not the idea. The fix is one template for every concept, a balance review by someone outside the project, and testing price separately.

   *Why not B:* Nothing suggests the sample was small. The flaw is in how the concepts were written, which a larger sample would only confirm.

   *Why not C:* This is the near miss: design choice matters, but switching designs would not fix unequal concepts. Both designs need concepts written to the same template.

   *Why not D:* A third concept would not correct the imbalance between the first two. The problem is unequal treatment, not a missing control.

3. A B2B finance software company runs a cheap concept test on a general consumer panel. Results are positive, but sales finds no demand at launch. What most likely went wrong?
   - **A.** The sample was not screened for segment, role and involvement in buying.
   - **B.** The test should have been monadic, so each respondent saw just one concept.
   - **C.** The concept should have included a price, so the respondents took it seriously.
   - **D.** The launch messaging should have been tested first, before the concept itself.

   **Correct answer: A.** A general panel will happily rate a B2B finance tool it would never buy. Strict screening on segment, role and buying involvement, using the ICP, is what makes the result meaningful.

   *Why not B:* Monadic design reduces order effects, but it cannot fix a sample of the wrong people. Screening is the missing step.

   *Why not C:* This is the near miss: price can sharpen responses, but even a priced concept rated by non-buyers tells you little. The respondents, not the concept, were the flaw.

   *Why not D:* Message testing comes after the concept is chosen. Testing messages with the same unscreened panel would repeat the error.

4. Product leadership is split between two directions for the next product. A PMM proposes a concept test. What should the PMM do before writing the concepts?
   - **A.** State the decision the result will drive and the threshold that changes it.
   - **B.** Recruit the panel and run the survey quickly, so results arrive before planning.
   - **C.** Choose the preferred direction, so the test can confirm it to the wider team.
   - **D.** Write the launch messaging for both directions, so it can be tested alongside.

   **Correct answer: A.** The method starts by writing the decision and the threshold, such as a minimum share saying they would buy. Without that, the team can read any result as support for the direction it already preferred.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: speed matters, but running fieldwork before agreeing the decision and threshold reverses the sequence. The result then has no agreed meaning.

   *Why not C:* Designing a test to confirm a preference defeats its purpose. A concept test exists to settle the argument with customer evidence.

   *Why not D:* Launch messaging belongs after the concept is chosen. Testing messages now would mix two questions in one study.

5. A PMM must screen five early product ideas for one segment on a tight budget, before choosing one or two for deeper work. Which design fits best?
   - **A.** Sequential monadic, with each respondent rating several ideas in turn.
   - **B.** Monadic, with a separate sample of at least 150 respondents for every idea.
   - **C.** A message test of five headlines, to see which wording customers prefer.
   - **D.** A pricing study on all five ideas, to see which can command the highest price.

   **Correct answer: A.** Sequential monadic needs fewer respondents and suits early screening of three to five ideas on a tight budget. Randomising order limits how much one idea colours the next.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: monadic gives the cleanest scores, but five separate samples is costly for early screening. It suits the later, high-stakes comparison.

   *Why not C:* Message testing asks which words describe an existing offer best. The question here is which offer customers want.

   *Why not D:* Pricing studies assume demand exists. Screening ideas for appeal comes before testing what each could cost.
