# Message Architecture (Messaging House)

**What it is:** A hierarchy that turns a positioning statement into copy a whole go-to-market team can write from. It is usually drawn as a "messaging house": a single core message sits at the top like a roof, three or four value pillars hold it up like walls, and proof points form the foundation beneath each pillar. The core message says what you stand for in one line. Each pillar names one reason a buyer should believe it. Each proof point is the evidence that makes the pillar credible. The framework fills a gap the knowledge base otherwise jumps over: positioning tells you what to stand for, and campaign copy is what a customer reads, but nothing connects the two. Without a message architecture, every writer, rep, and landing page reinvents the wording, and the market hears four products instead of one. The house is the shared source of truth that keeps a website headline, a sales deck, an analyst briefing, and a launch email all saying the same thing in the same order of priority.

**When to use it:**
- **You have a positioning statement but no copy standard.** Positioning is agreed, yet each asset phrases the value differently. Build the house before writing a website, a launch, or a sales deck.
- **Marketing, sales, and product tell different stories.** A messaging house forces one agreed hierarchy that all three write from.
- **You are about to launch or reposition.** The house is the brief every downstream asset is built against, so write it before collateral, not after.
- **You are briefing a new writer, agency, or account executive.** Handing over a house is faster and more consistent than handing over a folder of old decks.
- **A message test or win/loss study shows buyers are confused.** When prospects cannot repeat back what you do, the hierarchy is usually missing or overloaded.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company, a Head of Product Marketing owns the finished house and its governance, briefing content, sales enablement, and brand teams against it rather than letting each write independently. A solo or founding PMM owns the entire house alone and is usually also the one briefing the agency or first sales hire directly. Regardless of company stage, one named PMM should hold version control and the quarterly review; splitting ownership across multiple writers is what causes the drift the house exists to prevent.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Start from validated positioning.** The house is built on a positioning statement (from Geoffrey Moore's template or April Dunford's canvas) that already names the target segment, the category, and the core differentiator. Do not start here if positioning is still unsettled; you will only encode the confusion.
2. **Write the core message (the roof).** One sentence, in the buyer's language, led by the outcome rather than the mechanism. It should be repeatable from memory and true only of you. Draft three or four candidates and choose by clarity, not cleverness.
3. **Define three or four value pillars (the walls).** Each pillar is a distinct theme that maps to a priority the target buyer already has, and answers "why believe the core message?". Keep them to three or four, distinct enough that a claim clearly belongs under one pillar and not two. Name them as benefits, not feature categories.
4. **Populate proof points (the foundation).** Under each pillar, list two to four proofs: a specific capability, a quantified result, a named customer outcome, or third-party validation. A pillar with no proof is a slogan; cut it or find the evidence.
5. **Add the audience layer.** For each priority persona, note which pillars lead and how the wording shifts. A practical output is a message matrix: personas (or journey stages) down the side, the adapted core message and pillar emphasis across the top.
6. **Pressure-test and cut jargon.** Check every line for clarity, differentiation, and evidence. Test the draft with a target-buyer panel or with front-line sales; if they cannot restate the core message, simplify. Remove any claim a competitor could make word for word.
7. **Publish and govern.** Store the house as the single source of truth, brief every writer against it, and version it. Assign one owner (PMM) and review it each quarter or whenever positioning changes.

**Example:** Sentra, a fictional cloud-security scale-up, had a sharp positioning statement but four different pitches in the wild: the website said "cloud security posture management", the sales deck said "cloud risk platform", the analyst deck said "CNAPP", and the launch email led on a feature list. Inbound demo-request conversion sat at 1.9%, and new account executives took roughly 11 weeks to pitch confidently. PMM built a messaging house over two weeks. Core message: "Fix the cloud risks attackers can actually reach, before they do." Three pillars: prioritise by real exploitability rather than raw vulnerability counts; fix inside the developer workflow teams already use; prove posture to auditors in minutes. Each pillar carried proof: an average 80% cut in alert volume, native Jira and GitHub actions, and one-click SOC 2 evidence export, each tied to a named beta customer. The house became the brief for a website rebuild, the sales deck, and the launch. Within one quarter, demo-request conversion rose to 3.1%, message-test clarity scores (run with a 30-person buyer panel) improved from 54% to 82% "immediately clear", and new-hire ramp to first confident pitch fell from 11 weeks to 6. PMM tracked those three numbers as the explicit success measures for the project.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Pillars that are really a feature list.** When pillars read "Integrations", "Reporting", "Security", the house becomes a spec sheet and the buyer priority disappears. Why it matters: feature-named pillars invite endless additions and never test whether a claim matters to the buyer. Recovery: rewrite each pillar as a buyer benefit and require at least two proof points; a pillar that cannot earn proof gets cut.
- **Building the house before positioning is settled.** A polished hierarchy on top of vague positioning just spreads the vagueness faster and further. Why it matters: every downstream asset inherits the flaw, so the fix later is expensive. Recovery: gate the house behind a validated positioning statement; if you cannot fill the roof in one clear sentence, return to positioning first.
- **Writing it once and never governing it.** Teams launch the house, then quietly drift back to improvised wording within two quarters. Why it matters: the market re-fragments and the house becomes a stale document no one trusts. Recovery: give one owner (PMM) accountability, version the file, embed it in every campaign and enablement brief, and review it each quarter against live sales language.

**Sources:** Product Marketing Alliance
- No single originator; the messaging house has circulated in corporate communications and PR practice since at least the 1990s. The most documented current version for product marketing is Product Marketing Alliance's [guide to messaging frameworks](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/your-guide-to-messaging/), which sets out the same primary message, supporting pillars, and proof points structure under a different label.

**See also:** Geoffrey Moore's Positioning Statement Framework (produces the positioning statement the house is built on); April Dunford's 5-Component Positioning Canvas (an alternative positioning input that feeds the roof and pillars); Value Proposition Canvas (source pillars and proof points from documented customer gains and pains); Segmentation–Targeting–Positioning (STP) Framework (defines the personas the audience layer adapts the house for).
