# Category 3: Competitive Strategy

## Category Quiz

1. A board asks "where do we sit versus the competition?" for the second time after a detailed feature-comparison spreadsheet failed to land in the first briefing. The team needs a fast, visual way to show the market's structure on a dimension buyers actually care about. Which tool should the PMM reach for?
   - **A.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **B.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **C.** CI Program Maturity Model
   - **D.** Category Design

   **Correct answer: A.** The Perceptual Map is purpose-built for exactly this situation: a fast, visual read on where genuine open space exists, built from axes buyers actually care about, when a checklist of feature comparisons has already failed to communicate the finding.

   *Why not B:* Bowman's Strategic Clock always uses the same two fixed axes, price and perceived value; it is the right tool for a price-to-value defensibility question, not a general "where do we sit" visual briefing on buyer-chosen attributes.

   *Why not C:* The CI Program Maturity Model diagnoses the health of the competitive intelligence function itself, not the company's market position; it would not produce the visual comparison the board is asking for.

   *Why not D:* Category Design is a multi-year strategic bet to reframe the market entirely, a far larger commitment than what a board's positioning question calls for here.

2. A B2B SaaS company has just lost three competitive deals in a row to a new entrant. Sales reports that prospects keep citing the competitor's messaging around speed of deployment, and internal reviews confirm the product is not meaningfully behind on this dimension, it has simply never been positioned around it. Which framework should the PMM prioritise?
   - **A.** Product Differentiation Strategy Framework
   - **B.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **C.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **D.** CI Program Maturity Model

   **Correct answer: A.** This is a differentiation gap: a real strength exists but has not been identified, scored for meaningfulness, uniqueness, and defensibility, and embedded into messaging and sales conversations. The framework exists precisely to turn an unclaimed strength into an operational differentiator across product, pricing, and messaging.

   *Why not B:* Nothing in this scenario describes a price-to-value mismatch; the product's price has not changed and value has not visibly dropped. The issue is an unclaimed differentiator, not a defensibility problem on the price-value axis.

   *Why not C:* A Perceptual Map could visualise this gap, but it is a diagnostic snapshot, not the framework that builds the operational commitment, roadmap alignment, and sales enablement needed to close it.

   *Why not D:* The CI Program Maturity Model would help if the underlying problem were stale or poorly distributed competitive intelligence; here the intelligence is already known internally, the gap is in positioning and alignment, not the CI function.

3. A company already wins the majority of its head-to-head competitive deals and has a well-regarded differentiation story. Despite this, analysts and investors keep asking "so who do you actually take share from?", and revenue growth has plateaued because the existing category's overall ceiling is capped, regardless of the company's share within it. Which is the appropriate next step?
   - **A.** Category Design
   - **B.** Product Differentiation Strategy Framework
   - **C.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **D.** Bowman's Strategic Clock

   **Correct answer: A.** This is exactly the trigger the methodology names: out-executing competitors inside the existing category is not the constraint, since the company is already winning; the category itself has a capped ceiling. That calls for the multi-year, executive-sponsored bet of defining a new category, not further optimisation within the old one.

   *Why not B:* The company already has a strong, well-regarded differentiation story; the problem described is not a lack of differentiation but a structural ceiling on the category itself, which differentiation within the existing frame cannot solve.

   *Why not C:* A Perceptual Map would show where the company sits within the existing category's attributes; it cannot address a ceiling created by the category definition itself, which requires reframing the buyer's problem entirely.

   *Why not D:* Bowman's Clock addresses whether a price-to-value position is defensible; the scenario describes a market-size and framing ceiling, not a pricing or value-perception problem.

4. Sales reps say competitive intelligence is stale, hard to find, and sometimes contradicts what they hear live in deals. Leadership assumes the underlying research must be poor quality. Which tool should the PMM use to properly diagnose this?
   - **A.** CI Program Maturity Model
   - **B.** Category Design
   - **C.** Product Differentiation Strategy Framework
   - **D.** Perceptual Map (2x2)

   **Correct answer: A.** This is a textbook symptom the model is designed to diagnose: stale, hard-to-find, contradictory intel almost always points to a function stuck at Ad Hoc or Adopting on repository, process, and distribution, not a research-quality problem. Scoring the four dimensions reveals exactly where the gap sits.

   *Why not B:* Category Design addresses market and category framing, an entirely different problem from whether the CI function's internal processes and distribution are mature enough to serve sales reliably.

   *Why not C:* Product Differentiation Strategy Framework builds and embeds a competitive advantage into the product and messaging; it does not diagnose whether the underlying competitive intelligence operation itself is functioning well.

   *Why not D:* A Perceptual Map visualises competitive position on chosen buyer attributes; it says nothing about whether the internal CI process that would feed such a map is mature, current, or well distributed.

5. During a round of enterprise renewals, several long-standing customers say for the first time that the price no longer feels justified, even though nothing in the contract terms has changed. Which framework directly addresses this?
   - **A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **B.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **C.** Category Design
   - **D.** CI Program Maturity Model

   **Correct answer: A.** A shift in perceived value with price held constant is precisely the price-to-value mismatch Bowman's Strategic Clock is built to diagnose; pricing renegotiations that reveal customers no longer see the value they once did are one of its named triggers.

   *Why not B:* A Perceptual Map uses buyer-chosen axes that vary case by case; it is not the fixed price-and-value diagnostic this specific renewal-pricing problem calls for.

   *Why not C:* Category Design addresses whether the market frame itself is limiting growth; a shift in perceived value during renewals is a narrower, more immediate pricing and positioning question, not a category-reframing one.

   *Why not D:* The CI Program Maturity Model diagnoses the competitive intelligence function's own maturity; it has no direct bearing on a customer's changing perception of value during a renewal conversation.

6. A team needs to determine whether their current price is defensible against what customers perceive their product to be worth, using a fixed, repeatable diagnostic they can re-run every two quarters. Which tool is purpose-built for this, rather than one that would require choosing new axes each time?
   - **A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock
   - **B.** Perceptual Map (2x2)
   - **C.** Product Differentiation Strategy Framework
   - **D.** Category Design

   **Correct answer: A.** Bowman's Strategic Clock always uses the same two axes, price and perceived value, which is exactly what makes it repeatable and directly comparable across re-plotting cycles; the Perceptual Map, by contrast, is deliberately axis-agnostic and its dimensions are chosen fresh each time.

   *Why not B:* The Perceptual Map's strength is flexibility: choosing whichever two buyer-relevant attributes matter most for a given question. That flexibility makes it less suited to a fixed, repeatable price-versus-value tracking exercise than the clock's stable axes.

   *Why not C:* Product Differentiation Strategy Framework identifies and embeds a competitive advantage; it does not test price-to-value defensibility directly or provide a repeatable two-axis diagnostic.

   *Why not D:* Category Design operates at the level of market framing, not a repeatable price-value tracking exercise; it is a multi-year strategic bet, not a quarterly diagnostic.

7. A team has just validated a white-space finding with a Perceptual Map: buyers place them in an unoccupied quadrant they genuinely want. What should happen next?
   - **A.** Turn to the Product Differentiation Strategy Framework to embed the validated attributes into the roadmap, pricing, messaging, and sales process as an operational commitment.
   - **B.** Treat the map itself as the finished positioning deliverable and move on to the next project.
   - **C.** Re-run Bowman's Strategic Clock using the map's two axes in place of price and perceived value.
   - **D.** Immediately launch a category design programme built around the white-space quadrant.

   **Correct answer: A.** A validated map is explicitly an input to further work, not a finished positioning statement; the natural next step is operationalising the finding, embedding it into the roadmap, pricing, messaging, and sales process the way the Product Differentiation Strategy Framework's later steps describe.

   *Why not B:* The entry is explicit that the map is a diagnostic to run in an afternoon, not a replacement for the deeper positioning or differentiation work that must follow a validated finding.

   *Why not C:* Bowman's Clock's axes are fixed by design, price and perceived value; substituting the map's chosen axes changes what the clock is meant to test and is not how the two tools are meant to work together.

   *Why not D:* A single validated white-space finding does not by itself meet the bar for a multi-year, executive-sponsored category bet; that requires the additional evidence and executive sponsorship Category Design's own first step calls for.

8. A company has strong, well-embedded differentiation, confirmed through win-loss data, sales alignment, and consistent execution across every touchpoint. Growth has still plateaued, and analysts keep framing the company as a challenger inside a category it has already proven it can win consistently. What does this combination of facts suggest?
   - **A.** The differentiation work has succeeded, but the ceiling is now the category itself, which is the specific situation that justifies considering Category Design.
   - **B.** The differentiation strategy has failed and should be rebuilt from the MUD scoring stage.
   - **C.** The company should run a Perceptual Map to find a new differentiator, since the existing one is no longer working.
   - **D.** The CI Program Maturity Model should be applied to determine why analysts still frame the company as a challenger.

   **Correct answer: A.** Strong, validated differentiation combined with a plateau caused by the category's own ceiling, not competitive losses, is precisely the pattern that separates a differentiation problem from a category-framing problem; it is the trigger for considering the larger, multi-year Category Design bet.

   *Why not B:* The facts describe differentiation that is working, confirmed by win-loss data and consistent execution; there is no evidence the MUD scoring or execution has failed, which rules out rebuilding it from scratch.

   *Why not C:* A new differentiator would still operate inside the same category ceiling; the problem described is structural to the category itself, not a sign the current differentiation has stopped working.

   *Why not D:* Analyst framing here reflects the category's definition, not the competitive intelligence function's maturity; CI process quality is not what is causing analysts to view the company as a challenger.

9. A newly appointed CI lead scores the function as Impacting because the team recently produced one excellent, thorough battlecard for the company's top-named competitor. Four other competitors that come up regularly in live deals have no current materials at all. What is the flaw in this self-assessment?
   - **A.** The function should be scored on typical coverage across the full competitive set that matters in deals, not on its single strongest deliverable.
   - **B.** The score is correct, since reaching Impacting only requires one high-quality example.
   - **C.** The team should score itself as Transforming instead, since the one battlecard shows executive-level rigour.
   - **D.** The scoring exercise cannot be trusted unless it is conducted by an outside consultant.

   **Correct answer: A.** Scoring on the best deliverable rather than typical performance is a named pitfall of the model: a single excellent battlecard does not make the whole function Impacting if several regularly relevant competitors have nothing current at all. An honest score reflects coverage across the competitors that actually come up in live deals.

   *Why not B:* One strong example is explicitly insufficient evidence under the model; the four uncovered competitors are exactly where the real gap, and the real risk in live deals, will show up.

   *Why not C:* Transforming requires embedded, continuously measured, company-wide practice; a single battlecard for one competitor, however strong, falls far short of that bar.

   *Why not D:* The model does not require external validation; the fix here is applying the model's own scoring guidance honestly against typical coverage, which the internal team is capable of doing itself.

10. A company runs a successful category design launch, complete with a published Point of View, analyst briefings, and two flagship customer references. Six months later, the team is fully reassigned to other projects. A competitor then begins using the same category term without having done any of the underlying research. What was the failure?
    - **A.** The company fully resourced Define and Develop but abandoned Dominate, leaving the category vulnerable to being co-opted by a competitor who did none of the original work.
    - **B.** The original flagship customer references were not credible enough to sustain the category long-term.
    - **C.** The category name should have been trademarked before launch to prevent competitor use.
    - **D.** The analyst briefings should have been replaced with a larger paid advertising budget.

    **Correct answer: A.** Dominate is meant to run for years, not one launch cycle, through sustained evangelism, defending the term as imitators appear, and refreshing the point of view as the market evolves. Disbanding the team after a successful launch is exactly how a well-resourced competitor can adopt the vocabulary with none of the original research or credibility behind it.

    *Why not B:* The scenario describes a launch that succeeded well enough to draw a competitor's attention; the failure is what happened after launch, not a flaw in the original flagship customer selection.

    *Why not C:* Category names describing a market problem are generally not defensible through trademark in the way this situation would require; the real defence is sustained, ongoing evangelism and vocabulary ownership, not a legal filing.

    *Why not D:* Advertising does not build the credibility that ongoing analyst relationships and flagship customer proof provide; the entry favours sustained relationship-based seeding over advertising, and the failure here is stopping that work entirely, not the choice of channel.

## Bowman's Strategic Clock

**Type:** Framework.

**What it is:** A strategic positioning model that maps out eight different competitive strategies based on two dimensions: the price customers pay and the value they perceive in your product. Developed by Cliff Bowman and David Faulkner in 1996, it extends Porter's three generic strategies to show companies exactly where they sit in their market and where they should move to compete more effectively. The clock's central idea is that price and perceived value must be managed together, not separately; a product priced above the value customers perceive will lose share no matter how good the underlying engineering is, and a product priced well below its perceived value is simply leaving margin on the table.

**When to use it:** Use this when you're setting overall competitive strategy, pricing, and positioning for a market or customer segment. It answers: "What's our defensible competitive advantage?" and "How does our price-to-value ratio compare to rivals?" Apply it when entering a new segment, repositioning an existing product, or rationalising a product portfolio that's drifting between incompatible strategies. Quarterly portfolio reviews or moments when sales stalls are common triggers, as are pricing renegotiations that reveal customers no longer see the value they once did.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company with a specialised PMM team, a pricing-focused PMM or the Head of Pricing typically runs the research and plots the clock, but final decision rights on the target position sit with the VP Product or CPO, since the move commits cost structure and roadmap trade-offs beyond what PMM controls alone. At solo or founding-PMM stage, the founding PMM owns the whole exercise and takes the recommendation straight to the CEO, since there is no dedicated pricing or product function to hand it to.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Define your scope.** Pick one market segment and identify 3–5 direct competitors to plot alongside you. Trying to plot an entire portfolio against every rival at once produces a chart too noisy to act on; keep the scope tight enough that the positions are genuinely comparable.
2. **Assess perceived value.** Use customer research (win-loss interviews, conjoint analysis, NPS data) to measure what customers truly value: performance, reliability, brand, experience, ease of use. Weight these dimensions by how much they actually influence the purchase decision, not by how much internal effort your team has put into them; teams routinely overrate features they built and underrate ones customers mention unprompted.
3. **Quantify relative price.** Translate transaction cost, support cost, and switching cost into a relative price index versus competitors. List price alone understates true cost; implementation time, training, and integration effort all belong in the comparison, since customers experience them as part of the price even when no invoice line captures them.
4. **Map positions on the clock.** Plot your offering and competitors across the eight positions: low price/low value, low price, hybrid, differentiation, focused differentiation, and the three "increased price/standard value or lower" zones that sit outside a defensible strategy. Positions 1–5 are generally defensible; positions 6–8 are unstable except under monopoly conditions or where switching costs are unusually high.
5. **Diagnose gaps.** Identify where you sit. If in an unstable zone, decide deliberately: improve value (product, UX, features), lower price (cost redesign), or move to a defensible position that better matches your actual cost structure and brand strength.
6. **Align operations.** For your target position, align cost structure, roadmap, messaging, and pricing. Low-price plays need cost discipline and operational efficiency; differentiation plays need sustained R&D and brand investment. Trying to run both playbooks at once, cutting costs while also investing heavily in differentiation, is how companies drift into the unstable zone in the first place.
7. **Track the move over time.** Re-plot the clock every two quarters using fresh win-loss and pricing data. A successful repositioning should show measurable movement toward the target position, not just a one-off announcement that the strategy has changed.

**Example:** A $1.1B smartphone maker competed in position 6 to 8 (high price, low perceived value) due to bloated design and persistent battery issues that showed up repeatedly in customer complaints and review scores. Win-loss research across 45 lost enterprise deals and 300 consumer surveys showed customers consistently valued battery life, camera performance, and durability above the extra features the product had accumulated over three generations. Leadership targeted position 3, a hybrid strategy offering good value at a competitive price: they cut 40% of rarely used features, redesigned the chassis for cost efficiency while protecting the high-willingness-to-pay attributes (battery, camera, durability), and reduced the bill of materials by 18% without touching the components customers actually cared about. Messaging shifted from a spec sheet approach ("6.7-inch display, 12GB RAM") to an outcomes approach ("two-day battery life, no compromises"). Within 6 months, win rate improved from 22% to 34% in head-to-head competitive deals, average selling price held steady despite the cost reduction, and customer satisfaction scores for battery life rose from 58% to 79%, the specific metric the team had flagged at the outset as proof the repositioning had actually worked rather than just shifted the marketing copy.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Drifting between positions.** Companies gradually add features or cut costs without intentional strategy, ending up in position 6–8 (high price, low value). Decide once and commit.
- **Confusing perceived value with price.** A premium brand isn't "position 5" unless customers actually perceive the higher value. Without proof (certifications, outcomes, customer testimonials), you're just expensive.
- **Plotting the clock once and never revisiting it.** Teams often run the exercise as a one-time workshop ahead of an annual plan, then never check whether the intended move actually happened. Recovery: build the re-plotting step into a recurring cadence (every two quarters is usually enough), using the same win-loss and pricing data sources each time so the comparison is consistent, and hold whoever owns the target position accountable for showing measurable movement, not just intent.

**Sources:**
- Cliff Bowman & David Faulkner, [*Competitive and Corporate Strategy*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL10235705M/Competitive_and_Corporate_Strategy) (Irwin, 1996), the book that introduced the Strategic Clock, extending Michael Porter's generic strategies to eight positions based on price and perceived value.

**See also:** Product Differentiation Strategy Framework (develop the differentiation that supports your chosen position on the clock); STP Framework (ensure your target segment actually values the position you're claiming); Geoffrey Moore's Positioning Statement Framework (articulate how your clock position translates into a clear positioning statement).

Win-loss interviews, however, show buyers routinely describe the product as "expensive but not obviously better." What does this reveal?

   - **B.** The team likely cannot tell whether the intended repositioning actually happened, and should build re-plotting into a recurring cadence using consistent win-loss and pricing data.

   *Why not C:* The clock remains useful precisely because it can be re-run to track movement over time, not just as a one-off diagnostic; abandoning it discards the ongoing tracking value the framework is designed to provide.

   *Why not D:* Waiting for a new competitor as the sole trigger ignores that positions can drift from internal decisions alone, such as gradual feature creep or cost changes, entirely independent of competitor moves.

## Product Differentiation Strategy Framework

**Type:** Framework.

**What it is:** A structured approach to identifying what makes your product uniquely valuable and then embedding that distinctiveness into every part of how you build, position, and sell it. Rather than competing mainly on price, differentiation shifts competition toward features, quality, brand, experience, or ecosystem that customers value enough to pay a premium or stay loyal for. The framework treats differentiation as an operational commitment rather than a marketing slogan; a differentiator that only appears in the messaging and nowhere in the product roadmap, pricing, or sales process will not survive contact with a competitor's counter-pitch.

**When to use it:** Employ this when you need to stand out in a crowded market, boost margins, or reduce price sensitivity. It's essential during a new product launch, when a segment becomes commoditised, or when your product is losing to lower-cost competitors on features that customers do not actually prioritise. Reach for it when defining go-to-market strategy, building a value proposition, or architecting a product portfolio (for example, good-better-best tiers), and revisit it whenever a competitor's move starts pulling your win rate down in deals you previously expected to win.

It is also the right tool whenever a sales team reports that deals are increasingly decided on price alone. That pattern usually means the market can no longer tell your product apart from the alternatives, which is a differentiation failure even if no one on the team has framed it that way yet. Treat "everyone is just discounting to win" as an early warning sign, not simply a pricing problem to be solved with a bigger discount.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company with a specialised PMM team, the Head of Product Marketing owns the differentiation strategy and its MUD scoring, but final sign-off sits jointly with the VP Product, since committing the roadmap to a differentiator constrains build priorities outside PMM's authority. At solo or founding-PMM stage, the founding PMM owns the exercise outright, working directly with the founder or Head of Engineering to confirm what is feasible to build and defend.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Analyse your competitive environment.** Map direct and indirect competitors on dimensions like price, quality, features, brand, and customer experience. Identify white space: the combination of attributes no competitor currently owns credibly.
2. **Identify customer value drivers.** Run interviews, surveys, and win-loss analysis. What do high-value customers prioritise? What do they complain about in competitors? What problems do they have that no one is solving well, or at all?
3. **Map differentiation opportunities.** List the available levers: innovation, quality, features, branding, customer service, ecosystem, design, or pricing model. Score each on three criteria (Meaningfulness, Uniqueness, Defensibility, the MUD framework). A differentiator scores well only if customers actually care about it (meaningful), competitors cannot easily copy it (unique), and you can sustain it over time (defensible); a lever that fails any one of the three is not worth building a strategy around.
4. **Define your unique value proposition.** Crystallise the one or two things that set you apart and that customers value. Make it a clear point of view, not a generic "better, faster, cheaper" claim that could apply to any competitor in the category.
5. **Align your teams.** Ensure product, engineering, marketing, and sales all understand and commit to the differentiation. Inconsistent execution kills even strong strategies: a sales team that still leads with price undermines a differentiation strategy the roadmap has spent two quarters building toward.
6. **Implement across touchpoints.** Embed differentiation into the product roadmap, pricing, packaging, messaging, support, and sales processes. Every customer interaction should reinforce it, from the first marketing email to the renewal conversation eighteen months later.
7. **Measure and refine.** Track win rates, retention, NPS, and market share growth. Update quarterly as markets evolve and competitors respond; a differentiator that worked last year can be neutralised by a fast-following competitor within a single product cycle.
8. **Stress-test against imitation.** Before committing significant investment, ask specifically how a well-resourced competitor could copy the differentiator within twelve months, and what would make that harder. If the honest answer is "nothing," treat the differentiator as temporary and plan the next one in parallel rather than assuming it will hold indefinitely.

**Example:** A B2B SaaS company competing in mid-market discovered, through 30 win-loss interviews, that it was perceived as mid-value at mid-price, indistinguishable from three close competitors. Research revealed the next-tier segment (enterprise) valued security certifications, integration depth, and responsive support, but the incumbent enterprise vendor held that space almost by default, with an ageing product that customers tolerated rather than loved. The team reoriented toward a hybrid strategy over two quarters: they added high-willingness-to-pay integrations (Salesforce, SAP, and Workday connectors), achieved SOC 2 Type II compliance, and launched a dedicated enterprise support tier with a four-hour response SLA, while deliberately pruning three low-usage features that had been consuming a third of engineering capacity for minimal customer value. Messaging shifted from "we're like them but cheaper" to "purpose-built for compliance-heavy verticals." Enterprise win rate improved 10 points in two quarters, from 24% to 34%, average contract value in the enterprise segment rose 22%, and net revenue retention in that segment climbed from 96% to 108%, the trio of metrics the team used to confirm the repositioning had translated into real commercial outcomes rather than just a new tagline.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Chasing competitor features instead of building from your strengths.** Copying what competitors do makes you a second-best version of them. Identify your unique competencies (engineering depth, brand, customer obsession) and build outward from there.
- **Claiming differentiation without proof.** "We have great customer service" is not differentiation until you back it with outcomes, awards, or customer testimonials. Validate claims with proof points before messaging.
- **Choosing a differentiator competitors can copy within a quarter.** Teams sometimes pick a differentiator that is meaningful and unique today but has no real defensibility, such as a single integration or a pricing gimmick, and are surprised when a competitor matches it within a release cycle. Recovery: run the MUD scoring exercise again with defensibility weighted more heavily, and pair any imitable differentiator with a second, harder-to-copy one (deep technical integration, proprietary data, or an accumulating brand asset) so the strategy does not collapse the moment one competitor responds.

## Perceptual Map (2x2)

**Type:** Framework.

**What it is:** A visual competitive tool that plots your product and its rivals on a simple grid built from two attributes that matter most to buyers, one on each axis, so the market's actual structure becomes visible at a glance rather than staying buried in a spreadsheet of feature comparisons. Where Bowman's Strategic Clock always uses the same two axes (price and perceived value) to test whether a competitive strategy is defensible, the perceptual map is deliberately axis-agnostic: the two dimensions are chosen fresh each time from whatever buyers say actually drives their choice; speed versus depth, simplicity versus configurability, self-serve versus high-touch. Originating in classical marketing (Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning*, 1981, popularised perceptual mapping alongside their broader positioning theory), the map earns its keep in PMM work as a fast, visual complement to deeper positioning frameworks: it is a diagnostic to run in an afternoon, not a replacement for the research those frameworks demand.

**When to use it:**
- You are entering a crowded market and need a fast, visual read on where genuine open space exists before committing to a positioning direction.
- Leadership or the board asks "where do we sit versus the competition?" and a spreadsheet of feature checkmarks has failed to make the answer land.
- A new competitor enters, or an incumbent repositions, and you need to see immediately whether the move opens or closes space for you.
- You are briefing a new hire, an agency, or an analyst and need one slide that shows the competitive landscape rather than a paragraph explaining it.
- Win/loss interviews or a message test suggest buyers cannot articulate how you differ from a specific rival; a map often exposes that the two of you are sitting in the same spot.
- You have already run Bowman's Strategic Clock and want to check the price-value picture against a second, buyer-specific pair of attributes.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company with a specialised PMM team, a competitive intelligence or product marketing manager typically builds and maintains the map, while the Head of Product Marketing owns interpreting the white-space finding and, jointly with the VP Product Marketing or CMO, deciding whether it changes external positioning. At solo or founding-PMM stage, the founding PMM owns the whole exercise alone, from choosing the axes through validating placement with buyers.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Choose the two axes from buyer evidence, not internal opinion.** Pull the attributes buyers actually mention unprompted in win/loss interviews, sales call notes, or review sites, then pick the two that are both highly variable across competitors and highly important to the buyer. An axis every competitor scores similarly on wastes half the map; an axis buyers do not care about produces a chart that is visually tidy but strategically useless.
2. **Define each axis with a concrete, buyer-recognisable scale.** Write down what sits at each end in language a buyer would use, for example "fully self-serve" to "requires a dedicated implementation team," rather than an internal engineering measure nobody outside the company would recognise.
3. **List the competitive set.** Include direct competitors, the closest adjacent-category products, and, where relevant, the status quo (manual process or spreadsheet) if it is a genuine alternative buyers weigh. Five to eight logos is usually the right range; more and the map becomes unreadable, fewer and it understates the field.
4. **Plot each competitor from evidence, not assumption.** Score every competitor, including yourself, against both axes using the same sources: analyst reports, review-site comparison data, competitor demos, and win/loss interviews where prospects describe how they perceived each option. Plotting your own product where you wish you sat, rather than where buyers actually place you, is the single most common way this exercise misleads a team.
5. **Identify white space.** Look for quadrants or regions with no competitor, or only weak competitors, and ask whether a meaningful number of buyers actually want what sits there. Empty space with no buyer demand is not an opportunity; it is just empty for a reason.
6. **Check the white space against your actual capability.** A gap in the map only matters if you can credibly move into it, or already sit there, within the timeframe your GTM plan assumes. An open quadrant that would take three product cycles to reach is a roadmap input, not a positioning claim you can make today.
7. **Validate with target buyers.** Show the map, unlabelled with your own name at first, to five to eight prospects or customers and ask them to place your product on it themselves. If their placement differs materially from yours, that gap between self-perception and buyer perception is the most important finding the exercise produces.
8. **Translate the finding into positioning and messaging.** A validated map is an input to Geoffrey Moore's positioning statement or April Dunford's canvas, not a replacement for either; use the axis language and white-space finding to sharpen the positioning those frameworks produce.
9. **Refresh on a fixed cadence.** Re-plot every two quarters, or immediately after a competitor's major repositioning, using the same axes and sources each time so movement is genuinely comparable rather than an artefact of a different scoring method.

**Example:** Fieldstack, a fictional construction-management SaaS company, was preparing a Series B pitch and needed one slide that explained its market position to investors who had already seen three competitors present. A feature-checklist comparison had not landed in early rehearsals; investors kept asking "so who do you actually take share from?" PMM pulled 18 win/loss interviews and found buyers consistently talked about two things when comparing options: how much configuration a new site required before it was usable ("out-of-the-box" to "needs a consultant"), and how deep the tool went on a single job site versus how broadly it covered a whole portfolio of sites ("single-site depth" to "portfolio breadth"). Plotting the five most-named competitors against these two axes showed three incumbents clustered tightly in the "needs a consultant, portfolio breadth" corner, chasing large enterprise buyers, while a cheaper competitor sat at "out-of-the-box, single-site" for small contractors. Fieldstack's own product, validated against 14 recently won accounts, sat in previously unoccupied space: out-of-the-box setup with single-site depth, aimed at mid-size general contractors running five to twenty active sites, a buyer too complex for the small-contractor tool and too price-sensitive to justify a consultant-led enterprise rollout. PMM tested the unlabelled map with six target prospects; five placed Fieldstack in the same quadrant PMM had, without being told where it belonged. The finding reframed the pitch from "cheaper enterprise alternative" to "the only option that does not force mid-size contractors to choose between simplicity and depth." Within one quarter of rebuilding the sales deck and investor narrative around this map, mid-market win rate against the three enterprise incumbents rose from 19% to 31%, and average sales cycle shortened from 58 to 41 days, since the new pitch let reps skip the recurring "why not the enterprise tool" objection.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Choosing axes that flatter your own product.** Teams under pressure to show progress sometimes pick two attributes their product already wins on, producing a map that looks reassuring internally but means nothing to a buyer who does not weigh those attributes at all. Recovery: source the axis choice from unprompted buyer language in win/loss interviews or review sites, and treat any axis the team proposes internally as a hypothesis to check against that evidence, not a starting assumption.
- **Plotting competitors from their marketing rather than buyer perception.** A competitor's own website will always claim to be fast, simple, and comprehensive at once; plotting from their homepage copy instead of how buyers actually describe them produces a map that is really just a collection of marketing claims arranged on a grid. Recovery: score every competitor, including yourself, from the same evidence source (win/loss interviews, review-site text, analyst comparisons), and flag any competitor you cannot score from real buyer evidence as unverified rather than guessing.
- **Treating an empty quadrant as automatically valuable.** Space with no competitor sometimes means genuine opportunity, and sometimes means no buyer wants what sits there; conflating the two leads teams to chase white space that turns out to be empty for a good reason. Recovery: before committing a positioning claim to an empty quadrant, validate demand directly with five to eight target buyers, and treat silence or confusion in that validation as a signal to keep looking, not a reason to force the claim through anyway.

**Sources:**
- Al Ries & Jack Trout, [*Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*](https://www.alries.com/positioning) (1981), which popularised perceptual mapping alongside its broader positioning theory.

**See also:** Bowman's Strategic Clock (the fixed price-and-perceived-value version of this exercise; run both when price-value and a second buyer attribute both matter to the strategy question); Product Differentiation Strategy Framework (turn a validated white-space finding into an operational differentiation commitment); Segmentation–Targeting–Positioning (STP) Framework (confirm the segment that actually cares about the axes chosen before building the map); April Dunford's 5-Component Positioning Canvas (translate the map's white-space finding into a full positioning statement with a named competitive alternative).

## Category Design

**Type:** Methodology.

**What it is:** Category design is a discipline for defining, developing, and dominating a new market category, rather than competing for share inside an existing one, developed by Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney in *Play Bigger* (2016). Its central claim is that in a crowded or commoditised market the largest returns go not to whoever wins the most share of an existing category, but to whoever legitimately creates and owns a new category that reframes the buyer's problem: buyers stop asking "which vendor in this category is cheapest" and start asking "who invented the thing that solves this problem I didn't have a name for." The methodology runs in three continuous phases: **Define** (name the "Legendary Problem", the big, urgent problem the market has not yet recognised or named, and the "Point of View", or POV, that reframes it, positioning your product as the origin of the new category), **Develop** (build the proof, content, and flagship customers that make the category credible rather than a marketing invention), and **Dominate** (sustain and defend the category over years as competitors and imitators try to ignore or co-opt the vocabulary). It differs sharply from the other tools in this category: Bowman's Strategic Clock, the Perceptual Map, and Product Differentiation Strategy all help a company find or defend the best position inside an existing category; category design questions whether the existing category is the right container at all, and if not, sets out to build a new one.

**When to use it:**
- Every deal is decided on price or a feature checklist, and win/loss interviews show the market cannot tell you apart from several close competitors, no matter how the Perceptual Map or Bowman's Clock is redrawn.
- Your product solves a problem meaningfully differently from how the existing category defines it, and forcing it into that category's comparison criteria consistently undersells what it does.
- Analysts, press, or investors keep asking "so who do you take share from?" and the honest answer sounds defensive, a sign the existing category is capping the ceiling regardless of how much share you win within it.
- Leadership is weighing a genuinely novel go-to-market bet, not a messaging refresh, and has the multi-year budget and executive sponsorship a real category claim requires.
- A well-funded new entrant is attempting to define a new category around a problem adjacent to yours, and doing nothing risks ceding that framing to them by default.

**How to run it:**
1. **Test whether category design is actually warranted before starting.** Most companies should not attempt this: it is a multi-year, executive-sponsored bet, not a campaign. The test is whether winning more share inside the existing category would still leave you with a capped ceiling; if out-executing competitors inside the current category would solve the problem, use Product Differentiation Strategy instead.
2. **Name the Legendary Problem from evidence, not internal narrative.** Source it from win/loss interviews, Voice of the Customer signal, and discovery calls: the big, urgent problem buyers describe unprompted that no existing category's vendors are built to solve, not a problem your team has decided the market should care about.
3. **Draft the Point of View.** Write what is broken about how the market currently frames the problem, why now (the trend that makes the old frame obsolete), and the new category name and definition, positioning your product as the category's point of origin rather than a challenger within an old one.
4. **Build proof before claiming the category publicly.** Produce category-defining content (a report, original research) that demonstrates the Legendary Problem is real and widely felt, not just asserted; a category claim with no evidence behind it reads as a marketing wrapper the moment anyone tests it.
5. **Align every internal function on the same vocabulary before it goes external.** Sales, support, and product should all describe the problem and the category the same way; a company that markets a new category while sales still pitches the old one confuses buyers rather than reframing anything.
6. **Seed the category through analysts and flagship customers, not advertising.** Brief analyst firms on the vocabulary ahead of their research cycles, and recruit two or three flagship customers willing to speak publicly about it, since a category becomes real to a buyer once a peer they trust uses the term, not when a vendor coins it.
7. **Measure unprompted category-term usage, not just brand awareness.** Track how often prospects, press, and analysts use your category name unprompted, in inbound enquiries, analyst reports, or a competitor's own marketing; this is the real signal the category is taking hold.
8. **Sustain the category for years, not one launch cycle.** Treat Dominate as an ongoing programme: keep evangelising, defend the term as imitators start using it, and refresh the POV as the market evolves; ownership of a category name lapses the moment a company stops actively reinforcing it.

**Cadence & ownership:** PMM or marketing leadership originates and maintains the Point of View in direct partnership with the founder or CEO; a category claim only carries credibility with visible executive sponsorship, not a marketing team acting alone. Treat this as a multi-year programme, reviewed at least annually and whenever a new entrant attempts to co-opt the vocabulary, not a single campaign with a launch date and an end date. Analyst relations and content or PR functions execute the ongoing seeding cadence; the Quarterly PMM Planning Framework is the natural mechanism for tracking unprompted term-usage share each quarter and deciding whether the POV needs refreshing.

**Example:** Fictional workplace-safety compliance company Ridgeline had competed for three years inside the crowded "EHS (environment, health, and safety) software" category, where a dozen near-identical vendors sold checklist and incident-tracking tools and every deal came down to price; win rate held flat near 22% and average deal size had not moved in two years. Win/loss interviews found the real unmet problem was not "we need EHS software" but that safety leaders were held accountable for incidents no existing software gave any warning of; Ridgeline's product used sensor and behavioural data to flag risk before an incident occurred, a capability the EHS category's own vocabulary had no room to describe. Rather than continuing to compete as a better EHS tool, PMM and the CEO built a POV naming the Legendary Problem as reactive safety management leaving companies blind to preventable incidents, and defined a new category, "Predictive Safety," with Ridgeline positioned as its origin. They published a "State of Predictive Safety" research report surveying 200 safety leaders, 68% of whom described a preventable incident their existing software gave no advance warning of; briefed two industry analyst firms on the vocabulary ahead of their annual research cycle; and secured three flagship customers, a logistics operator, a manufacturing plant, and a construction firm, willing to speak publicly about switching from reactive to predictive safety. Within a year, unprompted use of "predictive safety" in inbound demo requests rose from zero to 34% of qualified pipeline, average deal size rose 41% as procurement stopped benchmarking Ridgeline against checklist-tool pricing, and win rate in deals where the prospect used the term unprompted reached 61%, more than double the 24% rate still recorded in deals framed as generic EHS software.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Declaring a category with no Legendary Problem behind it.** A category name invented from internal ambition rather than evidenced buyer pain reads as a marketing wrapper the first time a prospect or analyst tests it, and reps quietly revert to the old, familiar language because buyers do not recognise the new term. Recovery: revalidate the Legendary Problem against fresh win/loss and Voice of the Customer evidence; if buyers do not recognise the problem unprompted, the claim is premature and the budget should return to Product Differentiation Strategy instead.
- **Fully resourcing Define and Develop, then abandoning Dominate.** Companies often invest heavily in the initial launch, then stop, and a well-resourced competitor either ignores the new term or co-opts it without doing the underlying work. Recovery: budget category design as a standing multi-year programme with a named owner and a fixed cadence, and track unprompted term-usage share as an ongoing metric, not a launch-week result.
- **Confusing category design with a rebrand.** Swapping a homepage headline to a new category name without the Legendary Problem evidence and a flagship customer reference fools no one, and can damage credibility when a sceptical buyer tests the claim and finds nothing behind it. Recovery: gate any external category claim on the evidence base, the published POV, and at least one flagship reference being ready before the term appears in copy.

**Sources:**
- Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, & Kevin Maney, [*Play Bigger: How Pirates, Dreamers, and Innovators Create and Dominate Markets*](https://playbigger.com) (HarperBusiness, 2016), the book that named category design and its Define, Develop, Dominate phases.

**See also:** STP Framework (confirms the segment a new category claim is validated against); Bowman's Strategic Clock (use first, to confirm the existing category's ceiling is genuinely capped before attempting this); Product Differentiation Strategy Framework (the lower-risk alternative when out-competing inside the existing category would be enough); Message Architecture (Messaging House) (turns a validated Point of View into the copy hierarchy every category-defining asset writes from).

**Quiz:**

1. A Head of Product Marketing wants to pursue category design because a competitor recently coined a catchy new term for its market. Win-loss interviews, however, show the company already wins the majority of head-to-head deals inside its existing category. What should the PMM recommend?
   - **A.** Test whether winning more share inside the existing category would still leave a capped ceiling; since deals are already being won, Product Differentiation Strategy is the more appropriate tool here, not category design.
   - **B.** Proceed with category design immediately, since reacting quickly to a competitor's new vocabulary is essential to avoid losing ground.
   - **C.** Ignore the competitor's new term entirely and make no changes to strategy or messaging.
   - **D.** Ask engineering to build a genuinely new product category before making any positioning decisions.

   **Correct answer: A.** Category design is a multi-year, executive-sponsored bet reserved for situations where out-executing competitors inside the current category would still leave a capped ceiling. A company already winning most head-to-head deals has a differentiation opportunity, not evidence the whole category needs reframing.

   *Why not B:* Reacting to a competitor's vocabulary alone, without evidence that the existing category is genuinely capping outcomes, risks committing years of budget and executive attention to a bet the underlying data does not support.

   *Why not C:* Ignoring the move entirely skips the diagnostic step; the right response is to check the evidence first, not to default to inaction any more than to overreaction.

   *Why not D:* Category design does not require a new product first; it requires a genuine reframing of an already-real problem. Building new product features on the assumption a new category is warranted skips the evidence-gathering step entirely.

2. A PMM is drafting a Point of View for a proposed new category and writes the "Legendary Problem" based on what the leadership team believes the market should care about, without checking it against recent win-loss interviews. What risk does this create?
   - **A.** The category claim will likely read as a marketing wrapper the first time a prospect or analyst tests it, since the problem was not sourced from evidenced buyer pain.
   - **B.** No risk, since executive conviction is the primary requirement for a credible category claim.
   - **C.** The main risk is that the category name will be too difficult for sales to pronounce in a live pitch.
   - **D.** The Point of View will be too narrow in scope to support a multi-year programme.

   **Correct answer: A.** The Legendary Problem must come from evidence: win-loss interviews, Voice of the Customer signal, and discovery calls, not from what the team decides buyers should care about. A category invented from internal ambition rather than evidenced pain collapses the first time a sceptical buyer or analyst tests it against their own experience.

   *Why not B:* Executive sponsorship is necessary for credibility and follow-through, but it does not substitute for evidence that buyers actually experience the named problem; a category with strong internal conviction and no buyer evidence still fails externally.

   *Why not C:* Pronounceability is a minor packaging concern; the structural risk here is that the entire premise of the category rests on an unvalidated assumption about buyer pain.

   *Why not D:* Scope is not the issue described; the issue is the source of the problem statement, evidence versus internal belief, not how broad or narrow the resulting category definition is.

3. A company successfully launches a new category with strong analyst briefings and two flagship customer references, then redirects the team to other projects six months later. A competitor starts using the same category term without doing any of the underlying research. What went wrong?
   - **A.** The company fully resourced Define and Develop but abandoned Dominate, leaving the category vulnerable to a competitor co-opting the vocabulary without doing the work.
   - **B.** The flagship customers chosen were not credible enough to sustain the category on their own.
   - **C.** The analyst briefings should have been skipped in favour of paid advertising to build broader awareness faster.
   - **D.** The company should have patented the category name to prevent competitors from using it.

   **Correct answer: A.** Dominate is meant to run for years, not one launch cycle: sustained evangelism, defending the term as imitators appear, and refreshing the point of view as the market evolves. Stopping after a successful launch is exactly how a well-resourced competitor can adopt the vocabulary with none of the original research behind it.

   *Why not B:* The scenario describes a resourcing and sustainment failure after a successful launch, not a flaw in the original flagship customer selection, which appears to have worked well enough to launch successfully.

   *Why not C:* Advertising does not build the credibility that analyst relationships and flagship customer proof provide; the entry specifically favours those over advertising for seeding a new category. The failure here is stopping the work, not the choice of channel.

   *Why not D:* Category names describing a market problem are generally not defensible through a trademark or patent in the way a competitor's co-opting of common vocabulary would require; the real defence is sustained, ongoing evangelism, not a legal filing.

4. A team updates its homepage headline to use a newly invented category name, without publishing any supporting research or securing a flagship customer reference. What is the most likely outcome?
   - **A.** A sceptical buyer who tests the claim will find nothing behind it, since this is a rebrand dressed as category design rather than the genuine article.
   - **B.** The category claim will succeed regardless, since a strong headline is the most important factor in category adoption.
   - **C.** The claim will fail only if a competitor immediately contests the new term publicly.
   - **D.** This approach is a faster, equally valid alternative to the full Define, Develop, Dominate process.

   **Correct answer: A.** Swapping a headline to a new category name without the underlying evidence base, published Point of View, and at least one flagship reference is a named pitfall: it fools no one and can damage credibility once a sceptical buyer or analyst tests the claim and finds nothing substantive behind it.

   *Why not B:* A headline alone cannot manufacture the credibility a real category claim requires; without proof and flagship validation, the claim collapses under any real scrutiny regardless of how compelling the headline reads.

   *Why not C:* The claim is vulnerable the moment any buyer, analyst, or prospect tests it directly, not only if a competitor happens to contest it publicly; the absence of underlying substance is the core problem either way.

   *Why not D:* This is explicitly the pitfall the entry warns against, not a legitimate shortcut; skipping Develop's proof-building step is what separates a real category claim from an unsupported rebrand.

5. Twelve months into a category design programme, leadership asks how to measure whether it is actually working, beyond a subjective sense that the messaging feels stronger. What should the team track?
   - **A.** How often prospects, press, and analysts use the new category term unprompted, since that is the real signal the category is taking hold, not just internal brand-awareness metrics.
   - **B.** Total marketing spend allocated to the category launch campaign.
   - **C.** The number of internal employees who can recite the new Point of View from memory.
   - **D.** Website traffic to the page where the new category name first appeared.

   **Correct answer: A.** The methodology specifically calls for measuring unprompted category-term usage, in inbound enquiries, analyst reports, or even a competitor's own marketing, as the real evidence a category is taking hold, since a category becomes real to a buyer once a peer they trust uses the term unprompted.

   *Why not B:* Spend is an input measure, not an outcome measure; a large budget with no unprompted term usage to show for it does not indicate the category claim has actually landed with buyers.

   *Why not C:* Internal recall confirms the team has aligned on the vocabulary, which matters for consistent execution, but it says nothing about whether buyers, press, or analysts outside the company have adopted the term.

   *Why not D:* Traffic to a specific page reflects visits, not whether visitors internalised and started using the category language themselves; unprompted usage elsewhere is the stronger signal the methodology asks teams to track.

## CI Program Maturity Model

**Type:** Model.

**What it is:** A staged model for diagnosing how mature a company's competitive intelligence (CI) function actually is, the direct structural sibling of the Sales Enablement Maturity Model (Category 9) applied to the CI discipline rather than enablement. Klue, a competitive-enablement software vendor, published a five-stage version, the Competitive Enablement Maturity Model, developed with the Strategic and Competitive Intelligence Professionals association (SCIP) from its 2021 Competitive Enablement Report survey data: **Ad Hoc** (competitor knowledge lives in individual heads, no shared repository, no owner), **Adopting** (a named function exists and first attempts at battlecards and a shared repository appear, but intel is scattered across Slack, email, and shared drives), **Impacting** (structured processes and a dedicated CI tool are in place, with the first real cross-functional distribution to sales), **Influencing** (CI findings demonstrably shape roadmap, positioning, and go-to-market decisions, not just sales collateral), and **Transforming** (CI is embedded across the company as a continuously measured system with executive visibility). A separate academic systematic review (see Sources) surveyed fourteen distinct published CI maturity models and found consistent convergence around the same underlying dimensions, evidence this is a genuinely converged practice rather than one vendor's invention. This entry adapts that research tradition into a distinct model for this knowledge base rather than reproducing Klue's stage names verbatim, in the same spirit as this knowledge base's existing Forrester Customer Advocacy Model.

**When to use it:**
- **You are inheriting a CI function, or building one from scratch, with no clear picture of what exists.** A fast, honest diagnosis beats guessing from whatever battlecard was most recently updated.
- **Sales reports that competitive intel is stale, hard to find, or contradicts what they hear live in deals.** This is almost always a symptom of a function stuck at Ad Hoc or Adopting, not a research-quality problem alone.
- **Leadership asks why the company isn't in a specific analyst report, or seems consistently surprised by a competitor's move.** Both are classic Ad Hoc-to-Adopting symptoms: no systematic monitoring, no distribution loop.
- **You have built strong point-in-time artefacts (a battlecard, a perceptual map) but they decay within a quarter.** As with the Sales Enablement Maturity Model, that decay usually points to a stage gap in ownership or tooling, not an asset-quality gap.
- **You need a business case for CI headcount or a dedicated competitive-intelligence platform.** A scored stage and a named target give leadership a concrete "what this actually costs to fix" ask instead of an unscored list of requests.

**Ownership:** At a scaled company with a dedicated Competitive Intelligence Manager or a Head of Competitive Intelligence, that role owns the scoring exercise and the target-stage decision, with the Head of Product Marketing signing off since CI findings feed directly into positioning and battlecards. At a solo or founding-PMM stage, competitive intelligence usually has no dedicated owner at all, and the founding PMM runs the scoring, the interviews, and the target-setting alone, since there is no separate CI role to hand any of it to.

**How to read it:** Read the five stages as a ladder across four dimensions that must each clear a bar before a team can honestly claim the next stage. **Repository and process**: Ad Hoc has no shared location, Adopting has a first shared drive or Slack channel with no structure, Impacting has a dedicated CI tool with a defined intake process, Influencing has that process feeding a documented cadence of deliverables, Transforming has the process embedded in company-wide planning rituals. **Distribution to sales**: Ad Hoc is nothing beyond word of mouth, Adopting is a first static battlecard or deck, Impacting is regular, versioned battlecards distributed through a tool reps actually use, Influencing ties distribution to measured usage, Transforming ties usage to deal outcomes. **Cross-functional reach**: Ad Hoc is CI talking to itself, Adopting reaches sales only, Impacting reaches sales and marketing, Influencing reaches product and executive planning, Transforming is a standing input to strategic decisions across the company. **Measurement**: Ad Hoc tracks nothing, Adopting tracks anecdote, Impacting tracks content usage, Influencing ties usage to win rate, Transforming reports CI's revenue impact as a standing metric. A function is rarely at the same stage across all four dimensions at once; the gap between them is usually the real finding.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Score each of the four dimensions honestly and separately**, resisting a single blended score; a function can be Impacting on repository and process while still Ad Hoc on cross-functional reach, and averaging hides exactly the gap worth fixing.
2. **Interview a sample of the sales reps and product managers who should be consuming CI output**, not just whoever runs the function. Ask what they actually use in a live deal or planning cycle, and what they have built themselves because nothing existed; consumer behaviour is the real signal, not the CI team's own inventory count.
3. **Identify the lowest-scoring dimension as the priority, not the lowest-scoring stage overall.** A function stuck at Ad Hoc on measurement while Impacting everywhere else should instrument next, not produce more battlecards that will only compound the same measurement gap.
4. **Set a realistic target, one stage forward on the priority dimension, for the next two quarters**, rather than "Transforming" as an immediate goal.
5. **Name an owner and a review cadence for the dimension being advanced**, since ownership and cadence are themselves stage-defining criteria; a plan with no named owner cannot progress past Ad Hoc on repository and process, however much research exists.
6. **Re-score quarterly against the same four dimensions**, tracking movement rather than activity; a quarter of heavy research output that does not move the distribution or measurement score has not actually advanced the function's stage.
7. **Use the resulting stage and specific dimension gaps as the business case for the next CI investment**, the same pattern the Sales Enablement Maturity Model uses: a scored gap and its cost to close is a stronger ask than an unscored tooling wishlist.

**Example:** Fictional data-infrastructure company Vantark had grown its CI effort from a single competitive slide in the company wiki into a Slack channel where three different people posted competitor screenshots ad hoc, with no shared repository and no consistent distribution to sales. Scoring the four dimensions, PMM found repository and process at Adopting (a Slack channel existed but nothing was structured or searchable), distribution at Ad Hoc (sales heard about competitor moves only when someone happened to mention them in a deal-review call), cross-functional reach at Ad Hoc (product had never seen a single CI finding), and measurement at Ad Hoc (no usage data of any kind). Rep interviews confirmed the gap: 9 of 11 reps said they had no reliable way to find current competitor information and often learned about a competitor's new feature from the prospect, mid-call. PMM prioritised distribution and repository over deeper research output: consolidating the Slack history into a structured, tagged competitor database, and building a lightweight quarterly battlecard refresh cycle distributed through the CRM. Within two quarters, both dimensions moved from Ad Hoc and Adopting to Impacting, and reps reported using the battlecards in 68% of competitive deals, up from an estimated 15% under the old ad hoc Slack system; win rate in deals against the company's top-two named competitors rose from 29% to 38% over the same period.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Scoring the function on its best deliverable rather than its typical one.** A single excellent battlecard for one competitor does not mean the whole function is Impacting if five other competitors have nothing current. Recovery: score based on coverage across the full competitive set that actually matters in live deals, not the strongest single artefact.
- **Investing in a CI tool before the repository and process stage is solid.** Buying a competitive-intelligence platform while intel is still scattered across Slack and personal notes produces an expensive tool populated with the same fragmented content it was meant to fix. Recovery: reach Adopting or Impacting on repository and process, a single structured location with a defined intake habit, before evaluating a dedicated platform.
- **Treating CI as sales-only and never advancing cross-functional reach.** A function that only ever produces sales battlecards plateaus at Impacting and never demonstrates the strategic value that justifies further investment. Recovery: deliberately route at least one CI finding per quarter into a product or planning conversation, and track whether it changed a decision, as the specific evidence needed to advance the cross-functional-reach dimension.

**Quiz:**

1. Sales reps at a company complain that competitive intel is stale, hard to find, and sometimes contradicts what they hear live in deals. Leadership assumes this means the CI team's research quality is poor. What does the model suggest is the more likely explanation?
   - **A.** The CI function is likely stuck at Ad Hoc or Adopting, a symptom of a stage gap in repository, process, or distribution, not a research-quality problem.
   - **B.** The company should immediately hire more competitive analysts to increase research output.
   - **C.** Sales reps are simply not reading the materials that already exist.
   - **D.** The company should invest in a new competitive intelligence tool as the first step.

   **Correct answer: A.** Stale, hard-to-find intel that contradicts live deal reality is a classic Ad Hoc or Adopting symptom: no shared repository, no structured distribution, not a sign the underlying research itself is weak. Diagnosing the actual stage first prevents throwing more research effort at a problem that is really about process and access.

   *Why not B:* Adding headcount to produce more research does not fix a distribution or repository gap; the same output would still be scattered and hard for reps to find in the moment they need it.

   *Why not C:* Blaming reps for not consuming material assumes the material is well-organised and easy to access, which contradicts the very complaint described; the model points to a structural gap, not a rep behaviour problem.

   *Why not D:* Buying a tool before diagnosing which of the four dimensions is actually weak risks repeating the pitfall of investing in a platform before the repository and process stage is solid enough to populate it usefully.

2. A CI lead scores their function as Impacting overall because the team recently produced an excellent, well-researched battlecard for the company's top competitor. Four other competitors that come up regularly in deals have no current materials at all. What is wrong with this self-assessment?
   - **A.** The function should be scored on its typical coverage across the full competitive set that matters in live deals, not on its single strongest artefact.
   - **B.** The score is accurate, since Impacting only requires one strong example to demonstrate the stage has been reached.
   - **C.** The team should immediately move to score itself as Transforming, since the top competitor's battlecard shows executive-level rigour.
   - **D.** The scoring exercise is invalid unless every one of the four dimensions is assessed by an external consultant.

   **Correct answer: A.** Scoring on the best deliverable rather than typical performance is a named pitfall: a single excellent battlecard does not mean the whole function is Impacting if several other competitors that come up regularly have nothing current. An honest score reflects coverage across the competitors that actually matter in deals.

   *Why not B:* One strong example is not sufficient evidence of a stage; the model calls for assessing typical, ongoing coverage, since deals against the four uncovered competitors are exactly where the gap will show up.

   *Why not C:* Transforming requires embedded, company-wide, continuously measured practice; a single strong battlecard for one competitor falls well short of that bar, regardless of its quality.

   *Why not D:* External validation is not a requirement of the model; the fix for this scenario is applying the scoring honestly against typical coverage, which the internal team can do itself.

3. A company scores Impacting on repository and process, and Ad Hoc on cross-functional reach: CI findings never reach product or leadership planning. Leadership wants to invest in a dedicated competitive-intelligence platform next. What should the PMM recommend?
   - **A.** Prioritise advancing cross-functional reach, since that is the lowest-scoring dimension, rather than a tool investment that would mainly reinforce an already-solid repository and process stage.
   - **B.** Proceed with the platform purchase, since a strong repository and process stage means the team is ready for more sophisticated tooling.
   - **C.** Wait until all four dimensions reach Transforming before making any further investment decisions.
   - **D.** Replace the CI function entirely, since a stage gap this large usually means the current approach cannot be salvaged.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model calls for identifying the lowest-scoring dimension, not the lowest-scoring stage overall, as the priority. A team already Impacting on repository and process should route at least one CI finding into product or planning conversations next, rather than compounding strength in an area that is not the actual gap.

   *Why not B:* Being solid on repository and process does not mean the function is ready for more tooling; the actual gap, cross-functional reach, is a distribution and relationship problem a platform purchase does not directly solve.

   *Why not C:* Waiting for every dimension to reach the top stage before acting ignores the model's guidance to advance one stage at a time on the specific priority dimension over the next two quarters.

   *Why not D:* A stage gap between dimensions is a normal, expected finding, not evidence the function needs replacing; the model exists precisely to diagnose and close gaps like this one.

4. A CI team has produced strong, well-distributed battlecards for a full year, but has never tracked whether any of that material actually influenced a deal outcome. What should they instrument next to advance the measurement dimension?
   - **A.** Tie battlecard usage data to win rate in the deals where that usage occurred, rather than continuing to track anecdote or usage counts alone.
   - **B.** Produce even more battlecards, since quantity of output is what the measurement dimension evaluates.
   - **C.** Survey the CI team itself on how effective they believe their own materials have been.
   - **D.** Discontinue battlecard production, since a year without measurement suggests the format itself has failed.

   **Correct answer: A.** The measurement dimension progresses from tracking nothing, to anecdote, to content usage, to tying usage to win rate, to reporting revenue impact as a standing metric. A team with strong distribution but no outcome data should connect usage to win rate next, the specific step that advances Impacting toward Influencing.

   *Why not B:* More output does not address the measurement gap at all; the dimension is about whether usage is tied to outcomes, not about the volume of material produced.

   *Why not C:* The CI team's own belief in its effectiveness is exactly the kind of anecdote the lowest measurement stages rely on; the model calls for objective usage-to-outcome data instead.

   *Why not D:* Strong distribution is a genuine achievement worth keeping; the fix is adding measurement on top of it, not abandoning a format that reps are already using effectively.

5. A newly hired Head of Competitive Intelligence wants to justify budget for a larger team by presenting leadership with an unscored list of ten things the function needs. What would the model suggest as a stronger approach?
   - **A.** Score the function's current stage and specific dimension gaps first, then present the target stage and its cost to close as the business case, rather than an unscored wishlist.
   - **B.** Present the wishlist as written, since leadership responds better to a long list of tactical asks than to a staged framework.
   - **C.** Skip the business case entirely and simply start hiring, since the need for more CI investment is self-evident.
   - **D.** Ask each individual stakeholder to independently rank the ten items by personal preference before presenting anything to leadership.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly notes that a scored stage and named dimension gaps make a stronger business case than an unscored tooling wishlist, the same pattern used by the Sales Enablement Maturity Model. A concrete "this is the stage, this is the gap, this is what closing it costs" argument is easier for leadership to act on than an open-ended list.

   *Why not B:* An unscored wishlist gives leadership no way to judge priority or cost against a clear current-state diagnosis, which is exactly the weaker approach the model advises against.

   *Why not C:* Skipping the business case risks investment being cut or questioned later without a clear, structured justification; a scored diagnosis protects the investment once it is approved.

   *Why not D:* Individual stakeholder rankings do not replace a structured, dimension-based diagnosis; they risk producing a list driven by whoever is loudest rather than the function's actual, evidenced priority gap.
