# Quiz: Account-Based Everything (ABX) / TEAM Framework

1. A team renames its existing broad demand-generation campaign "account-based marketing" under pressure to show ABX results quickly, without tiering any accounts or building any committee-specific content. What does the methodology predict?
   - **A.** Audit the programme against the tier list and role-specific content it should have produced.
   - **B.** Add named-account targeting to the campaign, without building committee-specific content.
   - **C.** This is an acceptable shortcut as long as sales is informed of the rename.
   - **D.** The rename works fine as long as the account list stays under 50 accounts.

   **Correct answer: A.** Relabelling existing broad demand-generation campaigns as account-based marketing without actually tiering accounts or building committee-specific content is a named pitfall: teams see no improvement because nothing about the underlying motion changed. The fix is auditing any such programme against the explicit tier list and role-specific content it should have produced.

   *Why not B:* This does the Target half of the work without the Engage half: naming accounts still leaves every stakeholder getting the same generic pitch, so late-stage deals still stall the way they did before the rename.

   *Why not C:* Informing sales of a rename does not address the substantive gap, missing tiering and committee-specific content, that the methodology identifies as the actual cause of no improvement.

   *Why not D:* The pitfall applies regardless of account list size; the core issue is the absence of real tiering and content work, not the number of accounts on the list.

2. A team builds fully bespoke, one-to-one content and outreach for all 400 accounts on its target list, treating every account like a Tier 1 account regardless of its actual tier. What does the methodology warn about this approach?
   - **A.** Enforce tier-appropriate customisation across the full 400-account list.
   - **B.** Keep Tier 2 and Tier 3 fully bespoke too, cutting only Tier 1's extra customisation.
   - **C.** Tiering only matters for reporting, not for how much content or effort is actually built.
   - **D.** Abandon tiering entirely and treat every account identically going forward.

   **Correct answer: A.** Building Tier 1-level bespoke plays for every account regardless of tier is a named pitfall: it is not sustainable past a handful of accounts and burns out the team while diluting the genuine advantage of full customisation for accounts that actually deserve it. The fix is enforcing tier-appropriate customisation and auditing content production time by tier each quarter.

   *Why not B:* This trims the wrong end: Tier 2 and Tier 3 are where the volume sits, so leaving both fully bespoke still burns most of the team's capacity that tier-appropriate customisation is meant to free up.

   *Why not C:* Tiering directly determines how much bespoke content and effort an account receives, per the methodology's own Tier 1/2/3 definitions; it is not merely a reporting label.

   *Why not D:* Abandoning tiering removes the very mechanism that allocates effort proportionally; the fix is enforcing the tiering discipline more strictly, not eliminating it.

3. A company's account-based programme is measured on MQL volume, the same dashboard used for its broad-funnel motions. The dashboard makes the programme look like it's underperforming even though a small number of named accounts are progressing well through their buying committees. What does the methodology recommend?
   - **A.** Switch to account-level metrics before the programme launches.
   - **B.** Switch to account-level metrics, but only after the first full year has run.
   - **C.** Increase MQL volume targets for the account-based programme to compensate.
   - **D.** Continue using MQL volume, since it is the industry-standard metric.

   **Correct answer: A.** Measuring the programme with lead-volume metrics that do not map to an account-based motion is a named pitfall: a programme succeeding at the account level can look like it's failing on an MQL-volume dashboard, leading leadership to defund it prematurely. The fix is switching reporting to account-level metrics before the programme launches.

   *Why not B:* Waiting a full year still leaves the programme measured on the wrong metric for months, exactly the window in which leadership may defund it prematurely; the fix belongs before launch, not after a confusing first year.

   *Why not C:* Raising MQL targets does not fix the underlying measurement mismatch; a small, named account universe was never going to generate high MQL volume regardless of how well it's actually performing.

   *Why not D:* MQL volume is explicitly named as the wrong metric for this specific motion; the methodology calls for account-level metrics instead, regardless of how standard MQL volume is elsewhere.

4. A Tier 1 account has gone completely cold for two consecutive quarters, showing no engagement at all, while a Tier 3 account has suddenly started showing strong buying-committee engagement across multiple roles. What does the methodology recommend?
   - **A.** Treat both situations as triggers to re-tier immediately, not at the scheduled review.
   - **B.** Re-tier the cold account now, but wait for the scheduled review on the surging one.
   - **C.** Remove the cold Tier 1 account from the target list permanently.
   - **D.** Promote the Tier 3 account to Tier 1 without further validation.

   **Correct answer: A.** The methodology explicitly names a Tier 1 account going cold for two consecutive quarters, or a Tier 3 account suddenly showing strong intent, as explicit triggers to re-tier immediately rather than waiting for the scheduled quarterly review.

   *Why not B:* This acts on only one named trigger: the methodology treats a Tier 3 account's sudden strong intent as just as explicit a re-tiering trigger as a cold Tier 1 account, not one to leave for the standard review.

   *Why not C:* The methodology calls for re-tiering, which could include demoting or recycling, not permanent removal with no possibility of future re-engagement.

   *Why not D:* While the methodology does call for promoting accounts showing strong intent, it frames this as part of a re-tiering process based on the observed signal, not an instruction to skip further validation of that signal's strength or persistence.

5. Sales reports that late-stage deals keep stalling or being lost when a stakeholder nobody had engaged suddenly raises an objection during procurement. What does the methodology say caused this, and what is the fix?
   - **A.** Map the buying committee and engage it with role-specific content from the start.
   - **B.** Map the buying committee only once procurement raises the objection, then follow up.
   - **C.** Fix this by increasing outbound volume to reach more contacts across all accounts.
   - **D.** This deal size was too large; a self-serve motion should have been used instead.

   **Correct answer: A.** This is exactly the methodology's named trigger: deals currently won or lost on reaching a single contact, with no coordinated plan for the rest of the buying committee, and late-stage stalls when an unengaged stakeholder raises an objection. The fix is mapping the buying committee for each target account and engaging it with role-specific content from the start, not waiting until procurement.

   *Why not B:* Mapping the committee is the right work done at the wrong point: by the time procurement has already raised the objection, the deal has stalled, and reactive follow-up cannot recover the trust proactive, early engagement was meant to build.

   *Why not C:* More generic outbound volume does not address the core issue, missing role-specific engagement with the right stakeholders; the fix is targeted, role-specific committee engagement, not raw volume.

   *Why not D:* Large, committee-driven deals are exactly the scenario this methodology is built for; the pattern described indicates a missing account-based practice, not evidence the deal size is unsuited to it.
