# Quiz: 7-Step Product Marketing Framework

1. A growth-stage company hands Steps 3 through 7 to five different people who never coordinate with each other. Within weeks, messaging drifts between assets and work gets duplicated. What does the framework recommend?
   - **A.** Assign one owner for the whole sequence, requiring sign-off before any downstream asset ships.
   - **B.** Assign one owner for Steps 3 and 4 only, leaving demand gen and advocacy uncoordinated.
   - **C.** Consolidate all seven steps into a single person's job description immediately.
   - **D.** This is expected; distributing steps across specialists is exactly what is intended.

   **Correct answer: A.** Treating each step as a separate project rather than an interconnected cycle is a named pitfall: when steps are handed to people who never talk, work duplicates and messaging drifts within weeks. The recovery is one accountable owner for the whole sequence, with sign-off on positioning required before downstream assets ship.

   *Why not B:* This is the right fix at too narrow a scope: owning only content and enablement still leaves demand generation, alignment, and advocacy to drift independently, so the same duplication and messaging gaps persist further down the sequence.

   *Why not C:* The framework explicitly supports distributed execution across specialists; the fix is single ownership and accountability for the sequence, not consolidating every step into one role.

   *Why not D:* Distributing execution is fine; the named pitfall is the lack of a single accountable owner and the missing sign-off gate, not the distribution itself.

2. A team starts writing blog posts, case studies, and comparison pages while positioning is still being tested with prospects, reasoning that content production shouldn't wait. Weeks later, real sales conversations reveal the positioning needs to change, and most of the content has to be rewritten. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Freeze content production until at least five live sales conversations have used the new statement.
   - **B.** Pause new content until the positioning statement is drafted, then resume before sales test it.
   - **C.** Consolidate the content and enablement functions into a single combined role.
   - **D.** Treat this as unavoidable; content and positioning must be built at the same time.

   **Correct answer: A.** Over-investing in content (Step 3) before sales validates positioning (Step 2) is a named pitfall: writing collateral while positioning is still being tested means most of it needs rewriting once real objections surface. The recovery is freezing content production until at least five sales conversations have used the new positioning live and reps confirm it lands.

   *Why not B:* This stops one step too early: resuming content once a statement is merely drafted, rather than once sales has actually tested it live, still risks the same rewrite if the drafted statement turns out to be wrong.

   *Why not C:* Combining the two functions into one role does not address the sequencing failure, writing before validation, that actually caused the rework; the fix is a gate between steps, not a reorganisation of who holds them.

   *Why not D:* The framework explicitly sequences content behind positioning validation as a deliberate discipline; simultaneous development is named as the pitfall to avoid, not an unavoidable necessity.

3. A weekly sales-marketing alignment sync has become a read-out of pipeline numbers with no decisions made, week after week. What does the framework recommend?
   - **A.** End every sync with one concrete change to messaging or collateral, with an owner and a date.
   - **B.** Circulate a written recap of the pipeline numbers discussed after each sync.
   - **C.** Reduce the sync to a monthly cadence, since weekly is too frequent to produce decisions.
   - **D.** Replace the sync with an emailed report, removing live discussion entirely.

   **Correct answer: A.** Letting Step 6 alignment meetings become status updates rather than working sessions is a named pitfall: if the sync turns into a numbers read-out with no decisions, the feedback loop meant to refine positioning stalls. The recovery is ending every sync with one concrete, owned, dated change.

   *Why not B:* A recap still just documents the numbers rather than forcing a decision from them; it addresses the visibility of the read-out, not the missing decision the pitfall's recovery specifically requires.

   *Why not C:* The framework specifies a weekly cadence for this sync; the problem described is about content (no decisions made), not frequency, so reducing cadence does not address the actual issue.

   *Why not D:* Removing live discussion loses the collaborative decision-making the sync is meant to produce; the fix is structuring the existing live sync to end in a decision, not replacing it with a passive report.

4. A team runs Step 1 market research and Step 2 positioning once, at the company's founding, and has never revisited either step despite two consecutive quarters of declining win rate. What does the framework's cadence guidance say should happen?
   - **A.** Re-run Steps 1 and 2 from scratch once two consecutive quarters show declining win rate.
   - **B.** Patch the sales collateral and objection handling first, then revisit segmentation later.
   - **C.** Wait for four consecutive quarters of decline before considering any refresh.
   - **D.** Move directly to Step 7 advocacy work, since this is primarily a retention issue.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's cadence guidance is explicit: treat a new segment, a stalled conversion rate, or two consecutive quarters of declining win rate as the trigger to re-run Steps 1 and 2 from scratch rather than patching messaging in isolation.

   *Why not B:* This reverses the required order: patching downstream collateral before checking whether the underlying research and positioning still hold is exactly the isolated patching the framework's cadence guidance warns against.

   *Why not C:* The framework names two consecutive quarters, not four, as the explicit trigger; waiting longer delays a research refresh the data already calls for.

   *Why not D:* Declining win rate is treated as a positioning-and-research trigger under this framework, not automatically an advocacy or retention issue; Step 7 does not address the root cause the win-rate decline points to.

5. A company builds sales collateral (Step 4) without involving any front-line sales reps in reviewing the drafts. Reps subsequently ignore the material entirely. What does the framework say about this outcome?
   - **A.** Involve at least two front-line sales reps in reviewing drafts before publishing.
   - **B.** Share the finished collateral with sales for feedback after it is published.
   - **C.** Have sales leadership build the collateral entirely instead of PMM.
   - **D.** Treat this as unrelated; adoption depends only on the collateral's design quality.

   **Correct answer: A.** Step 4 explicitly names this pattern: collateral written without sales input is regularly ignored by the sales team, however good it looks. The fix is involving at least two front-line sales reps in reviewing drafts before publishing.

   *Why not B:* Gathering feedback only after publishing is the right instinct too late: reps have already formed the impression that the material was built without them, and revisions after the fact do not recover the trust the pre-publish review is meant to build.

   *Why not C:* PMM retains ownership of Step 4 under the framework's cadence guidance; the fix is involving reps in review, not transferring collateral ownership entirely away from PMM.

   *Why not D:* The framework explicitly ties adoption to rep involvement in the review process, not purely to design quality; even well-designed collateral gets ignored if reps had no input into it.
