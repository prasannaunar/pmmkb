# Quiz: T2D3 Framework (B2B SaaS)

1. A company misses its Year 3 T2D3 target by 30% for two consecutive quarters. Leadership wants to keep pushing the team harder on the exact same plan to hit the original multiple. What does the framework recommend instead?
   - **A.** Revisit the underlying assumptions: market size, sales capacity, and product readiness.
   - **B.** Push harder for one more quarter on the same plan before revisiting anything.
   - **C.** Abandon T2D3 entirely and adopt a completely different growth framework.
   - **D.** Reduce the Year 3 target retroactively without investigating why it was missed.

   **Correct answer: A.** Rigidly adhering to T2D3 targets without market feedback is a named pitfall: treating the multiples as a mandate rather than a planning guide burns out the team when the market cannot support that year's target. The recovery is using the framework as a guide, and revisiting the underlying assumptions when two consecutive quarters miss by more than 25%.

   *Why not B:* This delays the required response by another quarter: the two-consecutive-quarter, 25% threshold has already been crossed, so the framework calls for revisiting assumptions now, not after one more cycle on the same unchanged plan.

   *Why not C:* Abandoning the framework entirely skips the more measured response the framework itself prescribes, revisiting assumptions, which may still allow the underlying planning approach to remain useful.

   *Why not D:* Adjusting the target without investigating why it was missed skips the diagnostic step the framework calls for; the fix is examining market size, sales capacity, and product readiness first.

2. A company in Year 3 tries to hire a VP of Marketing, launch a new product line, and enter a new region all within the same quarter, since the "required" list for Year 3 and 4 has grown longer. What does the framework warn about this approach?
   - **A.** Sequence conservatively, testing at most one major new variable per quarter.
   - **B.** Sequence two of the three initiatives together this quarter, deferring only the third.
   - **C.** This is fine as long as the CEO personally approves each initiative individually.
   - **D.** The framework does not address sequencing; it only concerns revenue multiples.

   **Correct answer: A.** Trying to execute all five milestones simultaneously is a named pitfall, particularly acute in Years 3 and 4 when the required list grows longer. The recovery is sequencing conservatively: validate one segment before expanding to two, scale sales capacity before expanding the product line, so any given quarter has at most one major new variable being tested.

   *Why not B:* Running two of the three at once still tests two major variables in the same quarter; the framework's own guidance caps it at one, so this only halves the risk rather than removing it.

   *Why not C:* CEO approval of each initiative individually does not address the underlying sequencing risk; the issue is testing too many variables in the same quarter, regardless of who signed off on each one.

   *Why not D:* The framework's own pitfalls section explicitly addresses sequencing and pacing of the required operational milestones, not just the revenue multiples themselves.

3. A company applies the same playbook used to triple revenue from $500K to $1.5M when trying to triple again from $9M to $27M, assuming the same tactics will simply scale up. What does the framework say about this assumption?
   - **A.** Treat the required operational milestones, not just the multiple, as the real checklist.
   - **B.** Adjust only the hiring plan for the larger base, keeping the rest of the playbook the same.
   - **C.** This only matters for public companies; private ones can reuse the same playbook.
   - **D.** The framework does not distinguish operational requirements between different years.

   **Correct answer: A.** Benchmarking against T2D3 multiples without adjusting for starting ARR is a named pitfall: tripling from $500K is operationally very different from tripling from $5M or more. The recovery is treating the required operational milestones, not just the multiple, as the real checklist for the year.

   *Why not B:* Hiring is only one line of the required checklist; leaving marketing focus, sales focus, and specialisation unchanged still applies an early-stage playbook to a later-stage problem it was never built for.

   *Why not C:* The framework does not restrict this guidance to public companies; it applies generally to B2B SaaS companies scaling through these revenue bands, private or public.

   *Why not D:* The framework's own year-by-year breakdown explicitly names different required hires, marketing focus, and sales focus for each year; it very much distinguishes operational requirements by stage.

4. A company's Year 3 net revenue retention comes in well below 100%, even though new logo bookings are strong. What does the framework suggest this signals about the company's ability to hit later-year targets?
   - **A.** Investigate the weak NRR alongside ARR growth before trusting the trajectory.
   - **B.** Treat new logo bookings alone as sufficient evidence, since ARR is the headline metric.
   - **C.** Treat a below-100% NRR as immediate grounds to discard the framework entirely.
   - **D.** Weak NRR only matters in Year 5; it is not a meaningful signal in earlier years.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework names net revenue retention as a supporting metric to track alongside ARR, noting that best-in-class NRR (110%+) is treated as a reasonable marker since new logo growth alone rarely sustains tripling; a below-100% NRR is worth investigating even if the specific benchmark should be validated against the company's own segment.

   *Why not B:* This relies on the wrong evidence: strong new-logo bookings alone say nothing about whether existing customers are retained, and the framework specifically names NRR as the check that catches a retention problem bookings volume would hide.

   *Why not C:* A weak NRR signal calls for investigation and course-correction, in line with the framework's broader "revisit assumptions" guidance, not outright abandonment of the framework based on one metric.

   *Why not D:* The framework treats NRR as a relevant supporting metric across the growth trajectory, with 110%+ specifically flagged as a reasonable Year 3 marker; it is not framed as only mattering in Year 5.

5. A CEO wants to set the company's growth narrative around T2D3 for board reporting, and asks PMM to also independently decide the actual headcount and hiring plan needed to hit each year's target. What does the framework say about this division of responsibility?
   - **A.** The CEO and board own the target; PMM Team Scaling Framework governs hiring decisions.
   - **B.** PMM should own the growth narrative, while the CEO decides the hiring plan directly.
   - **C.** Hiring decisions sit entirely outside any framework, made case by case informally.
   - **D.** The board should make all hiring decisions itself, bypassing PMM and leadership.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's cadence and ownership guidance is explicit: the CEO and board own the T2D3 target as the company's growth narrative, while the PMM Team Scaling Framework, not T2D3 itself, governs the actual hiring decisions each year's required milestones imply; T2D3 only names when a hire becomes necessary.

   *Why not B:* This swaps the actual division: the framework has the CEO and board owning the growth narrative, with PMM Team Scaling, not the CEO directly, working out the resulting hiring plan.

   *Why not C:* Hiring decisions are explicitly governed by a named framework (PMM Team Scaling), not left to informal, case-by-case judgment outside any structured process.

   *Why not D:* The board owns the overall growth target as a narrative, not the operational hiring decisions themselves, which the framework routes through PMM Team Scaling instead.
