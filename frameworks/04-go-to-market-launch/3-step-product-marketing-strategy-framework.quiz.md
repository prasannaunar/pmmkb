# Quiz: 3-Step Product Marketing Strategy Framework

1. A pre-Series A startup with a single founding PMM tries to serve "startups and enterprises" with one positioning statement, hoping to cover more of the market. What does the framework say about this approach?
   - **A.** Pick the single strongest segment and own it ruthlessly before expanding to a second.
   - **B.** Use segment-specific language in new campaigns only, keeping the old blended line elsewhere.
   - **C.** Run the 3-Step and 7-Step frameworks in parallel, one dedicated to each segment.
   - **D.** Leave the blended message in place; confusion only matters once ARR passes $2M.

   **Correct answer: A.** Trying to serve multiple segments with one message is a named pitfall: it spreads positioning thin and confuses both groups, since pain points, buying processes, and proof points usually differ by segment. The recovery is picking the single strongest segment and owning it ruthlessly before expanding to a second with its own statement.

   *Why not B:* This is the right instinct stopping short of the fix: splitting messaging for new campaigns while leaving the old blended statement live elsewhere still confuses whichever segment meets that older material, so the pitfall persists half-solved.

   *Why not C:* The 7-Step framework is the next framework once a team outgrows single-segment focus; running both frameworks in parallel at this early stage contradicts the 3-Step framework's own scope, which assumes one small team owning one motion.

   *Why not D:* The pitfall is named as a problem at exactly this early stage, not something that only matters later; blended messaging confuses buyers regardless of company size.

2. A team skips customer interviews, assuming they already know the core pain is "teams need better visibility," and builds messaging around that assumption. Conversion stays low, and no one can explain why. What does the framework say went wrong?
   - **A.** Conduct 10 to 15 customer interviews and rebuild messaging on the language customers actually used.
   - **B.** Switch to a different outbound channel, since the current one is clearly underperforming.
   - **C.** Shorten the existing positioning statement to a single, crisper sentence.
   - **D.** Accept the low conversion; a new product always needs six months to find its message.

   **Correct answer: A.** Skipping interviews and assuming you know the pain is a named pitfall: an assumed pain point, rather than the customer's actual words, produces messaging that does not resonate, and the team cannot diagnose why conversion is low because the message never gets close enough to the truth to fail informatively.

   *Why not B:* The framework's own diagnostic principle is that a stalled or flat metric is usually a positioning problem, not a channel problem; switching channels first skips the more likely root cause.

   *Why not C:* Statement length is a separate concern from whether the underlying pain point reflects real customer language; a short statement built on an assumed pain would have the same underlying problem.

   *Why not D:* The framework does not treat slow conversion as an expected, time-bound outcome; it explicitly ties conversion problems back to whether the positioning reflects real, evidenced customer pain.

3. A team declares its outbound channel a failure after only 15 prospects contacted over two weeks, and pivots to a different channel. What does the framework say about this decision?
   - **A.** Keep running the channel until at least 100 prospects have been contacted, then judge it.
   - **B.** Pivot to a second channel immediately, running both at once to compare results faster.
   - **C.** Extend the same two-week window by one more week before making any decision.
   - **D.** Judge the channel now; two weeks is enough time once messaging has been validated.

   **Correct answer: A.** Declaring a channel "done" too early is a named pitfall: judging a channel after two or three weeks, before enough volume has accumulated, means the team can't separate signal from noise. The recovery is setting a minimum sample size (100+ prospects for outbound, 4 to 6 weeks for inbound) before deciding to pivot.

   *Why not B:* The framework's own guidance (Step 3) is to master one channel before diversifying, since adding a second channel too early just doubles operational load without doubling learning; pivoting immediately contradicts this.

   *Why not C:* One extra week still falls well short of the 100-prospect threshold the framework names; the fix is a defined sample size, not a small, arbitrary extension of the same short window.

   *Why not D:* Whether messaging has been validated is a separate question from whether enough volume has accumulated; the framework ties the judgment specifically to sample size, not to messaging confidence.

4. A founding PMM notices the single leading metric (trial signups) has been flat for seven weeks, despite the team executing consistently on the same channel. What should they do first?
   - **A.** Revisit the problem and positioning steps before making any change to the channel.
   - **B.** Rewrite the positioning statement only, without re-running any customer interviews.
   - **C.** Switch away from the current channel and test two new ones at once.
   - **D.** Increase spending on the current channel without changing the message at all.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's cadence guidance is explicit: a leading metric flat for six to eight weeks is the trigger to revisit the problem and positioning steps, since a stalled metric is usually a positioning problem rather than a channel problem.

   *Why not B:* This is the right step taken too narrowly: rewriting the statement without first re-running interviews skips the evidence Step 1 is meant to supply, so the new statement risks being built on the same untested assumption as the old one.

   *Why not C:* Testing multiple new channels at once contradicts the framework's emphasis on mastering one channel before diversifying, and skips the more likely root cause the framework calls for checking first.

   *Why not D:* Spending more against an unresonating message amplifies the cost of a positioning problem without fixing it; the framework calls for revisiting positioning first, not scaling spend on the same unproven message.

5. A team wants to validate a draft positioning statement before rolling it out broadly. What does the framework specifically recommend?
   - **A.** Test the statement on five outside people, including a prospect or two.
   - **B.** Test the statement only with the company's existing, already-happy customers.
   - **C.** Present the statement only to internal team members for feedback.
   - **D.** Skip validation and rely on the initial customer interviews alone.

   **Correct answer: A.** The framework's Step 2 calls for testing the positioning statement on five people outside the company, ideally including a prospect or two, and revising until they can repeat the value back in their own words without prompting.

   *Why not B:* This is the right kind of test aimed at too narrow a group: existing happy customers already know why they bought, so their feedback cannot show whether a genuinely new prospect finds the statement clear.

   *Why not C:* Internal-only testing misses exactly the outside perspective the framework calls for; people close to the company are more likely to already understand the intended meaning regardless of how clearly it's actually written.

   *Why not D:* Step 1's interviews inform the positioning hypothesis; Step 2 requires a separate validation test on the drafted statement itself before it is considered ready.
