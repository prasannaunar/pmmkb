# Quiz: Working Backwards (PR/FAQ)

1. A CPO asks the PMM to "write a PR/FAQ" for a supplier portal. Six engineers are already assigned and the first sprint starts on Monday. What should the PMM do before drafting?
   - **A.** Confirm that reshaping or stopping the portal is still a possible outcome.
   - **B.** Draft it now and raise any doubts about the portal's scope at launch review.
   - **C.** Write the document as launch copy, working from the existing sprint plan.
   - **D.** Decline the request and run a pre-mortem on the portal plan in its place.

   **Correct answer: A.** A PR/FAQ is a test of whether an idea is worth building, and it only has value while the answer can still be "build something different" or "do not build". Agreeing that up front stops the document becoming a justification for a decision already made.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it does write the PR/FAQ, but it moves the challenge to the wrong point in the sequence. Doubts raised at launch review arrive after the build budget is spent, which is exactly what working backwards exists to prevent.

   *Why not C:* Writing from the sprint plan turns the method into launch copy for a staffed roadmap item. The press release should shape what gets built, not describe it after the fact.

   *Why not D:* A pre-mortem stress-tests a plan that is already settled; it does not fix the customer narrative or decide what to build. Declining also gives up the PMM's chance to shape the story while it can still change.

2. A first-draft press release is headed "Tallyroute launches its new platform", and its problem paragraph describes the company's goal of cutting support tickets by 30%. Reviewers say they cannot judge the idea. What should the PMM change?
   - **A.** Rewrite the problem from customer evidence for one named customer segment.
   - **B.** Rewrite the problem from what the sales leaders believe customers want most.
   - **C.** Expand the solution paragraph so reviewers can see the full set of features.
   - **D.** Move the ticket target into the internal FAQ and keep the current headline.

   **Correct answer: A.** The draft is written for the company rather than the customer, so reviewers have no customer problem to judge. Grounding the problem paragraph in interview or ticket evidence for one named segment gives them something real to test.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it turns the paragraph towards the customer, but on the wrong evidence. Internal opinion about what customers want is the gap the method is meant to expose; the problem paragraph needs the customer's own words.

   *Why not C:* More features make the document longer without answering who has the problem or why it matters. The solution paragraph can only be judged once the problem paragraph is right.

   *Why not D:* Moving the target tidies the internal metric away but leaves a headline and problem paragraph that still say nothing to a customer. The press release must stand on its own as the customer-facing story.

3. A PMM at a 400-person company has drafted the press release and external FAQ for a new reporting product. The internal FAQ, covering build cost, technical risk and dependencies, is still blank, and the first review is next week. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Ask the product manager and engineering lead to write the internal FAQ.
   - **B.** Write the internal FAQ from roadmap documents and send it for sign-off.
   - **C.** Drop the internal FAQ and submit the press release on its own for review.
   - **D.** Postpone the review until the product is built and the real costs are known.

   **Correct answer: A.** At this stage the PMM owns the press release and external FAQ, while product and engineering hold the knowledge the internal FAQ needs. Getting the right authors keeps the cost and feasibility answers credible for the decision-maker.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it fills the right section, but with the wrong owner. A PMM working from roadmap documents can only guess at build cost and technical risk, and reviewers will discount answers the accountable team did not write.

   *Why not C:* Leadership needs the internal FAQ to make the build decision; without it, the review can admire the story but cannot decide. The two halves of the document do different jobs.

   *Why not D:* Waiting until the product is built defeats the purpose of writing before building. The internal FAQ is meant to estimate cost and risk while the scope can still change.

4. In the first PR/FAQ review, the PMM presents slides walking through the press release and rebuts each objection as it comes up. The hour ends with few questions and no decision. What should change for the next review?
   - **A.** Read the document in silence first, then collect every reviewer's questions.
   - **B.** Read in silence at the start, then have the PMM answer each objection raised.
   - **C.** Cut the press release to three bullet points so the slides run more quickly.
   - **D.** Invite more senior leaders so a final decision can be reached in the room.

   **Correct answer: A.** Bryar and Carr describe the review as a chance for readers to understand the idea and draw out questions, not a pitch. Silent reading followed by open questioning surfaces the weak points the document has to address.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it adopts silent reading but keeps the pitching posture. A PMM who defends every objection hides the gaps the review exists to find; the questions belong in the FAQ, not in a rebuttal.

   *Why not C:* Shortening the document removes the detail reviewers need to find problems. The issue was how the meeting ran, not how long the press release was.

   *Why not D:* More senior people in a pitch meeting add pressure without adding understanding. A decision depends on reviewers having read and questioned the document, not on who attends.

5. Leadership must choose between three proposed product bets for next year. Each is described only as a feature list, no build work has started, and product, engineering and PMM each describe the bets differently. Which approach fits best?
   - **A.** Have each bet written up as a press release and FAQ for one named customer.
   - **B.** Map each bet's assumptions by importance and evidence, then test the riskiest.
   - **C.** Classify each bet with the Launch Tier Framework and compare their tiers.
   - **D.** Run a pre-mortem on each bet that imagines it has failed after launch.

   **Correct answer: A.** Feature lists, no build work and three conflicting pictures are the triggers for Working Backwards. Writing each bet in the same customer-facing format gives leadership one comparable document per bet before any headcount is committed.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: Assumptions Mapping also works before build, but it ranks the beliefs inside a plan that has already been stated. Here no shared statement of each bet exists yet; the PR/FAQ creates it, and its FAQ then feeds the map.

   *Why not C:* Tiering decides how much launch process an approved release gets, not which bet deserves to be built. It assumes the scope is already settled.

   *Why not D:* A pre-mortem stress-tests a settled plan for risks. It cannot compare three bets that are still described as feature lists with no agreed customer or problem.
