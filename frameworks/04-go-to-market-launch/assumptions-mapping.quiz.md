# Quiz: Assumptions Mapping

1. A PMM planning a move into a new segment has mapped 24 assumptions. The team has started by A/B testing button colours on the existing sign-up page, while "the new segment will pay our current price" sits important and unproven. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Redirect testing to the important assumptions that have the least evidence.
   - **B.** Test the most important assumptions first, starting with those already proven.
   - **C.** Finish the button test first, so the team builds an experimentation habit.
   - **D.** Test all 24 assumptions in parallel, so no single risk gets overlooked.

   **Correct answer: A.** The map exists to focus testing on the top-right corner: assumptions that would sink the plan if wrong and that have no observed evidence behind them. The pricing belief is exactly that; the button test moves nothing that matters.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it reads the importance axis correctly but ignores the evidence axis. An important assumption that already has proof needs monitoring, not a test; the scarce testing time belongs to the important ones with no evidence.

   *Why not C:* Bland names this pattern directly: teams run easy tests while the riskiest assumptions go untested. A habit built on unimportant tests delays the one result that could change the plan.

   *Why not D:* Testing everything spreads a small budget thinly and treats unimportant beliefs as equal to the ones that would sink the plan. Ranking by importance and evidence is the whole point of the grid.

2. A team runs a landing page smoke test for a new tier and gets a 3% sign-up rate. The head of product calls it a strong result; the sales lead calls it weak. Nobody set a target beforehand. What went wrong, and what should happen next?
   - **A.** No success criterion was set; fill one in on the Test Card before the next run.
   - **B.** No criterion was set; agree a fair threshold now and judge this result against it.
   - **C.** The sample was too small; run the same page longer until a clear winner emerges.
   - **D.** The wrong test was chosen; replace the smoke test with customer interviews.

   **Correct answer: A.** A Test Card fixes the metric and the threshold before the test runs, so the result is read against a criterion rather than against each person's hopes. Without one, any number can be argued either way.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it identifies the missing criterion, but setting it after seeing the result is still rationalising. A threshold agreed once the number is known will tend to drift towards whichever reading the room prefers.

   *Why not C:* Nothing suggests the sample was the problem; the dispute is about what counts as success. More traffic gives a more precise 3% and the same argument.

   *Why not D:* Interviews are weaker evidence than sign-ups, since they record what people say rather than what they do. Switching tests does not fix the absence of a pre-set criterion.

3. A single landing page smoke test for a new segment converts at 6%, well above its 4% criterion. The VP of marketing wants to commit the full £120,000 launch budget straight away. What should the PMM recommend?
   - **A.** Follow it with a stronger test, such as a pre-sale, before committing the budget.
   - **B.** Re-run the same smoke test with double the traffic to confirm the 6% result holds.
   - **C.** Commit the budget now, since the result cleared the criterion set in advance.
   - **D.** Commit half the budget now and hold the rest back until launch results arrive.

   **Correct answer: A.** A click shows interest, not willingness to pay, so one passing smoke test is weak evidence for a large spend. A pre-sale or letter of intent tests the same belief with what buyers actually commit to.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: repeating the test feels rigorous, but it gathers more of the same weak kind of evidence. A larger sample of clicks still says nothing about whether buyers will pay.

   *Why not C:* Clearing the criterion means the weak test passed, not that the assumption is proven. Treating one smoke test as proof is the pitfall the method warns against.

   *Why not D:* Splitting the budget reduces the loss if the plan fails but still spends on unproven evidence. A cheap stronger test answers the question before any launch money moves.

4. Six months before entering the dental clinic market, a PMM notices the launch plan rests on beliefs about the buyer, price and channel that nobody has checked. The plan is still open to change. Which approach fits best?
   - **A.** Map the beliefs by importance and evidence, then test the riskiest ones first.
   - **B.** Run a pre-mortem that imagines the entry has failed and lists the likely causes.
   - **C.** Message-test two candidate headlines with a verified panel of practice managers.
   - **D.** Classify the entry with the Launch Tier Framework to set its process and lead time.

   **Correct answer: A.** The situation is a set of untested beliefs and time to test them, which is what Assumptions Mapping is for. It ranks each belief by importance and evidence and turns the risky ones into designed tests.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: a pre-mortem also surfaces risk before launch, but by imagining failure rather than grading existing beliefs by evidence. It produces mitigations, not a ranked set of experiments that prove or disprove the buyer, price and channel beliefs.

   *Why not C:* Message testing is one experiment for one kind of assumption. Running it first skips the step that decides whether messaging is even the riskiest belief here.

   *Why not D:* Tiering decides how much process a launch gets, not which of its beliefs are true. It is a useful companion, but it leaves the untested assumptions untested.

5. A team drew its assumptions map at kick-off in January. By April, three tests have finished, a competitor has cut prices, and the team is still choosing experiments from the January map. What should the PMM change?
   - **A.** Re-plot the map after each Learning Card decision and at each launch checkpoint.
   - **B.** Re-plot the map once at the launch retrospective, when all results are available.
   - **C.** Add the competitor's price cut to the list but keep the January rankings as they are.
   - **D.** Stop mapping and switch to a pre-mortem now that the launch is getting close.

   **Correct answer: A.** Tests move assumptions to the evidence side and the market creates new ones, so a map that is never revisited keeps the team testing yesterday's risks. Re-plotting after each decision keeps the top-right corner current.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it does revisit the map, but on the wrong cadence. A map updated only after launch cannot steer the experiments that were meant to shape the launch.

   *Why not C:* Adding the new risk without re-ranking leaves finished tests in the unproven corner and the new belief unplaced. The grid only guides testing when every position reflects current evidence.

   *Why not D:* A pre-mortem is a useful late check, but it does not replace evidence-based testing. The fix is to keep the map current, not to abandon it.
