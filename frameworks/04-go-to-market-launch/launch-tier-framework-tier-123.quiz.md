# Quiz: Launch Tier Framework (Tier 1/2/3)

1. A company automatically classifies every "new feature" launch as Tier 2, regardless of what else the specific feature involves. One such feature this quarter also introduces a pricing change, but the team tiers it as Tier 2 anyway, matching the pattern. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Re-score every launch against the four criteria, not by pattern.
   - **B.** Re-score only the revenue-impact criterion, keeping the other three assumed.
   - **C.** Consistent tiering by launch type is correct practice for planning purposes.
   - **D.** This means the quarterly retrospective was never run for this launch type.

   **Correct answer: A.** Treating a launch type, rather than each individual launch, as permanently tiered is a named pitfall: a "new feature" is not automatically Tier 2 every time, since one that also introduces a pricing change can score Tier 1 on revenue or strategic impact. The fix is re-scoring every launch against the four criteria individually, not pattern-matching to a similarly named past launch.

   *Why not B:* Checking only revenue impact still assumes the other three criteria from the old pattern; a genuine re-score covers cross-functional dependency, visibility, and customer-facing change too, any of which could also have shifted.

   *Why not C:* Consistent tiering by type is exactly the named pitfall; the pricing change is a genuine Tier 1 signal (revenue or strategic impact) that pattern-matching by launch type causes the team to miss.

   *Why not D:* Nothing in the scenario describes a stale, unreviewed threshold; the issue is applying a fixed pattern to a launch that has materially different criteria this time.

2. Teams learn that Tier 1 launches come with an executive sponsor and press attention, and several teams start arguing their routine launches deserve Tier 1 treatment too. What does the model recommend?
   - **A.** Gate assignment through one owner, requiring high scores on at least two criteria.
   - **B.** Gate assignment through one owner, but let any single high score qualify for Tier 1.
   - **C.** Grant Tier 1 status automatically to any team that asks for it.
   - **D.** Eliminate Tier 1 entirely, since it creates an incentive problem.

   **Correct answer: A.** Tier inflation from teams seeking more resourcing or visibility is a named pitfall: once Tier 1 is known to come with an executive sponsor and press attention, teams have an incentive to argue every launch belongs there. The fix is gating assignment through a single owner scoring against published criteria, requiring genuinely high scores on at least two of the four, not just enthusiasm.

   *Why not B:* Centralising the decision fixes who decides but not the threshold: a single high score on one criterion is exactly the bar the model warns is too low, since it still lets a routine launch with one standout factor claim Tier 1.

   *Why not C:* Enthusiasm or a team's desire for visibility is explicitly not a valid basis for tier assignment; the model calls for scoring against defined criteria, not granting tier status based on requests.

   *Why not D:* Eliminating Tier 1 removes the model's ability to properly resource genuinely major launches; the fix is stricter, centrally gated assignment, not removing the tier altogether.

3. A company scored as Tier 1 for a five-person PMM team three years ago is still automatically treated as Tier 1 today, even though the team has since built a mature enablement motion that easily absorbs this type of launch as routine work. What does the model recommend?
   - **A.** Run the quarterly retrospective, revising thresholds after repeated mis-scoring.
   - **B.** Run the retrospective, but only once a year instead of every quarter.
   - **C.** Downgrade all launches of this type to Tier 3 without running a retrospective.
   - **D.** Tier thresholds are fixed constants that never vary with company maturity.

   **Correct answer: A.** Never running the retrospective, so tier thresholds drift out of date as the company scales, is a named pitfall: a launch that was major for a small team can become routine work with a mature motion in place, but an unreviewed model keeps treating it as major. The fix is running the quarterly retrospective without exception and revising thresholds after repeated mis-scoring.

   *Why not B:* An annual cadence still leaves up to three quarters of mis-scored launches accumulating before the next check; the model names the quarterly rhythm specifically so drift gets caught faster than that.

   *Why not C:* An immediate downgrade without running the retrospective process skips the model's own evidence-gathering step; the fix is a structured review, not an ungoverned, ad hoc reclassification.

   *Why not D:* The model explicitly ties threshold calibration to company maturity and scale via the quarterly retrospective; it treats thresholds as something that should shift over time, not as fixed constants.

4. A company builds its quarterly launch calendar and finds it contains eight "Tier 1" launches in a single quarter. What does the model suggest this likely indicates?
   - **A.** The calendar is likely mis-scored; use the distribution as a sense check.
   - **B.** Recount to confirm eight is accurate, but leave the underlying scoring unexamined.
   - **C.** This is a sign of strong execution and readiness for even more Tier 1 launches.
   - **D.** Leave the calendar unchanged, since tier count has no bearing on scoring accuracy.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly calls for building the calendar around tier distribution as a sense check: eight Tier 1 launches in one quarter is almost certainly mis-scored, since a healthy, fast-shipping product organisation should see Tier 3 as its largest bucket.

   *Why not B:* Confirming the count alone doesn't answer the actual question, whether each of those eight genuinely scores high on the four criteria; the sense check is meant to trigger re-scoring the launches, not just re-checking arithmetic.

   *Why not C:* A cluster of eight Tier 1 launches is named as a red flag for mis-scoring, not a sign of readiness for even more; the model treats this distribution as evidence something in the scoring needs re-checking.

   *Why not D:* The model explicitly uses distribution as a sense check on scoring accuracy; tier count is directly tied to whether individual launches were scored correctly against the criteria.

5. A "minor" feature update, originally scored Tier 3, is later found to require a pricing change and a partner integration that fell through mid-development. The team continues running it on its original Tier 3 resourcing plan. What does the model recommend?
   - **A.** Re-score the launch now against the model's four criteria.
   - **B.** Note the scope change at the quarterly retro, but keep the current Tier 3 plan until then.
   - **C.** Cancel the launch entirely, since any scope change disqualifies it from proceeding.
   - **D.** Escalate straight to Tier 1 without any further scoring against the criteria.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly calls for re-scoring a launch if its scope changes materially after initial classification, exactly this scenario (a pricing change added to what was scored as routine), rather than leaving it running on inadequate, now-mismatched resourcing.

   *Why not B:* Waiting for the scheduled quarterly retrospective leaves the launch running on an inadequate resourcing plan for however long remains in the quarter; the model calls for re-scoring as soon as the scope change is known, not deferring it to the next review.

   *Why not C:* A scope change calls for re-scoring and adjusting resourcing, not automatic cancellation; the model treats this as a resourcing correction, not grounds for stopping the launch.

   *Why not D:* Defaulting to the highest tier without scoring skips the model's actual process; the fix is re-scoring against the four criteria to determine the launch's genuinely appropriate new tier, which may or may not be Tier 1.
