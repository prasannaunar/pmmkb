# Quiz: Pre-mortem

1. A facilitator opens a pre-launch risk session by asking the group, "What could possibly go wrong with this launch?" rather than stating that the launch has already failed. What does Klein's technique suggest about this framing choice?
   - **A.** The already-happened framing produces more specific reasons than an open prompt.
   - **B.** Ask the group to imagine failure as "likely," rather than framing it as already happened.
   - **C.** "What could go wrong" is actually the more effective, less confrontational framing.
   - **D.** Framing does not matter as long as the session includes cross-functional participants.

   **Correct answer: A.** The technique's core mechanism is prospective hindsight: people are measurably better at generating specific explanations for an outcome framed as already having happened than for one framed as merely possible. An open "what could go wrong" prompt does not produce this effect; the premise must be stated as an already-happened fact.

   *Why not B:* This softens the premise back toward possibility: "likely to fail" still leaves room to hedge and defend the plan, whereas only the stated-as-fact premise removes that social pressure and produces the prospective-hindsight effect.

   *Why not C:* Feeling less confrontational is not the criterion Klein's research is built on; the specific, already-failed framing is what removes the social pressure to defend a plan and produces more specific reasons, regardless of comparative confrontation level.

   *Why not D:* Cross-functional participation matters, but it does not substitute for the specific framing; even a diverse group asked an open, hypothetical question produces less specific results than the stated-failure premise.

2. A facilitator skips the silent, individual brainstorming step and goes straight into open group discussion about what might cause the launch to fail. What does the entry predict will happen?
   - **A.** Groupthink and seniority bias take over; only the loudest voices make the list.
   - **B.** Write silently, but have the most senior person read their list aloud first.
   - **C.** Skipping the silent step has no meaningful effect on the resulting list.
   - **D.** This recommendation only matters for teams larger than fifteen people.

   **Correct answer: A.** Skipping the silent, individual brainstorm and going straight to open discussion is a named pitfall: without it, groupthink and seniority bias take over immediately, and only the most senior or vocal person's concerns make the list. The recovery is enforcing the individual-writing step before any discussion begins, without exception.

   *Why not B:* Writing silently first is the right step, but reading the most senior person's list aloud before anyone else's still anchors the room to their concerns first, undercutting the point of collecting everyone's list independently.

   *Why not C:* The entry explicitly ties the silent step to preventing anchoring on the most senior or vocal person's first guess; skipping it has a real, named negative effect on the resulting list's diversity and quality.

   *Why not D:* The entry does not scope this recommendation to team size; the anchoring effect it prevents applies regardless of how many participants are in the room.

3. A team runs a pre-mortem, generates a long list of clever failure scenarios, ranks them by severity and likelihood, and considers the exercise complete once the ranked list is published. No one is assigned to act on any of the risks. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Require a named owner and dated mitigation for every top-ranked risk.
   - **B.** Assign owners to only the top three risks, leaving the rest of the list unowned.
   - **C.** The premise was stated as a possibility rather than an already-happened fact.
   - **D.** Ranking the risks by severity and likelihood is itself sufficient action.

   **Correct answer: A.** Listing risks without assigning owners or mitigations, then treating the exercise as complete, is a named pitfall: a long list of clever scenarios nobody is accountable for fixing produces no better outcome than never running the session. The recovery is requiring a named owner and a specific, dated mitigation for every top-ranked risk before the session is considered closed.

   *Why not B:* Owning only the top three still leaves the rest of the ranked list, some of it genuinely top-ranked, without anyone accountable; the entry calls for a named owner on every top-ranked risk, not a partial subset.

   *Why not C:* The scenario describes the session having correctly used the already-happened framing (it generated a ranked list of failure reasons); the missing piece is ownership and mitigation, not the opening premise's wording.

   *Why not D:* Ranking alone is explicitly named as insufficient; the entry requires a named owner and dated mitigation for every top-ranked risk before the exercise is considered complete.

4. Leadership schedules a pre-mortem for a major launch, but by the time the session runs, the launch plan, budget, and timeline are already fully locked and cannot realistically change. Participants sense this and hold back their real concerns. What does the entry recommend?
   - **A.** Schedule the session early enough that the plan can still change, and act on a risk.
   - **B.** Run the session and act on any risks found, without changing when it happens.
   - **C.** Cancel the pre-mortem entirely, since a locked plan has no use for the exercise.
   - **D.** Replace the live session with an anonymous written survey instead.

   **Correct answer: A.** Running it as a formality after the plan is already locked is a named pitfall: participants sense when a session cannot actually influence the outcome and pull their punches accordingly, defeating the exercise's purpose. The recovery is scheduling it early enough to still change the plan, and visibly acting on at least one surfaced risk every time so participants see it isn't theatre.

   *Why not B:* Acting on risks afterward does not fix the root cause: the entry ties candour specifically to timing, holding the session before the plan locks, not to whether risks get acted on once it's already too late to change course.

   *Why not C:* The fix is scheduling the session earlier in the process, not cancelling it once timing has already slipped; the entry's recovery is proactive scheduling, not abandonment.

   *Why not D:* An anonymous written survey is not named as an equivalent substitute; the entry's specific technique relies on the structured silent-brainstorm-then-group-share sequence, not a generic survey format.

5. A pre-mortem for a major pricing change surfaces a risk that the new billing system might not hold up under real customer volume. The risk is ranked highest, assigned to engineering with a two-week deadline, and engineering runs a load test that reveals the system would indeed fail. What does this outcome demonstrate about the exercise's value?
   - **A.** It surfaced a defect that would otherwise have shipped live in production.
   - **B.** It shows pre-mortems are useful, but only for technical or engineering risks.
   - **C.** The exercise's value here was purely coincidental, not the technique's mechanism.
   - **D.** This shows pre-mortems should replace load testing entirely going forward.

   **Correct answer: A.** This matches the entry's own worked example directly: assigning a named owner and a dated mitigation to a top-ranked risk led to a load test that caught a defect before launch, exactly the value the exercise is designed to produce by surfacing specific, actionable failure modes early enough to fix them.

   *Why not B:* The technique is described as broadly applicable across risk types, technical, go-to-market, and customer-facing alike; the billing example is one instance, not evidence the method only works for engineering-flavoured risks.

   *Why not C:* The outcome follows directly from the exercise's mechanism, prospective hindsight surfacing specific, concrete risks, then assigning ownership and mitigation; it is not framed as coincidental in the entry.

   *Why not D:* The pre-mortem surfaced the need for a load test; it did not replace the load test itself, which was still necessary to confirm and fix the specific technical risk identified.
