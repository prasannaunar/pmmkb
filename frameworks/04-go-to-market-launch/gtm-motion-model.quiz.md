# Quiz: GTM Motion Model

1. A new VP of Sales with an enterprise-software background joins a company selling a $2,000-ACV product to a single decision-maker, and immediately starts building a field sales team by instinct. What does the model say about this decision?
   - **A.** Run the deal-data analysis and require a CAC-payback model before any hiring decision.
   - **B.** Trust the new VP's enterprise-sales background as sufficient evidence for this deal size.
   - **C.** Default to field sales regardless of ACV, since it applies the most oversight to every deal.
   - **D.** Treat this as a problem only once ACV drops below $500, not at $2,000.

   **Correct answer: A.** Defaulting to field sales because it is the motion leadership has run before is a named pitfall: a leader's habit can override the data, and a sales team's fully loaded cost rarely pays back against a sub-$5,000 ACV deal, guaranteeing a loss on every one closed. The recovery is running the deal-data analysis and requiring a CAC-payback model as the first artefact in the motion decision, not a hiring plan.

   *Why not B:* This substitutes the wrong evidence: a leader's experience from a different deal profile is exactly what the model warns can override the data, rather than the actual ACV-and-complexity analysis it calls for.

   *Why not C:* Field sales is explicitly matched to high ACV and high complexity; applying it regardless of deal size is exactly the named pitfall, not a universally safe default.

   *Why not D:* The model's guidance ties the mismatch to ACV relative to a sales team's fully loaded cost broadly, not to a specific lower threshold like $500; a $2,000 ACV deal already falls well within the range where this mismatch applies.

2. A company runs both a self-serve tier and a field-sales motion targeting the exact same mid-size customer segment, with no clear line between them. A prospect who self-serves at a lower price later encounters a rep quoting a much higher price for the same product. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Fence motions by an explicit threshold and route eligible leads back to self-serve.
   - **B.** Ask reps to voluntarily avoid quoting self-serve-eligible accounts, with no published rule.
   - **C.** Running two unfenced motions against the same segment is always beneficial.
   - **D.** Eliminate the field-sales motion entirely for this segment going forward.

   **Correct answer: A.** Running two motions against the same segment without fencing them is a named pitfall: reps and self-serve users compete for the same accounts, and a prospect finding a lower self-serve price after a rep has quoted higher loses trust in the whole pricing structure. The fix is fencing motions by an explicit, published threshold and routing self-serve-eligible leads back to that funnel.

   *Why not B:* An informal ask without a published, explicit threshold still leaves the boundary undefined and unenforceable; the fix is a fencing rule reps and the routing system can actually apply, not voluntary restraint.

   *Why not C:* Running multiple motions against the same unfenced segment is explicitly named as harmful, causing pricing inconsistency and lost trust, not a universally beneficial overlap.

   *Why not D:* The problem is the lack of fencing between two legitimate motions, not that field sales itself needs to be eliminated; both motions can coexist properly once clearly fenced.

3. A company launched with a pure self-serve motion at $49/month, which fit its buyers perfectly at the time. Three years later, average deal size has grown substantially and prospects increasingly ask for SSO and security questionnaires, but the company still runs the same self-serve-only funnel. What does the model say about this?
   - **A.** Re-run the motion assessment now, given how much the buyer profile has shifted.
   - **B.** Add SSO and the requested security features without re-running the motion assessment.
   - **C.** Retire self-serve entirely and switch fully to field sales for all customers.
   - **D.** This pattern is unrelated to the model, since it falls outside its stated scope.

   **Correct answer: A.** Treating the chosen motion as permanent is a named pitfall: a company still running pure self-serve against increasingly complex, committee-driven deals loses winnable business to competitors who already built the sales-assist or field motion those buyers expect. The model explicitly names sustained ACV growth and new buyer requirements as triggers to re-run the assessment.

   *Why not B:* Shipping the requested features answers a product question but skips the actual motion question; the model calls for re-running the ACV-and-complexity assessment itself, not just adding capabilities within the unchanged funnel.

   *Why not C:* Wholesale abandonment of self-serve is not the model's guidance; most companies end up running multiple motions simultaneously (self-serve for the core base, field sales for a new segment), as shown in the model's own Corvus Ops example.

   *Why not D:* ACV growth and new buyer requirements like SSO and security questionnaires are explicitly named as textbook triggers for re-running this model, squarely within its scope.

4. A team wants to determine which acquisition motion fits its product before writing a launch plan. What is the first concrete step the model recommends?
   - **A.** Plot the last 20 to 30 closed-won deals' ACV against stakeholder count.
   - **B.** Survey the sales team about which motion they personally believe fits best.
   - **C.** Adopt whichever motion the closest competitor is currently using.
   - **D.** Skip data analysis and choose the motion needing the least new hiring.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model's first applied step is explicit: plot actual deal data, ACV against stakeholder count, from the last 20 to 30 closed-won deals, before assuming a motion, since the data frequently contradicts what leadership assumes the company runs.

   *Why not B:* Rep preference reflects habit or comfort, not the actual deal-size and buyer-complexity evidence the model calls for using to match a motion to the product and segment.

   *Why not C:* A competitor's motion choice reflects their own deal profile, not necessarily this company's ACV and buyer complexity; the model calls for analysing this company's own deal data first.

   *Why not D:* Choosing based on minimal near-term hiring skips the actual diagnostic (ACV versus buyer complexity) the model is built to run, and risks a mismatch that costs more in CAC over time than it saves in short-term hiring effort.

5. A company's PLG motion shows a usage signal (adding more than 15 hosts) reliably predicting upgrade to a higher-priced plan. What should the company do with this signal, according to the model?
   - **A.** Confirm sales can act on the signal within a day or two, and build a sales-assist trigger.
   - **B.** Build the in-app upgrade prompt for the signal, without setting up any sales follow-up.
   - **C.** Let the signal pass with no follow-up, since PLG runs without human contact.
   - **D.** Route every account showing the signal directly to field sales, regardless of size.

   **Correct answer: A.** The model explicitly requires that a usage signal be specific enough that sales can act on it within a day or two of it firing; a signal no one follows up on is not a functioning motion, exactly the check this scenario calls for confirming before building further around the signal.

   *Why not B:* This builds only the automated half of the model's guidance; without a human sales-assist layer attached, an account whose signal implies real complexity still has no one to actually follow up with it.

   *Why not C:* PLG explicitly includes a sales-assist layer once usage signals indicate serious intent; it is not defined as having zero human involvement under any circumstances.

   *Why not D:* Escalating every triggered account straight to field sales overshoots the model's guidance that sales-assist is a lighter-touch layer, reserved for signals implying real deal size or complexity, not an automatic route to the heaviest motion.
