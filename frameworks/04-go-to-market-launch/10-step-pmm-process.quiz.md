# Quiz: 10-Step PMM Process

1. A Series B company with a specialised PMM team requires four separate approvals before any sales enablement asset can ship, and messaging is consistently stale by the time it reaches reps. What pitfall does this describe?
   - **A.** Cap sign-off at two approvers per asset, with a maximum five-day review turnaround.
   - **B.** Cap sign-off at two approvers per asset, but leave the review turnaround unset.
   - **C.** Build a lightweight weekly dashboard the team reviews before compiling monthly reporting.
   - **D.** Add a fifth approver from legal to catch anything the other four might miss.

   **Correct answer: A.** Over-documentation creating bureaucracy is a named pitfall: too many approval chains slow iteration until messaging is stale by the time it ships. The recovery is capping sign-off at two approvers per asset (typically PMM lead and sales lead) and setting a maximum five-business-day review turnaround.

   *Why not B:* This fixes only half the problem: capping the approver count without also capping the turnaround time still leaves an open-ended review window for messaging to go stale in.

   *Why not C:* A weekly dashboard is the recovery for a different named pitfall, measurement as a reporting afterthought; it does not address an approval chain that is itself too long.

   *Why not D:* Adding a fifth approver moves further in the direction the pitfall warns against; the fix is fewer sign-offs with a firm deadline, not additional review layers.

2. A PMM team compiles win rate, conversion, and collateral-quality data only once a month, purely to populate a leadership report, and never reviews it internally before then. Problems surface too late in the quarter to fix. What does the framework recommend?
   - **A.** Build a lightweight weekly dashboard the team reviews before compiling the monthly report.
   - **B.** Compile the same data weekly instead of monthly, purely to speed up the leadership report.
   - **C.** Stop tracking win rate and conversion internally; rely on sales' own reporting instead.
   - **D.** Reduce reporting frequency to quarterly, since monthly compilation is already too frequent.

   **Correct answer: A.** Treating measurement (Step 9) as a reporting afterthought is a named pitfall: compiling data only for a monthly leadership update means problems surface too late to fix within the quarter. The recovery is a lightweight weekly dashboard the PMM team reviews before the monthly version is compiled.

   *Why not B:* Compiling more often but still only for the leadership report misses the point: the fix is an internal weekly review the team acts on itself, not simply a faster feed into the same monthly report.

   *Why not C:* Relying solely on sales' reporting does not give PMM the internal, weekly visibility the framework calls for to catch issues before the monthly compilation.

   *Why not D:* Reducing frequency further delays problem detection even more; the fix is a more frequent, internal review cadence, not a less frequent one.

3. A team runs the full ten-step process, with all its rigour, for a small regional campaign with limited scope and revenue impact. What does the framework suggest instead?
   - **A.** Define a lightweight version of the process (Steps 1, 4, 5, 7, and 9) for smaller initiatives.
   - **B.** Keep all ten steps, but compress each step's timeline for smaller campaigns.
   - **C.** Skip the process entirely for small campaigns, applying no structure at all.
   - **D.** Reduce the process to a single step for every campaign, regardless of scale.

   **Correct answer: A.** Running all ten steps at full depth for every campaign, regardless of scale, is a named pitfall: a small regional campaign does not need the same rigour as a full launch, and forcing it through the complete process wastes time. The recovery is a defined lightweight version (Steps 1, 4, 5, 7, and 9) for smaller initiatives.

   *Why not B:* This shortens the wrong thing: compressing every step's timeline still forces the campaign through steps it does not need at all, rather than dropping to the lightweight subset the framework names.

   *Why not C:* Skipping process entirely removes the useful lightweight structure the framework recommends for smaller initiatives; some structure, just less of it, is the fix.

   *Why not D:* A single step for every campaign under-serves major launches that genuinely need the full ten-step rigour; the framework calls for scaling the process to match scope, not applying one fixed minimal version to everything.

4. A team completes Step 3's annual win/loss interview round but does not share findings with sales and product leadership for six weeks. What does the framework's guidance on Step 3 suggest is the risk?
   - **A.** Share a summary with sales and product within two weeks.
   - **B.** Share the summary, but only during the fixed annual formal review cycle.
   - **C.** Discard the interviews and re-run the whole round from scratch.
   - **D.** The delay only matters if fewer than 20 customers were interviewed.

   **Correct answer: A.** Step 3 explicitly calls for sharing a summary with sales and product leadership within two weeks of completing the interview round, precisely so insights do not go stale; a six-week delay works against that stated purpose.

   *Why not B:* Folding the summary into the annual review cycle still leaves it stale by the time it is shared; the two-week window exists precisely to beat that slower, calendar-driven cadence.

   *Why not C:* The interviews themselves remain valid data; the issue is the delay in sharing them, not a flaw in the interviews requiring them to be redone.

   *Why not D:* The two-week sharing guidance applies regardless of interview count; the risk of insights going stale is about timing, not sample size.

5. A campaign brief for a paid demand generation push is handed to the creative team without PMM or the campaign owner formally signing off on positioning, key message, or audience first. Creative production begins immediately. What does the framework say about this sequencing?
   - **A.** Require sign-off from both PMM and the campaign owner before creative production starts.
   - **B.** Require PMM's sign-off alone before creative starts, without the campaign owner's.
   - **C.** Require sign-off only for Tier 1 launches, not standard demand generation campaigns.
   - **D.** Let the creative team define positioning and messaging independently of PMM.

   **Correct answer: A.** Step 8 explicitly requires sign-off from both PMM and the campaign owner before creative production starts, specifically to avoid rework after assets are built once positioning, message, audience, or timing turns out to be wrong.

   *Why not B:* This covers only half the required gate: Step 8 names both PMM and the campaign owner as sign-off holders, and dropping the campaign owner still leaves audience and timing details unchecked before production starts.

   *Why not C:* Sign-off is named as a general requirement of Step 8 within the 10-Step Process itself; it is not scoped only to Tier 1 launches under a separate tiering model.

   *Why not D:* The campaign brief exists precisely so PMM supplies positioning and key message to the creative team; having creative define these independently reverses the framework's intended division of responsibility.
