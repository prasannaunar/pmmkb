# Concepts

## GTM Strategy vs Product Marketing

**Type:** Primer.

**What it is:** Go-to-market (GTM) strategy and product marketing are not the same thing, though the two are commonly used interchangeably in PMM job postings and internal decks. GTM strategy is the company-wide plan for how a product reaches, wins, and keeps customers: it spans product packaging, pricing, the sales motion, demand generation, partnerships, customer success, and often legal and finance (contracts, revenue recognition). Product marketing is one function that contributes to that plan, and frequently orchestrates it, but does not own every part of it. Confusing the two leads to two opposite failure modes: PMMs who assume they are accountable for outcomes (pipeline, close rate, retention) that actually depend on sales execution or product quality, and PMMs who defer entirely on questions (positioning, messaging, launch sequencing) that are squarely theirs to own. This primer draws the line, because every other entry in this knowledge base assumes you already know where PMM's remit starts and stops.

**Why it matters:** Every framework in Categories 4, 5, and 7 (Go-to-Market & Launch, Lifecycle & Workflow, Strategy & Planning) describes work PMM does inside a GTM plan, not the whole plan itself. A PMM who reads the 10-Step PMM Process or the Complete GTM Workflow Stages and treats it as "the GTM strategy" will over-promise on results that require sales capacity, pricing decisions, or product investment PMM does not control. Equally, a PMM who assumes GTM strategy belongs entirely to a VP of Marketing or a Chief Revenue Officer, and waits to be told what to do, will miss the parts of the plan (positioning, message architecture, sales enablement content, launch sequencing) that no one else in the company is naturally positioned to author. Getting this distinction right is also the difference between a defensible answer and a vague one when leadership asks "why did the launch underperform": a clear ownership map lets you say precisely which part of the plan broke, rather than absorbing blame for the whole outcome or deflecting all of it.

**Key distinctions:**
- **GTM strategy is cross-functional and outcome-owned by revenue leadership** (typically a CRO, CMO, or CEO in an early-stage company); it covers the full commercial system, product, pricing, channel, sales capacity, and customer success, not just messaging.
- **Product marketing is a function that contributes expertise to specific parts of that plan.** It does not have its own P&L, does not set sales quota, and does not decide product roadmap, even though it influences all three through positioning, market input, and launch requirements.
- **PMM frequently orchestrates GTM execution without owning the GTM strategy itself.** The frameworks in Category 4 (3-Step, 7-Step, 10-Step PMM Process, Complete GTM Workflow Stages) describe PMM's operational role in sequencing a launch; they are not a substitute for a company-level GTM strategy that also has to decide, for example, whether to sell through direct sales or a partner channel, or how much to invest in paid acquisition versus organic.
- **A simple ownership map clarifies the split.** PMM typically **owns**: positioning, messaging, launch narrative, sales enablement content, competitive intelligence synthesis, and buyer research synthesis. PMM typically **contributes to**: pricing and packaging (input, not final call), channel and motion selection (see the GTM Motion Model in Category 4), campaign briefs, and content strategy. PMM typically **supports, but does not own**: sales quota attainment, demand generation execution, product roadmap prioritisation, and customer success playbooks. The exact split varies by company size and maturity (a solo PMM at a 10-person startup often owns more of this list by necessity), but the categories themselves hold up across stages.
- **"GTM plan" and "launch plan" are not synonyms either.** A launch plan is a single execution of part of the GTM strategy, typically for one product, feature, or market entry. The GTM strategy is the standing, company-level answer to how the business goes to market at all; a launch plan is downstream of it, not a replacement for it.

**Where PMM fits:** In practice, PMM is usually the function best placed to hold the pen on cross-functional GTM documents (a positioning brief, a launch plan, a campaign brief), because PMM sits at the intersection of product, sales, and marketing and is the one role expected to translate between all three. That drafting role can look, from the outside, like PMM "owns" GTM strategy. It does not: PMM authors the parts of the plan that require deep customer and market fluency, and coordinates the parts that require other functions' commitment (a sales leader's headcount plan, a demand generation budget, a product team's release date), but PMM cannot unilaterally commit those other functions' resources. When a launch or GTM motion underperforms, the useful diagnostic question is not "did PMM fail," but "which specific piece of the ownership map broke": was positioning untested, was the channel wrong for the segment, did sales capacity fall short of the plan, or did the product not meet the bar the messaging promised. Naming the specific failure point is what makes a post-mortem actionable instead of a general finger-pointing exercise.
