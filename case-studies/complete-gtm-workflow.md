## Freightline Builds a GTM Motion from Foundation through Scale

**Framework:** Complete GTM Workflow Stages | **Category:** 4: Go-to-Market & Launch
**Company:** Freightline, a fictional pre-Series A supply chain analytics SaaS company with 12 employees, founded by two former logistics operations managers

### Before state

Freightline had built a working product that analysed shipment data to predict delivery delays before they happened. The two founders had closed 8 paying customers through personal networks, generating $140,000 in ARR. But growth had stalled. The founders had tried three marketing tactics simultaneously: sponsoring a logistics trade publication, running LinkedIn ads targeting supply chain directors, and cold-emailing freight brokers pulled from an industry directory. None of the three produced a measurable result, and the team could not tell whether the problem was the product, the message, or the channel. The company was three months from a Series A fundraise and needed a defensible GTM narrative, not just more customers, but a repeatable motion that investors could believe would scale.

The founding PMM, the company's ninth hire, introduced the Complete GTM Workflow Stages as the operating framework to sequence the build rather than continuing to test channels against unvalidated positioning.

### Framework applied

**Phase 1: Foundation (Weeks 1 to 8).** PMM conducted 24 customer discovery interviews: 8 with existing customers, 10 with prospects who had seen a demo but not bought, and 6 with companies that had never heard of Freightline. The interview guide focused on three questions: "What happens when a shipment arrives late?", "How do you find out it's going to be late?", and "What does that cost you?"

The pattern was clear. Nineteen of 24 interviewees described the same core pain in near-identical language: "We find out a shipment is late when the customer calls to complain." The cost was specific and quantifiable: late-delivery penalties averaging $2,200 per incident for mid-size shippers (those moving 500 to 3,000 shipments per month), plus an estimated 15% of affected customers reducing their next order after a late delivery. The ICP crystallised around mid-size freight shippers, not brokers and not enterprise logistics firms, because mid-size shippers felt the per-incident cost most acutely and lacked the in-house analytics teams that enterprise firms already had.

The positioning hypothesis was drafted: "For mid-size freight shippers who find out about delays when customers complain, Freightline predicts late shipments 6 to 12 hours before arrival, so your team can intervene before the customer ever notices." PMM explicitly labelled this as a hypothesis to be tested in Phase 2.

The success metric for Foundation was 70% of interviewees describing the same core pain unprompted. The actual figure was 79% (19 of 24), so the team moved to Phase 2.

**Phase 2: Launch (Weeks 9 to 16).** PMM built a minimal asset set: a single landing page, a one-pager, and a demo script, all built around the "predict before the customer calls" hypothesis. No case studies were produced yet; instead, PMM asked the two most engaged existing customers for a short, attributable quote each, holding full case studies for Phase 3 when more outcome data would be available.

The team chose cold outbound as the single primary channel, because the ICP (operations managers at mid-size freight shippers) was reachable via LinkedIn and industry directories, the deal size ($18,000 to $36,000 ACV) justified a human touch, and the founders had already proven they could close deals over video calls. PMM wrote a three-email outbound sequence, tested on 80 prospects over four weeks. The opening line of the first email named the pain directly: "Most shippers find out about a delay when the customer calls. What if your team knew 12 hours earlier?"

Conversion from outbound to demo was 16%, above the 15% success threshold. Sales confirmed messaging alignment in 85% of qualified conversations, above the 80% threshold. Phase 2 passed.

**Phase 3: Scale (Months 5 to 12).** With outbound validated, PMM recommended adding capacity before adding a second channel. The company hired one SDR to run the outbound motion full-time, freeing the founders to focus on closing. Monthly outbound volume rose from 80 to 300 prospects contacted. By month 8, the pipeline had grown to 45 SQLs per month, against a Phase 3 target of 3x the Phase 2 baseline.

Win rate held at 23%, above the 20% threshold. CAC payback sat at 14 months, within the under-18-month gate. PMM produced three customer case studies during this phase, each with specific, measurable outcomes: one customer reduced late-delivery complaints by 62% in its first quarter; another avoided $84,000 in penalty fees over six months; a third credited Freightline with retaining two accounts that had been on the verge of switching providers.

A secondary channel, a monthly webinar co-hosted with a logistics industry association, was launched in month 10 only after the primary channel showed consistent CAC payback. The webinar generated an additional 8 to 12 qualified leads per month at a CAC roughly 40% lower than outbound, confirming it as a viable second motion.

PMM established a monthly win/loss review cadence in month 6, interviewing 4 to 6 recent wins and losses each month. The most actionable finding from this cadence was that 5 of 8 losses in months 7 and 8 cited "integration with our TMS" (transport management system) as the blocker; this fed directly into a product roadmap decision to build a native integration with the two most common TMS platforms in the segment.

### Results

By the end of month 12 (the end of Phase 3):

- ARR had grown from $140,000 to $1.1M, an approximate 7.8x increase from the pre-framework baseline.
- The company had 38 paying customers, up from 8, all within the validated mid-size shipper ICP.
- Win rate held steady at 22 to 25% across the Scale phase, confirming the motion was repeatable rather than dependent on founder relationships.
- CAC payback improved from 14 months at the start of Scale to 11 months by the end, driven by the lower-cost webinar channel and shorter sales cycles as case studies and customer references became available.
- The Series A closed successfully in month 9, with investors citing the phased GTM data, specifically the validated conversion rates and the gated progression between phases, as a key factor in their confidence.

### What they would do differently

The founding PMM would have enforced the Phase 2 success gate more strictly. At week 14, two weeks before the Phase 2 deadline, outbound conversion sat at only 12%, below the 15% threshold. Rather than staying in Phase 2 and iterating on the outbound sequence, the founders pushed to begin hiring the SDR (a Phase 3 activity) in parallel, arguing that the hire would take weeks to onboard anyway. PMM relented. In hindsight, the two additional weeks of Phase 2 iteration, which eventually moved the conversion rate from 12% to 16%, should have been completed before any Phase 3 commitment was made. The SDR started onboarding against messaging that was still being revised, and their first three weeks of outreach used a sequence that was replaced two weeks later.

The team also wished they had started the monthly win/loss cadence in Phase 2 rather than Phase 3. The TMS integration blocker, which emerged as the top loss reason in months 7 and 8, likely existed earlier but was invisible because no structured loss interviews were running during the Launch phase. Starting that cadence sooner would have surfaced the integration gap four to six months earlier, giving product more time to build the fix before it became a pattern of lost deals.
