## Procurewise Discovers Its Real Value Proposition

**Framework:** Value Proposition Canvas | **Category:** 2: Positioning & Messaging
**Company:** Procurewise, a fictional mid-market procurement automation SaaS company with 80 employees and 350 customers

### Before state

Procurewise had built a procurement automation product with a strong feature set: purchase order automation, supplier management, budget tracking, approval workflows, and a reporting dashboard. Marketing led with a feature tour on the homepage, and the sales deck walked prospects through every module in sequence. Despite solid product reviews and a low churn rate among retained customers, two problems persisted. Trial-to-paid conversion sat at 6%, roughly half the company's internal target. Win/loss interviews surfaced a recurring comment from lost deals: "It looks capable, but we couldn't figure out which part solves our specific problem." Prospects were not rejecting the product; they were failing to connect it to their own pain.

The VP of Product Marketing suspected the issue was not the product but the way it was presented. Every feature was given equal prominence, and nothing in the messaging told a prospect which capability mattered most for their situation.

### Framework applied

**Step 1: Profile the customer.** PMM ran 16 interviews with customers in Procurewise's primary segment: finance operations managers at mid-market companies with 200 to 1,500 employees and no dedicated procurement team. The interviews used open-ended questions drawn from the framework's guidance: "What are you trying to accomplish?", "What frustrates you most about the current process?", and "What would a good outcome look like?"

- **Jobs:** Get purchase requests approved and tracked without chasing people by email; maintain visibility over company spend without building manual spreadsheets; stay within budget without policing every individual purchase.
- **Pains:** Approval bottlenecks (the most frequently cited pain, mentioned by 13 of 16 interviewees); no real-time spend visibility, so budget overruns surfaced only at month-end close; supplier invoices arriving with no matching PO, creating reconciliation headaches; the finance manager personally acting as the approval bottleneck because there was no automated routing.
- **Gains:** Approvals that happen in hours, not days; a live spend dashboard the CFO can check without asking anyone; suppliers paid on time without manual follow-up; the finance manager freed from being the single point of failure in every purchase.

**Step 2: Map the value proposition.**

- **Features:** Purchase order automation, configurable approval workflows, real-time budget dashboards, supplier portal, invoice matching, reporting module.
- **Pain relievers:** Approval workflows route requests automatically based on amount and department, removing the finance manager as the bottleneck (directly addresses the top pain). Real-time budget dashboards surface overspend the day it happens, not at month-end (addresses the second-ranked pain). Invoice matching flags PO mismatches before payment, cutting reconciliation time (addresses the third-ranked pain).
- **Gain creators:** Automated routing means approvals complete in a median of 4 hours rather than 3 days (the figure came from existing customer usage data). The live dashboard gives the CFO a self-serve spend view, removing the finance manager from reporting requests entirely.

**Step 3: Analyse the fit.**

PMM scored the fit for each documented pain and gain as strong, partial, or none.

- Approval bottleneck: strong fit; the workflow engine addressed it directly and usage data confirmed the outcome.
- No real-time spend visibility: strong fit; the dashboard was already in production and actively used.
- Invoice reconciliation: partial fit; the matching feature caught mismatches but did not yet auto-resolve them, requiring manual follow-up.
- Supplier payment timeliness: partial fit; Procurewise tracked POs but did not automate payment itself.
- The reporting module, the feature the marketing team had previously used as the homepage hero screenshot, scored as addressing no top-ranked pain or gain. Customers described it as "fine" but never cited it as a reason they bought or stayed.

**Step 4: Refine messaging.** PMM restructured messaging priority based on the fit analysis. The homepage lead changed from "All-in-one procurement automation" (a feature-tour framing) to "Approvals in hours, not days: procurement automation that removes the bottleneck" (a pain-reliever framing tied to the top customer pain). The sales deck was reordered to open with the approval-workflow demo rather than the supplier-management module. The reporting module was moved from the homepage to a secondary features page.

**Step 5: Iterate.** PMM scheduled a quarterly canvas review, pulling fresh interview data each cycle.

**Step 6: Validate.** PMM showed the revised messaging to eight target buyers in the ICP. Six of eight said the approval-bottleneck framing "describes exactly what I'm dealing with." Two flagged the invoice-matching partial fit, noting they needed auto-resolution, not just flagging; this finding was routed to the product roadmap.

### Results

Within one quarter of rolling out the revised messaging:

- Trial-to-paid conversion rose from 6% to 11%, because prospects arriving through the new messaging immediately understood which problem the product solved for them.
- Sales cycle shortened from 44 days to 31 days. Sales reported that the approval-bottleneck framing cut two to three discovery-call exchanges that had previously been spent helping prospects figure out which module was relevant to them.
- Homepage-to-trial conversion rose from 2.4% to 4.1%, driven by the new hero section leading with a recognised pain rather than a product tour.
- The invoice-matching gap identified in the fit analysis entered the product roadmap and shipped the following quarter; once it did, PMM upgraded the fit score from partial to strong and updated the messaging to include auto-resolution.

### What they would do differently

The team would have involved sales earlier in the fit analysis. Two of the three pain relievers identified in Step 2 were already being used informally by the top-performing sales rep, who had independently discovered that opening with the approval-workflow demo closed deals faster. Had PMM consulted sales before building the canvas, the fit analysis would have been confirmed weeks earlier, and the revised messaging could have shipped sooner.

PMM also underestimated how much internal resistance the reporting module's demotion would generate. The engineering team had spent a full quarter building the latest version of the dashboard, and moving it off the homepage felt like a public deprioritisation of their work. In hindsight, PMM would have shared the canvas's fit analysis with engineering before making the change, so the decision read as evidence-based rather than dismissive.
