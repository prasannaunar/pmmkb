## Relaytics Scales PMM from One Generalist to a Structured Team

**Framework:** 10-Step PMM Process | **Category:** 4: Go-to-Market & Launch
**Company:** Relaytics, a fictional Series C customer intelligence platform with 210 employees, $22M ARR, and three PMMs

### Before state

Relaytics had grown from $4M to $22M ARR over three years, but its product marketing function had not kept pace. The original founding PMM had left six months earlier, and the three PMMs now on staff operated independently: one wrote content on request from demand generation, another built sales decks whenever a rep asked, and the third focused on competitive intelligence with no defined cadence or audience. There was no shared positioning document, no quarterly review, and no measurement of whether PMM's output was actually moving pipeline or win rates. Each PMM produced work that was individually competent but collectively incoherent. The sales team reported that the pitch varied depending on which PMM had built the deck for that quarter, and two large deals were lost in the same month with loss notes citing "confusing and inconsistent messaging."

The newly hired VP of Marketing brought in the 10-Step PMM Process as the operating framework to give the team structure, clear ownership boundaries, and a measurement cadence.

### Framework applied

**Steps 1 and 2: Market Sizing, Segmentation, and Buyer Journey Mapping.** The VP of Marketing assigned the competitive-intelligence PMM to lead these steps, since that role already held the closest relationship with sales and the most CRM access. The team pulled TAM and SAM data for Relaytics's two segments: mid-market e-commerce companies (200 to 2,000 employees) and enterprise retail brands (2,000+ employees). Buyer journey mapping involved shadowing 8 live sales calls and reviewing 14 call recordings, which revealed a finding no one had documented: the mid-market buyer made a decision within two calls, while the enterprise buyer's process involved a separate security review and a procurement step that added four to six weeks. This meant the two segments needed not just different messaging but different collateral entirely; the enterprise segment needed a pre-built security questionnaire and a procurement-friendly ROI model that the mid-market segment never saw.

**Step 3: Win/Loss Analysis.** The team interviewed 22 recent deals (10 wins, 12 losses) over four weeks. The biggest finding was that 7 of 12 losses cited "we weren't sure what problem you solve that [named competitor] doesn't" as the primary reason. This was the same differentiation gap the two lost deals had flagged, now confirmed across a larger sample.

**Steps 4 and 5: Positioning Statement Development and Message Architecture.** PMM drafted segment-specific positioning, tested it internally with 6 sales reps, and revised it based on two rounds of feedback. The core message became: "See which customers are about to churn, and why, before your renewal team finds out the hard way." Three value pillars sat beneath it: predictive churn signals drawn from product-usage patterns, renewal-risk scoring that updated daily rather than quarterly, and integration with existing CRM and CS platforms so the insight lived where reps already worked. Each pillar carried proof points sourced from customer data: a named mid-market customer whose renewal team caught 34 at-risk accounts in its first month using the predictive signal, and an enterprise customer whose time from risk detection to intervention dropped from 18 days to 3 days.

**Step 6: Collateral and Channel Strategy.** PMM mapped messaging to four channels: website, email nurture, events, and sales. Each channel was assigned an owner from the PMM team, and a refresh cadence was set: quarterly for the website and sales deck, twice yearly for event collateral. This step also uncovered dead content: 14 blog posts and 3 one-pagers still live on the website referenced the old, inconsistent positioning. PMM deprecated them within two weeks.

**Step 7: Sales Enablement.** PMM built a new sales deck, two segment-specific one-pagers, a competitive battlecard against the named competitor from the win/loss analysis, and an objection-handling guide. Critically, the team ran a 30-minute quarterly survey of the sales team asking which assets they actually opened during live deals; the first survey revealed that 40% of reps had never opened the previous quarter's battlecard because it was stored in a folder they did not check.

**Steps 8 and 9: Campaign Brief and Performance Measurement.** PMM wrote a campaign brief for the demand-generation team's next quarter, specifying positioning, audience, success metrics, and timing. Monthly reporting was established tracking three numbers: win rate by segment, conversion rate by channel, and collateral usage rate (the percentage of deals where a rep opened at least one current-quarter asset). This last metric, new to the team, became the most discussed number in monthly reviews.

**Step 10: Iteration and Refresh Cycle.** The VP of Marketing set a fixed 90-day review cadence with a calendar invite and a decision log. The first review, 90 days after the process launched, found that mid-market win rate had improved but enterprise had not, prompting a second round of win/loss interviews focused solely on enterprise deals.

### Results

Over two quarters of running the 10-Step process:

- Mid-market win rate rose from 24% to 37%. Enterprise win rate moved more slowly, from 14% to 19%, but the direction was positive and a dedicated enterprise messaging revision was underway.
- Sales cycle for mid-market shortened from 34 days to 26 days, attributable to the pre-built security questionnaire and the clearer positioning that reduced discovery-call confusion.
- Collateral usage rate, the new metric, rose from 38% to 71% after the team moved all assets into the CRM's content library (where reps already worked) rather than a separate shared drive.
- Zero deals in the second quarter cited "inconsistent messaging" as a loss reason, down from two per month before the process launched.

### What they would do differently

The team would have started with Step 3 (Win/Loss Analysis) before Steps 1 and 2 (Market Sizing and Buyer Journey Mapping). The win/loss interviews produced the most actionable insight, the differentiation gap, and that finding would have sharpened the buyer journey mapping that preceded it. Running win/loss first would have saved roughly two weeks of rework on the buyer journey maps.

The VP of Marketing also wished she had defined the "lightweight" version of the process earlier. Within the first quarter, two minor feature launches were forced through the full 10-step sequence, consuming PMM time that would have been better spent on the enterprise messaging revision. Adding a formal lightweight track (Steps 4, 5, 7, and 9 only) for Tier 2 and Tier 3 launches at the outset would have prevented this.
