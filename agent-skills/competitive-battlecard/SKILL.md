# Competitive battlecard

A battlecard is a field tool a rep reads in the ninety seconds before a call.
It is not a research document. Everything that is not immediately usable in a
live conversation belongs in a linked appendix.

Paths below are relative to the knowledge base root. Full entries are in
`frameworks/09-sales-enablement.md` (Competitive Battlecard Framework, VARS)
and `frameworks/07-strategy-planning.md` (Competitive Intelligence & Positioning
Update Framework).

## Procedure

1. **Confirm the competitor deserves a card.** A reasonable bar is ten or more
   competitive deals a quarter. A card for a rarely met competitor is wasted
   effort and one more document that erodes trust in the whole set.
2. **Capture their pitch in their words.** Watch a demo if you can get one,
   read their site and pricing page, pull their capture from recent lost-deal
   notes, and ask reps what the prospect repeated back after meeting them.
   Never write a strawman version; it collapses the moment a prepared prospect
   quotes them accurately.
3. **Build the counter-position per headline claim.** Not "we are better" but
   the mechanism and the proof. If they lead on price, the counter is a total
   cost of ownership comparison, not an assertion of worth.
4. **Plant one or two landmines.** Questions a rep asks early, before the
   competitor is in the room, that expose a known weakness without naming
   anyone: "how does your current process handle multi-region compliance?" is a
   landmine when you know their product does not.
5. **Write objection responses verbatim.** For the three to five objections
   reps hear most about this competitor, write the exact sentence to say. Reps
   under pressure read a script far better than they improvise from themes.
6. **Attach proof to every claim.** A named customer outcome, a benchmark, or
   third-party validation. Pull it from win/loss interviews and named
   references. Prospects increasingly ask for evidence live on the call.
7. **Cut to one page.** Ruthlessly. Push depth into a linked appendix.
8. **Train the delivery, not just the content.** Run VARS with reps: Validate
   (learn what is actually on the buyer's mind before rebutting), Acknowledge
   (name the competitor's genuine strength), Reframe (shift to a more
   fundamental problem they are not positioned to solve), Specify (close on a
   concrete proof point, demo moment, or named outcome). Role-play all four
   steps with two or three reps pushing back as sceptical prospects, and revise
   any language that does not survive contact.
9. **Version, distribute, refresh.** One owner, a version number on the card, a
   quarterly refresh cycle, and an off-cycle update whenever the competitor
   launches, reprices, or repositions.

## Gotchas

- **A card without win/loss evidence is an internal opinion.** The counter-
  positions have to come from what buyers said, not from what product
  management believes about the rival. If no evidence exists, run
  `win-loss-programme` first or scope the card to the one competitor where it
  does.
- **Reps get defensive because they were given content but no technique.** If
  reps have a card and still stumble, the gap is delivery. Train VARS rather
  than rewriting the card again.
- **Naming the competitor in a landmine defeats it.** The question has to be
  answerable innocently by the prospect; the moment it names a vendor, it reads
  as a trap.
- **Never dismiss an obvious competitor strength.** A buyer who has used the
  rival for years will stop trusting everything said afterwards. Acknowledge
  it, then reframe.
- **Stale cards are worse than none.** A counter-position aimed at a feature
  gap the competitor closed last quarter loses the deal and the reps' trust in
  the set. Date every card and audit at the quarterly refresh.
- **One card per competitor, not per deal.** Deal-specific detail belongs in
  the CRM opportunity, not in a document distributed company-wide.

## Output

Deliver the completed one-pager (`assets/battlecard-template.md`), the linked
appendix of supporting research, the role-play notes and the revisions they
forced, and the refresh schedule with a named owner.
