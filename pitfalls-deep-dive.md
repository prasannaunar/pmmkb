# Pitfalls Deep Dive

Each of the 66 entries in this knowledge base includes a Pitfalls section with two or three common mistakes and recovery steps. This document goes deeper on the pitfalls that compound most severely when left unchecked, covering four high-frequency framework areas: STP, positioning, GTM execution, and competitive strategy. It adds cascade effects, early warning signs, and detailed recovery playbooks that the per-entry sections do not have room for.

Use this alongside the original entries, not instead of them.

---

## STP: Targeting Too Many Segments at Once

**The mistake:** Choosing three or four target segments simultaneously instead of committing to one, because the team cannot agree which single segment to own first.

**How it compounds:** In the first month, messaging tries to serve all segments and ends up generic. By month three, sales collateral contains competing value propositions that reps pick between based on gut feel. By month six, marketing spend is fragmented with no segment generating enough volume for a statistically meaningful read on what is working. The next planning cycle repeats the same "let's keep all three" decision.

**Early warning signs:**
- The positioning statement uses "and" more than once in the target customer clause.
- Sales reps describe the ICP differently from one another in pipeline reviews.
- Win rate by segment cannot be reported because the CRM does not tag deals by segment consistently.

**Recovery playbook:** Pull two quarters of closed-won deals and rank segments by win rate, sales cycle length, and CAC payback. The segment winning on at least two of three is your primary. Commit for two full quarters: rewrite messaging for that segment only, pause spend on the others. Timeline: two to four weeks to score and rewrite; two quarters to validate.

**Example:** Growthstack, a fictional B2B analytics company, targeted "startups, mid-market, and enterprise" for eighteen months. Blended win rate sat at 14%. When PMM pulled segment-level data, mid-market showed a 21% win rate and 23-day cycle versus 9% and 67 days for enterprise. Committing exclusively to mid-market lifted win rate to 31% within six months.

## STP: Positioning That Describes the Category, Not a Position

**The mistake:** Writing a positioning statement that any competitor could use verbatim, such as "we are an expense management platform that helps finance teams track spending."

**How it compounds:** By month two, sales reports prospects asking "what makes you different?" in every discovery call. By month four, win/loss interviews show prospects who chose a competitor could not distinguish the two products from the messaging. By month six, a full quarter of marketing budget has amplified a message that does not differentiate.

**Early warning signs:**
- Prospects, asked to describe your product to a colleague, use the same words they would use for any competitor.
- The "unlike" clause in Moore's positioning template is missing or names a competitor without explaining why the difference matters.
- The homepage hero section could be swapped onto a competitor's site without anyone noticing.

**Recovery playbook:** Pull five to eight recent win/loss interviews and isolate why winners chose you over the named alternative. Rewrite the statement so the differentiator appears in the main clause. Test with ten target buyers cold; if fewer than seven can describe what makes the product different, revise again. Timeline: three weeks for review and rewrite; one quarter to roll out.

**Example:** April Dunford documented this pattern in *Obviously Awesome* (2019), noting that most companies begin by describing their product in generic category terms. Her diagnostic question, "what would your best customers use if you did not exist?", consistently surfaces a different competitive set and a different differentiator from the one the current positioning leads with.

## Positioning: Positioning by Committee

**The mistake:** Running the positioning process as a consensus exercise across six or more stakeholders, where every function adds its preferred angle until the statement tries to say everything.

**How it compounds:** By the second revision, the statement has four benefit claims and two target descriptions because cutting any one would overrule someone senior. By month two, it is too long for a homepage hero, so each team writes its own shorter version. By month four, three or four competing messages exist in market.

**Early warning signs:**
- The statement has been through more than four revision rounds with no convergence.
- The draft contains qualifying language ("we also", "in addition") signalling unresolved internal debate.
- Sales and marketing are both writing their own one-line descriptions because the official statement is too long to use.

**Recovery playbook:** Designate one owner with explicit authority to cut. Run a forced-rank exercise: present six to eight candidate benefit claims drawn from win/loss interviews. Each participant ranks them silently before discussion. The top two claims become the statement; the rest become supporting proof points. Timeline: one focused session to force-rank; one week to draft and circulate.

**Example:** Harmon Analytics, a fictional mid-market BI company, ran a positioning workshop with twelve stakeholders. The resulting statement claimed "the most intuitive dashboards, the deepest integrations, and the strongest security, built for data teams, operations leaders, and executives." Message testing scored it 24% "immediately clear." A forced-rank rerun using buyer language from fifteen win/loss interviews produced "dashboards your whole team actually uses, without a training session," which scored above 60% clarity.

## Positioning: Failing to Test Before Scaling

**The mistake:** Committing budget to a positioning statement that has never been tested with real target buyers outside the company.

**How it compounds:** By week four, paid campaigns lock in spend against the untested message. By month two, conversion data looks soft but the team attributes it to "campaign ramp-up." By month four, enough data confirms the messaging is not landing, but reversing course means rewriting assets and re-briefing sales. Sunk-cost pressure delays the fix by another quarter.

**Early warning signs:**
- No target buyer outside the company has seen the statement before it ships.
- Confidence is based on internal enthusiasm rather than external evidence.
- The first paid campaign under-performs the previous campaign's conversion rate after four weeks.

**Recovery playbook:** Before any positioning ships at scale, test two or three candidate versions with 30 to 50 verified target buyers, scoring each on clarity, relevance, and differentiation. If the winner scores below 50% clarity, revise and retest. Build the test into the launch timeline as a fixed three to five business day gate. For an already-shipped underperformer, pull qualitative feedback from the first 30 days and rewrite the specific point where prospects disengage.

**Example:** Peep Laja, founder of the B2B message-testing platform Wynter, has documented that internal teams are consistently the worst judges of whether their own messaging is clear to an outsider, because they already understand the product and cannot simulate a cold first impression. Wynter's published case data shows versions the internal team rated highest frequently underperform in blind buyer testing.

## GTM Execution: Skipping Validation Before Scaling

**The mistake:** Moving budget into Phase 3 (Scale) of the Complete GTM Workflow Stages before Phase 2 (Launch) has validated positioning with a baseline conversion rate.

**How it compounds:** By month two, CAC is two to three times target because the message is not converting, but the team reads this as a channel problem and shifts spend rather than questioning the message. By month four, the budget has been spent scaling a motion never proven to work, and data is confounded by multiple channel switches, making it impossible to isolate the problem. Recovery requires resetting to Phase 2 with less budget and less internal credibility.

**Early warning signs:**
- Phase 2's conversion threshold (15% in the GTM Workflow Stages entry) has not been met, but Phase 3 budget is approved anyway.
- The team is optimising ad creative before confirming the core positioning converts in any channel.
- CAC payback in the first month of scaled spend is more than double the target.

**Recovery playbook:** Pause scaled spend. Run the current positioning against 50 target prospects in a single controlled channel. If conversion is below 15%, the problem is the message; rewrite using win/loss interview language and retest. Re-enter Phase 3 only once two consecutive cohorts clear the threshold. Timeline: four to six weeks to test and confirm; two to four more weeks to re-scale if positioning needed revision.

**Example:** Pipeshift, a fictional sales-engagement SaaS company, committed $120,000 in quarterly ad spend after three weeks of validation at a 9% conversion rate. Four landing page variants over eight weeks failed to move conversion above 11%. Only after pausing and running 20 outbound prospect conversations did PMM discover the positioning led with "AI-powered outreach" while buyers cared about "reply rates." Rewriting around reply rates produced a 22% conversion rate and a CAC 40% lower than the failed attempt.

## GTM Execution: Misaligned Timing Between Product and Launch

**The mistake:** Locking a launch date to an external commitment and shipping even when the product, enablement, or evidence is not ready.

**How it compounds:** By week four, early adopters hit the gap between what was promised and what exists; support tickets spike. By month two, sales quietly stops pitching the feature because it creates more objections than it resolves. By month four, the feature has an internal reputation problem ("we launched that too early") that persists long after the product has caught up, making a re-launch harder.

**Early warning signs:**
- The launch date was set before readiness criteria were defined.
- Engineering is closing critical issues in the final week; "launch" has been redefined to mean "public announcement" rather than "customers can use it reliably."
- No beta customer has agreed to be a named reference.
- Enablement materials reference capabilities still marked "coming soon."

**Recovery playbook:** If already shipped prematurely, do not attempt a second press push. Fix the product gaps over four to six weeks, then re-enable sales with updated collateral in a focused 30-minute session. For future launches, require three readiness gates before any date is locked: product readiness (works for at least five beta users), enablement readiness (sales can demo without caveats), and evidence readiness (one named reference exists). Timeline: four to six weeks to fix; the gates add two to three weeks of lead time.

**Example:** The Pre-mortem entry documents Harborcode, a fictional developer-tools company, which nearly launched a pricing change before a pre-mortem surfaced that the billing system could not handle real-world volume. The exercise caught the timing gap before launch; without it, the company would have shipped a pricing model that failed under load.

## Competitive Strategy: Differentiating on Features Competitors Can Copy

**The mistake:** Building the competitive strategy around a feature differentiator a well-resourced competitor can replicate within one or two product cycles.

**How it compounds:** In month one, the differentiator is unique and win rates improve. By month four, a competitor announces a similar capability; prospects say "they have that too now." By month six, the differentiator is neutralised but the entire messaging still leads with it. The team is back to competing on price, with six months of opportunity cost spent on a strategy that did not hold.

**Early warning signs:**
- The differentiator is a single feature, not a compound advantage from multiple reinforcing capabilities.
- The honest answer to "how long could a well-funded competitor match this?" is "one to two quarters."
- MUD scoring shows high Meaningful and Unique but low Defensible, and nobody has addressed the gap.
- Competitive intelligence shows a rival hiring for the capability your differentiator depends on.

**Recovery playbook:** Treat any differentiator scoring low on Defensibility as temporary by design. Build a second, harder-to-copy advantage in parallel: proprietary data, deep workflow integration creating switching costs, or a brand position that takes years to build. Run the "stress test against imitation" (step 8 of the Product Differentiation Strategy Framework) before committing messaging. Timeline: one session for the stress test; a quarter minimum for a defensible second differentiator.

**Example:** The Product Differentiation Strategy entry documents a B2B SaaS company perceived as "mid-value at mid-price, indistinguishable from three close competitors" across 30 win/loss interviews. Recovery involved adding SOC 2 Type II certification and deep integrations (Salesforce, SAP, Workday), both of which take quarters for a competitor to replicate. Enterprise win rate improved from 24% to 34% over two quarters.

## Competitive Strategy: Ignoring Price-Value Alignment

**The mistake:** Pricing above the value customers actually perceive, either because perceived value has never been measured or because internal cost pressures pushed price up without a corresponding increase in what customers experience.

**How it compounds:** By month three, win/loss data shows a pattern: prospects liked the product but chose a cheaper alternative because they could not see enough difference to justify the premium. By month six, the sales team discounts heavily to close deals, eroding margin without fixing the perception gap. The company is stuck in Bowman's Strategic Clock positions six to eight, the zone the framework flags as indefensible.

**Early warning signs:**
- Discounting frequency and depth rise quarter over quarter.
- "Too expensive" is the top loss reason in win/loss interviews, but the list price has not changed; perceived value relative to alternatives has.
- A competitor has recently lowered its price or launched a lower-tier alternative, and your win rate against them has dropped.

**Recovery playbook:** Run a fresh perceived-value assessment using win/loss interviews. Plot your product and two to three competitors on Bowman's Strategic Clock. If in positions six to eight, choose one move: increase perceived value by investing in the attributes customers say they value most, or realign price to match current perception. Do not attempt both at once. Timeline: two to four weeks for assessment; one quarter to execute.

**Example:** The Bowman's Strategic Clock entry documents a smartphone maker in positions six to eight due to bloated design and battery issues. Research across 45 lost deals showed buyers valued battery life and durability above the extra features accumulated over three generations. The company cut 40% of rarely used features, reduced bill of materials by 18%, and repositioned. Win rate improved from 22% to 34% within six months.

## Competitive Strategy: Battlecard Theatre

**The mistake:** Building competitive battlecards that look thorough but are never used in live deals, because they are too long, too generic, or too hard to find mid-call.

**How it compounds:** By month two, reps have opened the battlecards once, found them too dense, and reverted to improvising. By month four, PMM reports high "creation" metrics while win/loss shows reps cannot articulate the differentiator against top competitors. The CI function produces artefacts without influencing deals.

**Early warning signs:**
- Battlecard open rates drop sharply after the first week.
- Reps describe competitive responses in pipeline reviews that do not match the battlecard.
- Win/loss shows deals lost to a competitor where the rep did not use available positioning, not from disagreement but because they could not find it mid-conversation.

**Recovery playbook:** Start from rep behaviour. Interview five reps: "When a competitor comes up on a call, what do you actually do?" Build the next battlecard around that workflow. If reps look things up on their phone, the card must be scannable in under 30 seconds. Require one metric: percentage of competitive deals using the battlecard, tracked via a CRM field. Set a 50% usage target within one quarter; anything below is a distribution problem, not a content problem. Timeline: two weeks to interview and redesign; one quarter to measure adoption.

**Example:** The CI Program Maturity Model entry describes Vantark, a fictional data-infrastructure company, where 9 of 11 reps said they had no reliable way to find competitor information despite a Slack channel of screenshots. The fix was consolidating intel into a structured database distributed through the CRM. Usage in competitive deals rose from 15% to 68%, and win rate against the top two competitors improved from 29% to 38%.
