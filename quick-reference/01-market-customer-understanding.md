## Segmentation–Targeting–Positioning (STP) Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** STP is a three-step sequence: split the market into distinct customer groups (Segmentation), pick the best groups to pursue (Targeting), then define how your product should be perceived by those groups (Positioning). It is foundational; every later messaging, pricing, and go-to-market decision inherits gaps left by a weak STP pass. You cannot position credibly until you know who you are speaking to.

**When to use it:**
- Launching into a new market or vertical
- Sales cycles lengthening or win rates falling
- Product expansion changes your addressable market
- A new competitor reshapes the category
- As a standing quarterly competitive and portfolio review item

**Key steps:**
1. Segment the market using firmographic plus behavioural data; narrow 6-8 candidates to 4-6 distinct groups.
2. Score segments on size, growth, competitive intensity, and fit; pick one or two primary segments.
3. Draft a positioning statement per segment (target, need, category, benefit, differentiator).
4. Validate with 10-15 customer interviews (mix of wins, losses, prospects).
5. Align product, pricing, messaging and sales process to the chosen segment.
6. Monitor win rate, sales cycle, CAC, and NPS quarterly; revisit STP when the market shifts.

**Apply it:** Sketch a three-column worksheet: Segment | Targeting score (size, growth, competitive intensity, fit) | Positioning statement. Add a row per candidate segment and rank by weighted score.

**Success metrics:**
- Sales cycle length by segment (e.g. reduced by 30%)
- CAC and CAC payback period (e.g. improved from 14 to 9 months)
- Trial-to-paid conversion rate
- Segment-level NPS

## Complete Product Experience Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** Positioning is not just what marketing says; it is what a customer actually experiences across seven touchpoints: Marketing, Sales, Product/Features, Technology, Supporting Systems, Third-party Integrations, and Support. A claim repeated in every advert still fails if the product, sales process, or support queue contradicts it. The framework is a diagnostic tool for finding exactly where "what we say" and "what customers feel" have drifted apart.

**When to use it:**
- Sales claims don't match the real onboarding or product experience
- Marketing positioning contradicts what support or product delivers
- NPS verbatims repeatedly contradict homepage messaging
- Quarterly coherence check, or immediately after any repositioning
- Churn rises without an obvious single cause

**Key steps:**
1. Agree the one-sentence positioning statement and three core benefits as the baseline.
2. Map each of the seven touchpoints against that baseline, using evidence not opinion.
3. Flag gaps with specific evidence (a quote, a delay, a recurring feature request).
4. Prioritise fixes by impact versus effort, usually Sales, Product, or Support first.
5. Iterate: refresh the audit quarterly as fixing one area surfaces gaps elsewhere.

**Apply it:** Draw a seven-row table, one row per touchpoint, with columns for "Positioning claim", "Current reality (evidence)", "Gap", and "Fix priority (impact x effort)".

**Success metrics:**
- NPS movement (e.g. up 18 points)
- Churn rate change (e.g. down 5 percentage points)
- Adoption of the feature/claim in question (e.g. mobile usage reaching 34% of sessions)
- Support response time against promised SLA

## Product Development Stage Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A five-phase playbook, ideation, prototype, beta, launch, and maturity, that defines what product marketing should be doing at each stage of building a product. PMM's input is most valuable early, when direction can still change cheaply, and least valuable at launch, once the expensive decisions are locked in. The framework stops PMM from joining only at launch, when it is too late to influence anything important.

**When to use it:**
- Launching a new product, major feature, or entering a new market
- From day one of any new initiative, not after the build
- As a diagnostic: if you can't name the current phase or PMM's expected output, the initiative is under-resourced on marketing

**Key steps:**
1. Ideation & research (weeks 1-4): validate the problem, urgency, and competitive landscape; produce a positioning hypothesis.
2. Prototype & validation (weeks 5-12): test with 15-20 target customers for problem-solution fit; refine positioning and ICP.
3. Beta & messaging (weeks 13-20): recruit 5-10 beta customers, draft full positioning and messages, build sales enablement.
4. Launch (week 21+): coordinate go-to-market across channels, build full collateral, run sales training.
5. Maturity & optimisation (month 4+): monitor win/loss signal, iterate messaging, expand to adjacent segments.

**Apply it:** Build a five-row table, one per phase, with columns for "Weeks", "PMM activities", and "Output", and fill in as the initiative progresses so gaps in ownership are visible early.

**Success metrics:**
- Number of trials and paid customers versus target (e.g. 8 paid against a target of 5)
- ARR and customer count at a set milestone
- Trial-to-paid conversion rate
- Monthly logo churn and time-to-first-value

## Voice of the Customer (VoC) Programme

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A continuous listening system that pulls customer signal from every existing source (surveys, interviews, reviews, support tickets, sales calls, win/loss, churn reasons, product usage) and routes it into positioning, roadmap, and messaging decisions. It is the ongoing system that connects point-in-time inputs like NPS and Win/Loss, so decisions are based on patterns across many customers rather than the loudest recent voice.

**When to use it:**
- Customer insight is anecdotal and different teams quote different versions of "the customer"
- The organisation is scaling past founder intuition
- Churn is rising with no clear single cause
- Signal (tickets, reviews, calls) exists but nothing turns it into decisions
- Ahead of a repositioning or roadmap reprioritisation

**Key steps:**
1. List the decisions VoC must inform (positioning, roadmap, retention).
2. Inventory sources and what each is good for.
3. Tag all signal against one shared theme taxonomy in a single repository.
4. Synthesise into themes, sized by frequency, severity, segment, and revenue.
5. Route each priority theme to an owner with a clear next step.
6. Close the loop by telling customers what changed.
7. Measure whether decisions and outcomes actually moved.

**Apply it:** Sketch a tracker with columns: Theme | Source(s) | Frequency | Severity | Segment | Revenue at stake | Owner | Status ("you said, we did"). One row per theme, updated on a weekly triage.

**Success metrics:**
- Share of feedback tied to an actioned theme (frequency in verbatims, e.g. 34%)
- Churn rate change (e.g. down from 2.4% to 1.6%)
- NPS movement (e.g. 28 to 41)
- Time from issue raised to appearing in a theme report (e.g. under two weeks)

## JTBD Switch Interview Method

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A structured 45-60 minute interview that reconstructs the timeline of a customer's switch from an old solution to a new one, working backwards from the purchase moment to find the specific triggering event, not a vague dissatisfaction, that started the search. It is the research method for finding the "job" a customer hired your product for, feeding the Jobs-to-be-Done Positioning Framework rather than replacing it.

**When to use it:**
- Running the "test with customers" validation step of the JTBD Positioning Framework
- Win/loss interviews keep surfacing generic reasons like "better fit"
- A Voice of the Customer programme needs deeper qualitative input
- Building positioning for a new category or unfamiliar segment
- Sales closes deals for reasons marketing cannot explain

**Key steps:**
1. Recruit buyers who switched in the last one to three months, not long-tenured customers.
2. Start at the purchase moment and work backwards event by event.
3. Find the specific "first thought" trigger event that started the switch.
4. Map the four forces: push, pull, anxiety, and habit/attachment.
5. Ask about everyone involved in the decision, not just the buyer.
6. Capture verbatim quotes, especially for the trigger and the anxiety.
7. Synthesise across 8-12 interviews before generalising into a positioning hypothesis.

**Apply it:** Sketch a timeline worksheet per interview: a horizontal line from "first thought" to "purchase", with push/pull/anxiety/habit noted at each key point, plus a verbatim quote box for the trigger event.

**Success metrics:**
- Number of interviews showing the same trigger/anxiety (e.g. 8 of 10)
- Demo-to-trial conversion rate change (e.g. 22% to 34%)
- Consistency of the trigger story surfaced in later sales discovery calls

## ICP Development Methodology

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A repeatable practice for building an Ideal Customer Profile: a scored, evidence-based model of which accounts are worth pursuing, built from firmographic, technographic, and behavioural data. Where STP's Targeting step chooses the strategic segment, ICP development is the operational layer that turns that choice into a scoring model reps and marketing automation can apply account by account.

**When to use it:**
- Sales prospects broadly with no consistent qualification standard
- Marketing and sales disagree on what a "good" lead looks like
- Launching account-based marketing or outbound prospecting
- Win rate or deal size varies with no obvious pattern
- The customer base has drifted since the ICP was last defined

**Key steps:**
1. Start from the segment STP already chose; don't re-segment from scratch.
2. Pull 12-24 months of closed-won and closed-lost CRM data (50-100+ accounts).
3. Compare wins against losses to isolate the firmographic, technographic, and behavioural attributes that predict a win.
4. Build a weighted scoring model and group scores into fit tiers (e.g. A/B/C).
5. Validate the model against a holdout set of recent closed deals.
6. Push the score into the CRM as a live field on every account.
7. Brief sales and marketing on what to do differently for each tier.

**Apply it:** Build a simple scorecard table: Attribute | Win rate with it | Win rate without it | Weight assigned. Below it, list the resulting tiers (A/B/C) with the score range and the action for each.

**Success metrics:**
- Close rate uplift by tier versus blended baseline (e.g. 4% to 9%)
- Win rate gap between top and bottom tier (e.g. 24% versus 2%)
- Sales cycle length reduction for top-tier accounts (e.g. 35% shorter)

## Sean Ellis 40% Test (PMF Survey)

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A single-question survey ("How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?") that gives an early, standardised read on product-market fit (PMF, meaning the product genuinely satisfies strong market demand). Crossing roughly 40% "Very disappointed" signals the product can sustain aggressive growth spend; below it, growth spend is likely wasted. Product management owns the survey and the resulting decision; PMM's role is segment design and translating results into messaging.

**When to use it:**
- Product leadership is deciding whether to shift from finding fit to scaling growth spend
- Usage looks reasonable but growth spend isn't compounding
- Customers describe the product as merely "convenient", not essential
- Before committing budget to a growth-stage scaling plan
- Just after a repositioning or new-segment launch

**Key steps:**
1. Recruit only active users (used a core feature within the last two weeks).
2. Send the single question with four fixed answers, an open "why" follow-up, and a segment field.
3. Collect at least 30-40 responses per segment before concluding anything.
4. Calculate the Very-disappointed percentage overall and by segment.
5. Read the open-ended responses from the Very-disappointed group for messaging language.
6. Hand the segmented result and quotes to product, who owns the go/no-go call.
7. Re-run every two quarters, or off-cycle after a repositioning or major release.

**Apply it:** Sketch a simple results table: Segment | Respondents | % Very disappointed | Top verbatim theme | Messaging action. Highlight any segment above the 40% line.

**Success metrics:**
- Very-disappointed percentage overall and by segment (e.g. 34% blended, 51% in one segment)
- Trial-to-paid conversion change in the strong-fit segment (e.g. 11% to 17%)
- Consistency of the score on re-run (e.g. held at 54%)
