## Quarterly PMM Planning Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A structured way to decide what PMM works on each quarter, connecting company-wide goals (such as revenue growth or a new market entry) to specific PMM deliverables, owners, and measurable outcomes. It stops the team from spreading effort thin across too many initiatives and makes clear, up front, what is being deliberately left undone.

**When to use it:**
- Every quarter, before the quarter begins
- When business goals shift materially (new leadership, new product line, strategy pivot)
- To align the team on priorities and prevent scope creep
- To make explicit what is being deprioritised, not just what is being done

**Key steps:**
1. Confirm the quarter's business goals directly with leadership.
2. Translate each business goal into supporting PMM work.
3. Set two to three measurable OKRs per goal, each with an owner and deadline.
4. Estimate available people-weeks, with a 10 to 15% buffer for reactive work.
5. Stack-rank initiatives by impact multiplied by confidence; cut what falls below the line.
6. Map cross-functional handoffs and dependencies before the quarter starts.
7. Run weekly standups and a quarterly retrospective to capture what was learned.

**Apply it:** Sketch a single page with four columns: Business goal, PMM initiative, OKR/success metric, Owner and people-weeks. Add a short "not doing this quarter" list beneath it so deprioritised work is visible, not silently dropped.

**Success metrics:**
- At least 80% of OKRs green or amber by week 6
- Under 15% of team capacity absorbed by unplanned, reactive work
- Zero shipped initiatives without a documented business goal attached
- Weekly tracked completion percentage per initiative

## Competitive Intelligence & Positioning Update Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** An ongoing discipline, not a one-off project, for tracking competitor moves and updating positioning and messaging in response. It runs on three cadences: a light monthly scan, a substantive quarterly deep dive, and an annual strategic review of whether the whole competitive landscape has shifted.

**When to use it:**
- Monthly, as routine monitoring of competitor pricing, features, and messaging
- Quarterly, for deeper analysis and positioning review
- Whenever a competitor makes a significant move (funding, acquisition, launch, aggressive pricing)
- Before sales kick-offs, product launches, and pricing discussions

**Key steps:**
1. Define and tier the competitive set (Tier 1 direct, Tier 2 adjacent), revisited every two quarters.
2. Run a monthly scan across five sources: website/pricing, press/hiring signals, reviews, social/analyst commentary, and sales intelligence.
3. Run a quarterly deep dive: win/loss analysis by competitor, updated pricing and feature matrix, 4 to 6 customer interviews, and a fresh positioning-gap review.
4. Update and validate positioning with real customer conversations before rolling it out.
5. Brief sales, product, and executives within a week, and refresh battlecards.
6. Hold a standalone annual review of whether the category itself has shifted.

**Apply it:** Keep a one-page tracker with a row per competitor showing: Tier, last scan date, key recent move, "so what" for our positioning, and any playbook update triggered. Refresh it monthly; annotate it more fully at each quarterly deep dive.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate by named competitor, tracked quarter over quarter
- Percentage of deals where competitive battlecards were actually used (from CRM notes)
- Time-to-brief-sales after a material competitor move (target: within 5 business days)
- Win rate against top two competitors holding or improving as the market grows

## PMM Team Scaling Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A model for growing PMM from a single generalist into a specialised team as the business scales, without adding headcount blindly. It forces clarity on which gap a new hire fixes, what that hire owns, and how their success will be measured, so growth adds capability instead of confusion.

**When to use it:**
- When a team of one is struggling to keep up with demand
- When deciding which specialised role to hire first
- Quarterly, when reviewing team capacity and workload
- Whenever the business scales into new products, segments, or geographies

**Key steps:**
1. Audit the current state: what is covered well, what has no clear owner, and who is overloaded.
2. Define the future state needed over the next 12 to 18 months, anchored to the company's growth plan.
3. Prioritise the most painful gap by business impact (commonly: enablement, then content, then competitive intelligence, then segment-specific PMM).
4. Write the new role's scope before posting the job, distinguishing it clearly from existing roles.
5. Document the core playbook so new hires can operate independently within a month.
6. Set 30/60/90-day success metrics before day one.
7. Review the whole team structure quarterly as the business keeps changing.

**Apply it:** Sketch a one-page team map with rows for each current and planned role, and columns for Scope, Owner, Key metric, and Gap severity (high/medium/low). Use the gap-severity column to decide the next hire.

**Success metrics:**
- New hires hit their defined 90-day metrics within the expected ramp window
- Team size roughly tracks revenue (example benchmark: one PMM per $5M ARR)
- Cross-functional satisfaction (sales, product, customer success) holds or improves as headcount grows
- Voluntary attrition on the PMM team stays low (example target: under 15% annually)

## Analyst Relations Tiering & Cadence Model

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A way of sorting the industry analysts covering your category, such as Gartner, Forrester, and IDC, plus boutique analysts, into three tiers by their influence on buyer shortlists, then running a minimum contact cadence per tier all year round. It replaces the common pattern of contacting analysts only once, right before a major report deadline, which reads as transactional and rarely shifts an existing placement.

**When to use it:**
- Analysts are only contacted reactively, right before a report submission deadline
- The company is preparing for its first inclusion in a major analyst report
- Leadership asks why the company is not on a Magic Quadrant or Wave, with no programme to answer
- Competitive deals are being lost partly on analyst-report citations
- Quarterly planning, to allocate limited briefing capacity across a growing analyst list

**Key steps:**
1. Build the full analyst universe from report citations, conference lists, and firm directories.
2. Score each analyst on shortlist influence, coverage depth, and reciprocity, then tier as 1, 2, or 3.
3. Set a minimum cadence per tier (for example: Tier 1 quarterly plus ad hoc, Tier 2 semi-annual, Tier 3 annual).
4. Prepare a standing briefing kit: roadmap update, proof points, competitive summary.
5. Run briefings and log takeaways, routing them to competitive intelligence and product.
6. Track every published mention, its sentiment, and quadrant or wave placement.
7. Re-tier quarterly, treating new report cycles or beat changes as automatic triggers.

**Apply it:** Build a single-page grid: one row per analyst, columns for Tier, Firm, Last briefing date, Next due date, and Key takeaway. Colour Tier 1 rows to make cadence gaps visible at a glance.

**Success metrics:**
- Calendar time spent per tier roughly tracks the tiering weighting
- Movement in named placement (example: Niche Player to Visionary) against a stated goal per report cycle
- Analyst citations raised unprompted by prospects in competitive deals (example: cited in 4 of 6 RFPs)
- Certification/briefing cadence hit consistently, not only before deadlines

## Pragmatic Institute Framework

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** Pragmatic Institute maps the full product lifecycle as a grid of roughly 37 activities. This entry uses only two column-groups that fill a genuine gap elsewhere in the knowledge base: Enablement (training curricula, certification, and channel/partner roll-out) and Business (ongoing commercial health checks such as revenue-versus-business-case and pricing health, run independently of any active pricing project). The remaining boxes duplicate frameworks already covered elsewhere and are deliberately excluded.

**When to use it:**
- Sales or channel partners have had initial training but no certification or ongoing curriculum
- Enablement is being handed to an indirect channel (resellers, systems integrators) for the first time
- Leadership asks whether a product is still commercially healthy 12 to 18 months after launch
- A pricing change or competitor move may have shifted a product's health, with no standing review to catch it
- Building or refreshing a PMM operating model and needing a checklist just for enablement and business health

**Key steps (how to read and apply it):**
1. Read Enablement boxes on a maturity axis: curriculum exists, completion is certified, channel-specific content exists, content refreshes on a cadence.
2. Read Business boxes on a commercial-health axis: revenue/margin tracked against the original business case, pricing health reviewed on a schedule, win/loss patterns reviewed at product level.
3. Score every box Yes, Partial, or No with one line of concrete evidence.
4. Prioritise Nos and Partials by revenue risk.
5. Build the specific missing layer for each Enablement gap (certification quiz, channel onboarding path, refresh cadence).
6. Stand up the specific missing check for each Business gap (quarterly revenue review, standing pricing health review).
7. Assign an owner and cadence to every box, including ones that already scored Yes, and report the scorecard inside existing Quarterly PMM Planning.

**Apply it:** Draw a simple scorecard with two blocks, Enablement and Business, each listing its component boxes with a Yes/Partial/No column, one line of evidence, an owner, and a next review date.

**Success metrics:**
- Certification completion rate across direct and channel reps (example: 92%)
- Recertification held to a fixed cadence (example: every two quarters)
- Standing pricing health review run on schedule regardless of active projects
- Win rate recovered following a caught pricing or margin gap (example: three points against price-cutting competitors)
