## PMM Lifecycle Management Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** This methodology defines what product marketing should deliver at every stage a customer passes through: awareness, consideration, purchase, onboarding, adoption, expansion, and advocacy. It exists because PMM is often reduced to "launches and messaging," which understates the role. Each stage needs its own touchpoints, owners, and content, not just a burst of activity around the sale.

**When to use it:**
- To scope PMM responsibilities and prioritise work across the customer journey
- When deciding which touchpoints PMM owns versus sales, support, or customer success
- During quarterly planning to allocate budget across lifecycle stages
- To spot messaging gaps, such as strong launch content but no expansion content
- When leadership questions PMM's impact beyond launches

**Key steps:**
1. Map your actual customer journey stages by interviewing sales and customer success.
2. List at least three touchpoints per stage where customers interact with your brand.
3. Assign a specific PMM deliverable to each touchpoint.
4. Build a RACI to clarify ownership and handoffs with other teams.
5. Audit content per stage to identify gaps or under-resourced stages.
6. Prioritise the highest-impact stages using your own funnel data.
7. Set and review a success metric per stage monthly.
8. Re-run the whole mapping exercise every quarter.

**Apply it:** Sketch a single-page table with a row per lifecycle stage and columns for touchpoints, PMM deliverable, owner (RACI), asset count, and the stage's success metric; fill it in from current reality, then mark gaps.

**Success metrics:**
- Asset count per stage (flag any stage with fewer than five assets)
- Stage metrics: organic traffic, demo requests, win rate, time-to-first-value, feature adoption rate, upsell attach rate, NPS
- A measurable lift in at least one rebalanced stage within two quarters (example: upsell attach rate rose from 12% to 19% in six months)

## Go-to-Market Motion Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** This methodology coordinates a launch or major campaign as a single choreographed sequence across channels, messaging, content, partnerships, events, sales enablement, and customer communication, rather than a single announcement date. It treats sequencing and accountability as the main determinants of launch success, since most launches fail from poor coordination rather than a weak idea.

**When to use it:**
- For any product launch, major feature release, or category creation
- When entering a new market or segment
- When multiple teams need to execute in sync
- When a previous launch underperformed and a repeatable process is needed
- When the launch has hard external dependencies, such as an embargo or partner announcement

**Key steps:**
1. Define and test the core launch narrative with three to five customers.
2. Plan pre-launch (4-8 weeks), launch week, and post-launch (4-12 weeks) phases.
3. Assign a named owner to each channel: website, email, PR, social, sales enablement, and more.
4. Build a dependency map showing what must happen before what.
5. Set sync points: daily standups pre-launch, weekly post-launch.
6. Agree a rollback plan for slipped dependencies.
7. Measure engagement, SQL volume, win rate, and revenue attribution for at least 90 days.

**Apply it:** Sketch a one-page Gantt-style grid with rows for each channel owner and columns for pre-launch, launch week, and post-launch weeks 1-12, marking dependencies with arrows and sync points as milestones.

**Success metrics:**
- Launch-week traffic lift, email open rate, and SQL volume in launch week
- Feature or offer adoption rate at 60 days (target: at least 40% of the eligible base)
- Commercial impact within the following quarter: win rate, average selling price, or sales cycle time (example: 18% faster cycle time and 11% higher ASP)

## Feature Adoption Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** This methodology applies product marketing discipline, segmentation, messaging, and measurement, to driving adoption of features customers already have access to but don't use. It treats an in-app feature the same as an external product launch, because building a feature is not the same as customers discovering and using it.

**When to use it:**
- After launching a new feature, to ensure customers discover and use it
- For features with low adoption despite strong product-market fit
- To accelerate adoption of features that enable upsell
- When customers use a workaround instead of a built-in feature
- Quarterly, or whenever new features ship

**Key steps:**
1. Interview the product manager and at least five non-adopters to find the real adoption barrier.
2. Segment users by persona, company size, or use case using actual usage data.
3. Build a campaign combining in-app messaging, targeted email, matched enablement content, and high-touch outreach for key accounts.
4. Agree a realistic adoption target before launching the campaign.
5. Measure activation rate, 30/90-day retention, and business impact.
6. Iterate on messaging or simplify the feature if adoption stays low.
7. Share adoption wins internally and externally.

**Apply it:** Sketch a one-page grid with rows for each user segment and columns for barrier, channel (in-app, email, content, outreach), message, and target adoption rate, to plan a differentiated campaign at a glance.

**Success metrics:**
- Activation rate against the pre-agreed target (example: 25% at 60 days)
- 90-day retention of activated users (target: above 70%)
- Downstream business impact: upsell rate, NPS, or churn movement (example: feature referenced in 30% of upsell conversations)
