## 3-Step Product Marketing Strategy Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A stripped-down approach for teams too small to specialise: understand the customer's problem, articulate the solution's unique value, then execute one go-to-market channel well. It trades comprehensiveness for speed, on the view that a narrow, well-executed plan beats a broad, half-finished one. One person or a small pod owns the whole motion end to end.

**When to use it:**
- Early-stage SaaS, typically pre-Series A
- Resetting messaging after a product pivot
- Team too small to split across content, demand generation, and enablement
- Research shows one dominant customer pain point to own
- Poor fit once you have more than one validated segment or channel

**Key steps:**
1. Define the problem: 10-15 customer interviews, distil the core pain into one sentence.
2. Build positioning: one-sentence statement mapping the pain to your unique capability; test on five outsiders.
3. Execute one channel: pick the strongest lever, master it, track one leading metric weekly before adding a second channel.

**Apply it:** Sketch a single page with three boxes: "The pain (one sentence, in customer words)", "Our positioning statement", and "This week's channel metric". Update the last box weekly.

**Success metrics:**
- Interview-to-positioning turnaround under 30 days
- 70%+ of test audience can restate the value proposition unprompted
- Channel conversion rate versus industry benchmark
- Minimum sample size before judging a channel (100+ prospects for outbound, 4-6 weeks for inbound)

**Source:** [The 3-Step Product Marketing Strategy Framework](https://www.courageous-careers.com/the-3-step-product-marketing-strategy-framework)

## 7-Step Product Marketing Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A funnel-based sequence that assigns specific PMM work to each stage of the buyer's journey, from market research through customer advocacy. It is more structured than the 3-Step framework but doesn't require dedicated specialists per function; each step depends on the output of the one before it, so skipping ahead causes rework.

**When to use it:**
- Growth-stage companies, roughly $2M-$15M ARR, with a dedicated PMM or small team
- Conversion rates plateau or sales cycles lengthen under single-segment messaging
- Clarifying handoffs between marketing, sales, and customer success
- A sensible framework to introduce right after a first PMM hire

**Key steps:**
1. Market research and segmentation: profile the ICP, validate with 15-20 conversations.
2. Positioning and messaging: distinct value propositions per segment.
3. Content and education: awareness and consideration-stage assets, sequenced after positioning sign-off.
4. Sales enablement: one-pagers and objection playbooks, reviewed by front-line reps.
5. Demand generation: brief targeting and messaging, lock hand-off SLAs.
6. Sales-marketing alignment: weekly sync on pipeline and messaging, iterate from win/loss data.
7. Customer advocacy: document wins, build case studies, seed referrals.

**Apply it:** Draw the funnel as a seven-box horizontal chain on one page; under each box note the owner and the one artefact that must exist before the next box can start.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate by segment (example: 15% blended to 32% for lead segment)
- Sales cycle length trend
- Collateral usage rate (below 50% signals a mismatch with what reps hear)
- Lead response time against the agreed SLA

**Source:** [monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/marketing/product-marketing-strategy/)

## 10-Step PMM Process

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A detailed, operational workflow for larger PMM teams with specialised roles, spanning positioning, messaging, enablement, and measurement, with explicit checkpoints so no single step becomes a bottleneck. Unlike the 7-Step framework, which assumes one generalist, this assumes a team with clear ownership boundaries and formal reporting, built to hold up across multiple product lines or regions.

**When to use it:**
- Series B+ companies, roughly $15M-$50M ARR, with two or more dedicated PMMs
- Need consistency across regions, product lines, or concurrent campaigns
- Onboarding new PMMs, since the ten steps double as a structured checklist
- Generally overkill below $15M ARR

**Key steps:**
1. Market sizing and segmentation (TAM/SAM/SOM, detailed ICPs).
2. Buyer journey mapping across Awareness through Advocacy.
3. Win/loss analysis: 20-30 interviews annually.
4. Positioning statement development, tested with sales and leadership.
5. Message architecture: core claim, benefits, proof points, proof assets.
6. Collateral and channel strategy, with owners and a refresh cadence.
7. Sales enablement: one-pagers, battle cards, objection library, usage tracked.
8. Campaign brief and creative direction, signed off before production.
9. Performance measurement: win rate, conversion, collateral feedback, reported monthly.
10. Iteration and refresh: a formal 90-day review of win/loss, competition, and product changes.

**Apply it:** Build a one-page ten-row table with columns for step, owner, cadence, and current status; use it as the standing agenda for the quarterly iteration checkpoint.

**Success metrics:**
- Cycle time from market sizing to a live channel strategy, shrinking over time
- Percentage of deals using current-quarter enablement material (below 60% is a warning sign)
- At least four iteration cycles completed per year
- Win rate and CAC payback by segment

**Source:** [Product Marketing Alliance](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/product-marketing-framework/)

## Complete GTM Workflow Stages

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** An end-to-end roadmap sequencing GTM activity across five calendar-based phases: Foundation, Launch, Scale, Optimise, and Expand. Each phase has explicit entry and exit criteria, timeline, ownership, and deliverables, preventing common mistakes like scaling demand before positioning is validated. Because it is organised by time, it also supports headcount and budget planning alongside the GTM motion.

**When to use it:**
- Founder-led companies preparing to hire their first marketer
- Established companies launching into new markets or product lines
- High GTM risk (new category or unfamiliar segment) needing checkpoints before heavy investment
- Rebuilding GTM after a pivot or major organisational change
- Board or investor reporting, since each phase has a clean exit criterion

**Key steps:**
1. Foundation (weeks 1-8): validate problem fit via 20+ interviews, document ICP, draft a positioning hypothesis.
2. Launch (weeks 9-16): build minimal assets, validate one channel on 50+ prospects, set up basic sales-marketing SLAs.
3. Scale (months 5-12): expand the winning channel incrementally, add a second channel only once CAC payback is under 18 months.
4. Optimise (months 13-18): win/loss analysis on 30+ deals, conversion testing, a third channel or adjacent segment.
5. Expand (months 19+): enter a new segment or geography using the validated playbook, build dedicated PMM roles.

**Apply it:** Sketch a five-column timeline on one page, one column per phase, with the phase's hard-gate success metric written at the bottom of each column; do not fill in the next column's budget until the current one's metric is hit.

**Success metrics:**
- Foundation: 70% of customers describe the same pain unprompted
- Launch: 15%+ conversion from the target segment
- Scale: 3x lead volume versus end of Launch, CAC payback under 18 months, win rate above 20%
- Optimise: CAC payback under 12 months, conversion up 15-20% versus Scale

**Source:** [2024 SaaS Benchmarks Report](https://www.highalpha.com/saas-benchmarks/2024)

## T2D3 Framework (B2B SaaS)

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A revenue-scaling methodology for B2B SaaS: "Triple, Triple, Double, Double, Double", meaning 3x growth in years 1 and 2, then 2x in years 3 through 5. It maps each year's revenue multiple to the operational milestones needed to hit it, on the insight that tripling from $1M looks nothing like doubling from $36M, so last year's playbook cannot simply repeat at a bigger scale.

**When to use it:**
- SaaS companies from pre-Series B through Series C with validated product-market fit
- Defining growth targets backward from fundraising milestones
- Scaling sales and marketing teams and needing a shared definition of "on track"
- Aligning PMM, sales leadership, and investors on growth cadence
- Poor fit before product-market fit is validated

**Key steps:**
1. Year 1 (3x): founding PMM messaging, outbound-only, land 30-50 customers.
2. Year 2 (3x): dedicated PMM hire, inbound plus paid channels, scale to 80-120 customers.
3. Year 3 (2x): PMM specialisation, competitive battle cards, begin regional expansion.
4. Year 4 (2x): VP of Marketing hire, segment-specific PMMs, mature sales operations.
5. Year 5 (2x): CMO-level hire, multi-product strategy, international expansion.

**Apply it:** Draw a five-year table with rows for ARR target, required PMM hire, and marketing/sales focus; fill it in as a planning exercise, then track actuals against it quarterly.

**Success metrics:**
- ARR growth landing within roughly 20% of that year's target
- Net revenue retention of 110%+ by Year 3
- CAC payback trending down or flat, not climbing, as the motion matures
- Two consecutive quarters missing target by more than 25% triggers a plan revisit

**Source:** [Neeraj Agrawal](https://techcrunch.com/2015/02/01/the-saas-travel-adventure/)

## GTM Motion Model

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** A taxonomy of the five ways a B2B SaaS company acquires and expands customers: self-serve, product-led growth (PLG), inside sales, field sales, and partner-channel. No company runs only one motion forever; the model helps you name the motion that fits your product and segment today, based on deal size and buyer complexity, rather than defaulting to whatever a leader ran previously.

**When to use it:**
- Before writing any launch or GTM plan, so it fits the motion your segment actually supports
- CAC or sales cycle length looks wrong for the deal size (a motion mismatch, not an execution problem)
- Entering a new segment or geography
- A self-serve or PLG product's average deal size is creeping upward
- Resourcing a new product line aimed at a different buyer

**Key steps:**
1. Plot your last 20-30 closed-won deals: ACV against number of decision-makers.
2. Assess self-serve feasibility: can a user reach real value unaided in 15 minutes?
3. Check for a natural sales-assist trigger for PLG (a usage signal sales can act on within a day or two).
4. Match ACV and buyer complexity to the closest motion (roughly: under $5,000 and one decision-maker points to self-serve/PLG; $5,000-$50,000 with a small group points to inside sales; above that with a committee points to field sales).
5. Decide whether a partner-channel overlay adds reach you cannot recover directly.
6. Pilot the candidate motion on 15-20 real prospects before committing headcount or budget.
7. Revisit at every segment or ACV shift.

**Apply it:** Plot a simple two-axis grid (deal size vertical, buyer complexity horizontal) on one page, mark where your last 20-30 deals actually fall, and circle which of the five motions that cluster matches.

**Success metrics:**
- Conversion from usage-signal to paid upgrade (example: 11% to 24% after adding a sales-assist trigger)
- CAC payback by motion, versus fully loaded cost of a sales team
- Deal cycle length and average contract value matching the assigned motion
- Re-run trigger: average deal size shifting by more than roughly 25% over two consecutive quarters

**Source:** [Dock](https://www.dock.us/library/go-to-market-motions)

## Product-Led Growth (PLG)

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A go-to-market practice where the product itself, not a sales conversation, drives acquisition, conversion, and expansion. A prospect signs up for a free trial or tier, reaches real value unaided, and upgrades because the product has already proven itself. PMM's job is to define which usage events count as "product-qualified" signals and own the in-product activation and upgrade messaging, distinct from product management's job of building the features that create those signals.

**When to use it:**
- The GTM Motion Model places your product in the self-serve or PLG quadrant
- A free trial or freemium tier already exists but converts poorly
- Sales reps are bottlenecked on small deals a self-serve flow could close
- Usage data exists (seat additions, feature-gate hits) but nobody acts on it
- Validating product-market fit for a new self-serve tier

**Key steps:**
1. Define the activation moment using behavioural data, not a time-based proxy.
2. Design the self-serve funnel around that moment; target time-to-activation under 15 minutes.
3. Instrument two to three product-qualified lead (PQL) signals that predict willingness to pay.
4. Build an in-product upgrade prompt tied to the value just experienced.
5. Layer in a sales-assist trigger only for accounts where the signal implies complexity self-serve can't handle.
6. Measure the funnel in stages: signup-to-activation, activation-to-PQL, PQL-to-paid.
7. Iterate activation and upgrade messaging on a fixed cadence.

**Apply it:** Sketch a three-stage funnel on one page (activation, PQL, paid) with the current conversion rate and one improvement idea written under each stage.

**Success metrics:**
- Trial-to-paid conversion rate (example: 4% to 17% after redefining activation)
- Time from signup to first value event
- Average contract value uplift from sales-assisted PQLs versus pure self-serve
- Quarterly re-validation of each PQL signal's predictive strength

**Source:** [Wes Bush](https://productled.com/book/product-led-growth)

## Account-Based Everything (ABX) / TEAM Framework

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A go-to-market methodology for complex B2B sales built around the TEAM structure from Sangram Vajre and Eric Spett's *ABM is B2B.* (2019): Target (select and tier named accounts), Engage (build role-specific messaging for the full buying committee), Activate (route sales effort to accounts showing multi-signal intent), and Measure (track account-level outcomes, not lead volume). It operationalises the field-sales motion the GTM Motion Model identifies for high-ACV, committee-driven deals; the structural mirror of Product-Led Growth on the self-serve side.

**When to use it:**
- The GTM Motion Model selects field sales and the team needs the practice for running it
- The addressable market is a small, named universe of accounts
- Deals stall when a stakeholder nobody engaged raises a late-stage objection
- Marketing and sales run disconnected programmes against the same named accounts
- Success is measured on MQL count when deals come from a small number of named accounts

**Key steps:**
1. Define account-selection criteria combining firmographics, buying signals, and strategic fit.
2. Tier accounts: Tier 1 (one-to-one), Tier 2 (one-to-few), Tier 3 (one-to-many).
3. Map the buying committee for each target account (economic buyer, champion, technical evaluator, end user).
4. Build role-specific content for each committee member, sequenced across the committee.
5. Instrument intent signals at the account level, not just contact level.
6. Activate sales on a multi-signal threshold with full account context.
7. Measure account-level outcomes: pipeline per account, win rate by tier, account penetration.
8. Re-tier accounts on a standing quarterly cadence.

**Apply it:** Build a one-page account dashboard with columns for account name, tier, committee roles engaged, intent signals, and pipeline stage; review weekly for Tier 1 accounts.

**Success metrics:**
- Buying-committee roles engaged before late stage (Ashford Data, a fictional company: 1.4 to 3.2)
- Win rate on Tier 1 accounts (21% to 38% in the example)
- Average deal cycle reduction (roughly three weeks shorter in the example)

**Source:** [Sangram Vajre & Eric Spett](https://www.porchlightbooks.com/products/abm-is-b2b-sangram-vajre-9781940858951)

## Bullseye Framework (Traction Channel Selection)

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A structured method, from Weinberg and Mares' book *Traction*, for finding which single channel actually drives growth. It sorts candidates from a brainstorm of 19 possible channels into three concentric rings: an outer ring of untested but plausible channels, a middle ring of three or four worth a cheap test, and an inner ring of the one channel with the strongest early signal, on which the team then concentrates real budget.

**When to use it:**
- Product-market fit is validated but no repeatable acquisition channel exists yet
- The team is running three or four channels at once with no clear read on what's working
- A previously reliable channel is degrading (rising CAC, falling conversion)
- Entering a new segment or geography where the old channel may not transfer
- Leadership is debating channel choice on opinion rather than data

**Key steps:**
1. Brainstorm across all 19 traction channels, dropping any with no plausible mechanism.
2. Rate outer-ring plausibility on cost, reach, and ICP fit.
3. Promote three or four channels to the middle ring with a capped budget and time-boxed test.
4. Run the middle-ring tests in parallel.
5. Compare results against the pre-agreed metric, not internal enthusiasm.
6. Promote the single strongest channel to the inner ring and commit real budget.
7. Re-run the brainstorm once the inner-ring channel plateaus.

**Apply it:** Draw three concentric circles on one page; list all plausible channels in the outer ring, move three or four into the middle ring with a budget cap and metric next to each, then circle the eventual inner-ring winner.

**Success metrics:**
- Cost per qualified lead, compared across tested channels
- Minimum spend per test sufficient for 20-30 leads or conversions
- Share of new signups or pipeline from the inner-ring channel (example: 58% of trial signups)
- Blended CAC improvement versus the previous multi-channel spread

**Source:** [Gabriel Weinberg & Justin Mares](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/319121/traction-by-gabriel-weinberg-and-justin-mares/)

## Launch Tier Framework (Tier 1/2/3)

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** A classification model that sorts every launch into Tier 1 (major, cross-functional, externally visible), Tier 2 (significant, single-team-led), or Tier 3 (minor, routine), so process and resourcing match a launch's actual scope. Without it, teams either run the full process on a routine update or under-resource a genuinely major launch.

**When to use it:**
- Building a quarterly launch calendar and deciding how much process each launch gets
- Every launch currently gets the same treatment regardless of scope
- Stakeholders question why a minor change needed so much coordination, or a major one felt thin
- Onboarding a new PMM who needs a shared vocabulary for launch scope
- A launch's scope changes materially after initial planning

**Key steps:**
1. Score each launch on four criteria: revenue/strategic impact, cross-functional dependency, external visibility, customer-facing change magnitude.
2. Map the dominant score to a tier and attach its pre-built resourcing template.
3. Communicate the tier to stakeholders early, at the same meeting the launch joins the calendar.
4. Build the quarterly calendar around tier distribution, not just launch count.
5. Re-score any launch whose scope changes materially.
6. Gate tier assignment through one owner using the published criteria.
7. Run a short quarterly retrospective to recalibrate thresholds.

**Apply it:** Make a one-page four-row scorecard (the four criteria) with a high/medium/low column per launch; the dominant rating maps straight to a tier and its resourcing template.

**Success metrics:**
- Sales enablement completion rate for Tier 1 launches (example: 65% to 95%)
- Tier distribution across the quarter (a healthy, fast-shipping org should see Tier 3 as the largest bucket)
- Lead time actually matching the tier (8-12 weeks for Tier 1, 3-4 for Tier 2)
- Quarterly retrospective flags on mis-scored launches, tracked over time

**Source:** [Use Product Launch Tiers to Allocate Launch Resources](https://www.pragmaticinstitute.com/resources/articles/product/prioritize-product-launch-resources-with-launch-tiers/)

## Pre-mortem

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A structured pre-launch exercise, developed by cognitive psychologist Gary Klein and published in the *Harvard Business Review* (2007), in which the team imagines the initiative has already failed completely, then works backwards to generate specific reasons why. Klein's technique exploits "prospective hindsight": people generate more specific explanations for an outcome framed as already having happened than for one framed as merely possible, bypassing the social pressure to defend a plan the group has invested in. Resulting risks are ranked, assigned owners and mitigations, and rolled into a go or no-go recommendation.

**When to use it:**
- Ahead of a Tier 1 launch where failure carries real revenue or reputational cost
- The team is confident and no one has voiced doubts in planning meetings
- A previous launch failed and its postmortem showed the failure mode was foreseeable but never raised
- Launching into unfamiliar territory (new segment, new GTM motion, new geography)

**Key steps:**
1. Assemble the team running the launch, plus genuine sceptics or a designated dissenter.
2. State the premise as fact: "It is [three to six months] from now. This launch has failed completely."
3. Have every participant write failure reasons individually and silently before any discussion.
4. Go around the group, one reason per turn, recording all without debate.
5. Cluster raw reasons into five to ten distinct failure themes.
6. Score each theme on severity and likelihood; rank the list.
7. Assign a named owner and dated mitigation to every top-ranked risk.
8. Decide whether any risk warrants delaying, rescoping, or cancelling the launch.
9. Revisit the risk list at the post-launch review.

**Apply it:** Sketch a one-page risk register with columns for failure theme, severity, likelihood, owner, and mitigation deadline; fill it during the session and check it off at the post-launch review.

**Success metrics:**
- Number of risks surfaced that would not have emerged in a standard planning meeting
- Share of top-ranked risks with a completed mitigation before launch (Harborcode, a fictional company: metering fix shipped pre-launch, zero capacity incidents in first month)
- Churn or incident rate versus pre-fix projections (roughly half the modelled churn in the example)

**Source:** [Gary Klein](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)
