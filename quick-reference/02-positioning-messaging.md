## Geoffrey Moore's Positioning Statement Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A single-paragraph template that forces a team to agree on six things: target customer, their need, product category, key benefit, main competitive alternative, and differentiation. Because it must fit one tightly worded paragraph, it is hard to hide vague thinking or unresolved internal debate behind it. It originated in enterprise tech marketing but is now used across consumer, services, and non-profit positioning too.

**When to use it:**
- Clarifying positioning before building marketing materials
- Aligning product, marketing, sales, and support around one message
- Positioning a new product, market, or segment
- Briefing a new hire, agency, or partner with one reference point
- Resolving a naming or category dispute

**Key steps:**
1. Define a specific target customer, not "everyone".
2. State their need or opportunity in their own words.
3. Name a product category that needs minimal explanation.
4. Identify the key benefit (an outcome, not a feature).
5. Name the primary competitive alternative honestly.
6. Articulate a differentiation a competitor could not credibly claim.
7. Test the statement aloud for clarity with someone outside the company.
8. Pressure-test it against three to five live sales opportunities.

**Apply it:** Sketch one paragraph filling the template: "For [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [differentiation]." Draft it, then have a rep read it against a real deal.

**Success metrics:**
- Win-rate versus named competitors (Hound example: 19% to 27%)
- Sales cycle length (54 to 39 days)
- Discovery-call-to-demo conversion (41% to 58%)

## April Dunford's 5-Component Positioning Canvas

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A working document, not a single sentence, that positions a product on five components: the competitive set (what customers would use instead), your unique attributes, how customers weight those attributes, the market segment that cares most, and the compelling reason to buy now. It pushes teams past feature lists to position on what actually drives buyer decisions, and only compresses into external copy once every component is settled.

**When to use it:**
- Positioning is uncertain or teams disagree on what makes the product unique
- Launching to a new segment or persona
- Testing multiple positioning hypotheses before committing
- A competitor repositions or a new entrant changes the alternative
- After a merger, acquisition, or major product expansion

**Key steps:**
1. List the true competitive set, including status quo and manual workarounds, from customers' own words.
2. Document your unique attributes; keep only those no competitor in the set has.
3. Weight attributes by uniqueness and stated customer importance.
4. Choose the segment that most values that specific attribute mix.
5. Craft the compelling reason to buy now, anchored to a trigger event.
6. Validate the positioning cold with five to eight target customers.
7. Hand the validated canvas to copywriters as a brief, not a script.

**Apply it:** Draw a five-box grid, one box per component, and fill each with evidence from win/loss interviews rather than opinion; score each attribute on uniqueness and importance before choosing the segment box.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate against new entrants (Flowlist: 22% to 41%)
- Average deal size in target segment (+15%)
- Number of target customers whose unprompted description matches intent (validation step)

## Jobs-to-be-Done Positioning Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** Customers do not buy products; they hire them to get a job done. The framework positions around the job (the outcome sought), the context (what triggers the need), and the competing solutions, including doing nothing. Because the underlying job is more stable than a customer's job title or firmographic profile, JTBD positioning tends to age better than persona-based positioning.

**When to use it:**
- The market is fragmented or the product serves multiple use cases
- Existing positioning feels generic or doesn't explain real buyer behaviour
- Launching a new category where traditional frameworks don't fit
- Churn or onboarding data reveals customers using the product for an unplanned purpose

**Key steps:**
1. Identify the core job as an outcome, not a tool (verb plus object plus context).
2. Define the functional, emotional, and social dimensions of the job.
3. Map the context and trigger that makes the job urgent.
4. Identify competing solutions, including manual workarounds and "do nothing".
5. Articulate the switch: push, pull, anxiety, and habit forces.
6. Test the job with customers via "switch interviews".
7. Build a messaging hierarchy: functional job first, emotional/social reinforcing, competitors handled in objection-handling.

**Apply it:** Sketch a single table with rows for functional, emotional, social job, context/trigger, and current (including non-product) solution; fill it from interview notes, not internal assumption.

**Success metrics:**
- Trial-to-paid conversion (Timeframe: 11% to 17%)
- Share of won deals citing the intended job as the reason (24% to 60%)
- Support signal tied to the emotional job (surveillance-related tickets halved)

## Value Proposition Canvas

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** Maps a customer profile (jobs, pains, gains) against a value map (features, pain relievers, gain creators) to check, item by item, whether the product actually addresses what the segment needs. It goes a layer deeper than Jobs-to-be-Done by auditing every pain and gain attached to the job, making it as useful for spotting roadmap gaps as for positioning.

**When to use it:**
- Checking that the roadmap aligns with customer jobs and pains
- Positioning differently for distinct segments (enterprise vs SMB)
- Finding unexplored messaging angles
- Before roadmap prioritisation, to check features map to real pains/gains
- When deals are lost to "good enough" incumbents

**Key steps:**
1. Profile the customer: jobs, pains (by severity), gains (required vs delighters).
2. List features plainly, without adjectives.
3. Map each feature to the pain it relieves and the mechanism.
4. Map each feature to the gain it creates and the mechanism.
5. Score the fit (strong/partial/no fit) for every pain and gain.
6. Cut or deprioritise features that map to nothing; refine messaging around the strongest fits.
7. Validate the canvas with five to eight target customers.

**Apply it:** Draw the classic two-circle canvas: customer profile (jobs/pains/gains) on one side, value map (features/relievers/creators) on the other, with arrows connecting each pain or gain to its matching reliever or creator.

**Success metrics:**
- Fit coverage (e.g. Peoplebase: strong fit on 4/4 pains, 5/5 gains)
- Homepage-to-trial conversion (3.1% to 4.6%)
- Close rate for leads citing the led-with pain (38% vs 24%)

## Message Architecture (Messaging House)

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** Turns a validated positioning statement into a "house": a core message (the roof), three or four value pillars (the walls), and proof points (the foundation). It is the missing link between positioning and campaign copy; without it, every writer and rep reinvents the wording and the market hears several different products instead of one.

**When to use it:**
- Positioning is agreed but every asset phrases it differently
- Marketing, sales, and product tell different stories
- Before a launch or reposition, as the brief for all collateral
- Briefing a new writer, agency, or account executive
- Message testing or win/loss shows buyers can't repeat back the value

**Key steps:**
1. Start from an already-validated positioning statement.
2. Write the core message: one repeatable, outcome-led sentence.
3. Define three or four value pillars that answer "why believe it".
4. Populate two to four proof points under each pillar.
5. Add an audience layer: a persona-by-pillar message matrix.
6. Pressure-test with buyers or sales; cut anything a competitor could also claim.
7. Publish, assign an owner, and review quarterly.

**Apply it:** Draw the house shape: one roof line for the core message, three or four wall columns for pillars, two to four foundation stones under each with the specific proof.

**Success metrics:**
- Demo-request conversion (Sentra: 1.9% to 3.1%)
- Message-test clarity score (54% to 82% "immediately clear")
- New-hire ramp to confident pitch (11 weeks to 6)

## Dunford's 10-Step Positioning Process

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** The step-by-step research and validation sequence that produces the evidence behind April Dunford's 5-Component Canvas. Where the canvas is the output template, this is the process, interviews, competitive research, and validation, that makes the canvas defensible rather than assumption-built.

**When to use it:**
- Positioning for the first time, or the market has moved on
- Teams disagree on differentiation with no evidence behind either side
- You inherited positioning you can't explain
- A merger, pivot, or expansion changed the competitive set or attributes
- Before filling in the 5-Component Canvas, to evidence its inputs

**Key steps:**
1. Interview 8-12 best-fit existing customers.
2. List true competitive alternatives, including "do nothing".
3. Isolate attributes that are genuinely unique versus the alternatives.
4. Map each unique attribute to customer value ("so what?").
5. Determine which segment cares most about that value.
6. Choose the best-fit market category; test at least two candidates.
7. Layer on a real market trend only if genuinely evidenced.
8. Vet the draft cold with five to eight target customers.
9. Write the pitch and the positioning canvas.
10. Align the whole organisation on the reasoning, not just the wording.

**Apply it:** Build a simple ten-row checklist, one row per step, each with a status (evidenced / not yet) and the source of evidence; nothing moves to step 9 until steps 1-8 have real customer data attached.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate against named competitors (Ferrycloud: 21% to 33%)
- Sales cycle length (71 to 52 days)
- Category-recall in cold validation (1/6 to 4/6 customers repeating the frame unprompted)

## Command of the Message

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A value-selling discipline (Force Management) that translates the messaging house into a structure a rep can run live in a deal: the buyer's required capabilities, your differentiators against the specific competitor in play, and the quantified business value of each differentiator in the buyer's financial terms. It is the live-conversation layer, not a second, competing messaging hierarchy.

**When to use it:**
- Reps default to feature tours instead of quantified value
- Deals stall because a champion can't repeat the value case in dollars
- Win/loss shows deals lost on "no differentiation" despite a genuinely differentiated product
- Rolling out a qualification methodology (MEDDIC etc.) that needs a messaging counterpart
- Onboarding a new sales cohort

**Key steps:**
1. Pull the core message and pillars from the existing messaging house.
2. List required capabilities per segment, in buyer language.
3. Map differentiators to the two or three competitors actually faced.
4. Quantify the value of each differentiator with a sourced number.
5. Sequence discovery, demo, and proposal around that value case.
6. Train and role-play the structure with reps against real competitor scenarios.
7. Instrument the CRM to track which value case was used per deal.

**Apply it:** Build a one-page grid: rows are required capabilities, columns are named competitor / your differentiator / quantified value; reps take this into discovery calls.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate against a named incumbent (Vantree: 24% to 39%)
- Close rate when the quantified value figure was logged (51% vs 28%)
- Average deal size in contested deals (+12%)

## Features-Advantages-Benefits (FAB) Ladder

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A three-rung translation technique for turning one raw feature into persuasive language: the feature (what it does, factual), the advantage (what that makes possible), and the benefit (the outcome the buyer experiences, ideally quantified). It operates a level below the messaging house and Command of the Message, as the sentence-by-sentence discipline for any single claim.

**When to use it:**
- A one-pager, release note, or demo script is just a list of capabilities
- Writing proof points for a messaging-house pillar
- A new PMM or rep needs a concrete drill for translating features to outcomes
- Engineering ships a feature and asks "how do we talk about this?"
- Auditing an existing website or deck for feature-heavy language

**Key steps:**
1. Write the feature plainly, with no adjectives.
2. State the advantage as a concrete before/after mechanism.
3. State the benefit, quantified where possible, pushing past the first (still functional) answer.
4. Cross-check the benefit against a real, documented buyer priority.
5. Reserve the full ladder for features that map to a pillar or contested buying criterion.
6. Write the external line benefit-first, advantage second, feature as proof.
7. Pressure-test the line with someone outside the product team.

**Apply it:** Draw a three-column table, Feature / Advantage / Benefit, one row per claim; only publish rows where the benefit column has a sourced number.

**Success metrics:**
- Message-test "clear and compelling" score (Ledgerline: 31% to 68%)
- Feature mention rate in sales calls (12% to 47%)

## StoryBrand SB7 Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A seven-part narrative template (Donald Miller) that makes the customer the hero and the brand the guide who helps them win: Character, Problem (external, internal, philosophical), Guide, Plan, Call to Action, Failure, and Success. People process messaging the way they watch a film, scanning for the hero and the stakes, so this fixes the common mistake of brands writing themselves as the hero.

**When to use it:**
- A homepage reads as a feature list, not a story worth following
- Writing a founder narrative, About page, or company video script
- A demo script lists capabilities in build order rather than buyer-friendly sequence
- Message testing shows poor clarity, not poor differentiation
- Briefing an agency or new hire with a simple narrative document

**Key steps:**
1. Define the Character: one sentence naming what they want.
2. Diagnose the external, internal, and philosophical Problem.
3. Position the brand as Guide, with one empathy line and one authority line.
4. Write the Plan as three or four numbered customer-experienced steps.
5. Write one explicit, visually dominant Call to Action.
6. Name the stakes of Failure specifically and truthfully.
7. Paint a vivid, sensory picture of Success.
8. Assemble all seven parts into a one-page brief for sign-off.

**Apply it:** Sketch a seven-box storyboard, one box per SB7 element in sequence, and fill each with one or two sentences before any copy is drafted.

**Success metrics:**
- Bounce rate (Ledgerly: 68% to 49%)
- Homepage-to-trial conversion (2.1% to 3.8%)
- Share of inbound leads who can describe the product correctly in the first call (30% to 70%)

## Message Testing (Message-Market Fit)

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** A repeatable practice for testing draft messaging against a verified panel of target buyers before it ships, scoring clarity, relevance, and differentiation. Popularised by Wynter, it treats "message-market fit" like product-market fit: messaging can be wrong, and the fix is testing against real buyers rather than internal opinion. Unlike a one-off positioning validation, it is meant to run repeatedly on any new copy.

**When to use it:**
- A major landing page, homepage, or ad campaign is about to ship
- Real budget is about to be committed to untested messaging
- Internal debate over two options has stalled on opinion
- New copy from a messaging house, FAB ladder, or SB7 draft needs proof it lands
- An evergreen, high-traffic asset hasn't been re-tested in over a year

**Key steps:**
1. Recruit a verified panel matched to the real ICP.
2. Draft at least two candidate versions; never test one in isolation.
3. Fix the scoring rubric and pass threshold before running the test.
4. Run with 30-50 verified respondents per version, collecting open-text reasoning.
5. Read qualitative comments before the aggregate score.
6. Ship the winner and log the loser's score and reasoning.
7. Re-test evergreen assets on a quarterly or semi-annual cadence.

**Apply it:** Sketch a simple scorecard: rows for each candidate version, columns for clarity/relevance/differentiation scores plus a notes column for the standout qualitative comments.

**Success metrics:**
- Clarity/compelling score comparison (Ironclad Cyber: 22% vs 61%)
- Campaign click-through rate versus historical average (3.8% vs 1.9%)
- Cost per qualified lead (-34%)
