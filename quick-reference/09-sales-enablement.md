## Sales Enablement Maturity Model

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** This model diagnoses how mature a company's sales enablement function is, across four stages: Ad Hoc (no owner, no shared library), Organised (a named owner and a basic template), Optimised (content tied to the buyer's journey, reps trained and certified, usage measured), and Transformative (a continuously measured system proven to move win rate and sales cycle). It scores the function itself, not any single asset, across four dimensions: ownership, training, tooling, and cross-functional integration.

**When to use it:**
- Inheriting sales enablement with no clear picture of what exists
- Reps say collateral is out of date or hard to find
- Building a business case for enablement headcount or tooling
- Sales team growth or a merger has outpaced the existing setup
- Strong point-in-time assets exist but decay within a quarter

**Key steps:**
1. Score the four dimensions (ownership, training, tooling, integration) separately and honestly.
2. Interview five to eight front-line reps on what they actually use and cannot find.
3. Identify the lowest-scoring dimension, not the lowest-scoring stage overall, as the priority.
4. Set a realistic target of moving one stage on that dimension over two quarters.
5. Name an owner and a review cadence for the dimension being advanced.
6. Re-score quarterly and track movement, not just activity.
7. Use the scored gaps as the evidence base for the next investment ask.

**Apply it:** Sketch a 4x4 grid: the four dimensions down the side, the four stages across the top; mark today's honest score in each row and circle the one cell you'll move next quarter.

**Success metrics:**
- Dimension score movement quarter over quarter (for example, governance and measurement moving from Ad Hoc to Organised in one quarter)
- Rate at which top assets are opened during flagged live deals (one company's battlecards reached 74% usage versus 20% for old collateral)
- Win rate in deals where governed content was used (31% to 44% in the example)

## MEDDIC / MEDDPICC

**Type:** Methodology

**Core concept:** MEDDIC is a sales-owned checklist for qualifying a deal: Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, and Champion; MEDDPICC adds Paper Process and Competition. PMM does not own the methodology, but supplies the evidenced content that answers several letters: quantified proof for Metrics, comparison content for Decision Criteria, battlecard material for Competition, and shareable material a Champion can forward internally.

**When to use it:**
- Sales is rolling out or refreshing MEDDIC/MEDDPICC and needs to know what content maps to which letter
- Forecast accuracy is poor because deals were called "commit" without a confirmed Economic Buyer or Decision Criteria
- Win/loss interviews show deals stalled because Decision Criteria were never mapped against your differentiators
- A new or newly segmented rep needs a fast way to find proof, positioning, and comparison content
- An enablement audit finds collateral organised by asset type instead of by which letter it answers

**Key steps:**
1. Agree which letters PMM owns content for (Metrics, Decision Criteria, Competition, part of Champion) versus sales-run conversations (Economic Buyer, Decision Process, Paper Process, Identify Pain).
2. Build Metrics content from sourced, real customer outcomes, not internal estimates.
3. Map Decision Criteria content to the buyer's actual checklist, sourced from win/loss interviews.
4. Feed the Competition letter directly from the Competitive Battlecard Framework.
5. Build Champion-ready material a non-expert can repeat internally.
6. Train reps on exactly where to find each letter's content.
7. Feed qualification failures from deal reviews back into the content brief pipeline.

**Apply it:** Draw a simple index card with one row per letter PMM owns (Metrics, Decision Criteria, Competition, Champion); in each row, name the single asset a rep should open for that letter and its link.

**Success metrics:**
- Slip-and-loss rate on "commit" deals (one company's fell from 40% to 24% in two quarters)
- Close rate for deals with a logged, sourced Metrics figure versus without (47% versus 29% in the example)
- Proportion of deal reviews citing a specific letter as consistently weak, tracked down over successive quarters

## Competitive Battlecard Framework

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A battlecard is a one-page field tool that gives a rep everything needed to win a live competitive conversation: the competitor's real pitch, your evidenced counter-position, landmine questions, verbatim objection responses, and named proof points. It is not a research report; it exists to be read in ninety seconds between a discovery call and a demo, turning slower competitive intelligence work into something actionable mid-deal.

**When to use it:**
- Sales is losing deals to a named competitor and cannot explain why
- A competitor enters the market or repositions and reps' understanding is out of date
- Win/loss interviews surface a recurring objection reps answer inconsistently
- Onboarding new sales hires who need fast competitive grounding
- A competitor changes pricing, features, or funding in a way that shifts contested deals
- An enablement audit flags competitive collateral as missing, stale, or inconsistent

**Key steps:**
1. Confirm the competitor is faced often enough to justify a card (a useful bar is 10+ competitive deals a quarter).
2. Capture the competitor's real pitch from demos, their site, and lost-deal notes, not a strawman version.
3. Write the evidenced counter-position for each of their headline claims.
4. Plant one or two early landmine questions that expose a known weakness without naming them.
5. Write verbatim objection responses for the three to five most common objections.
6. Attach a named proof point (customer, benchmark, or third-party validation) to every claim.
7. Keep it to one page and pressure-test it with reps in a live role-play before rollout.
8. Assign an owner, store it where reps already work, and refresh it on a fixed cadence and after material competitor changes.

**Apply it:** Sketch a one-page template with five boxes: their pitch, your counter-position, landmine questions, objection scripts, and named proof points; fill each box in the competitor's own words and yours, side by side.

**Success metrics:**
- Competitive win rate against the named competitor (one company rose from 40% to 58% in a quarter)
- Sales cycle length in contested deals (fell from 61 to 44 days in the example)
- Reference-call request rate as a proxy for reps actually using the card (reached 70% of contested deals in the example)

## Buyer's Journey Content Map

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** This is a grid that plots every piece of sales and marketing content against the stages a buyer moves through (Awareness, Consideration, Decision, Post-sale), showing at a glance where content exists, is thin, or is missing. It does not create content; it audits what already exists against what a buyer actually needs, turning a vague "sales wants more collateral" complaint into a specific, prioritised gap list.

**When to use it:**
- Sales repeatedly asks for "more content" without naming what is missing
- Enabling a new segment, vertical, or persona and confirming content exists at every stage
- Win/loss interviews or Voice of the Customer research surface a recurring information gap
- Preparing a launch tier or GTM motion and confirming enablement content is ready, not just messaging
- A sales cycle stalls at an identifiable stage and a content gap, not positioning or pricing, is suspected

**Key steps:**
1. Define the buyer journey stages (default: Awareness, Consideration, Decision, Post-sale), checked against real deal data.
2. Inventory every existing content asset from the CMS, enablement platform, and shared drives.
3. Tag each asset by the single stage it best serves and by format.
4. Build the grid and read off empty or thin cells as evidenced gaps.
5. Score each stage against the objections that actually recur, not just asset count.
6. Prioritise gaps by frequency and revenue at stake, using CRM stage-drop data.
7. Brief content production against the specific named gap, not a generic content calendar slot.
8. Re-run the audit quarterly or whenever a new segment or motion launches.

**Apply it:** Draw a four-column grid (Awareness, Consideration, Decision, Post-sale); list existing assets in each column, mark thin or empty cells, and write the one recurring objection each thin cell fails to answer.

**Success metrics:**
- Asset-to-stage ratio, flagging any stage below a minimum threshold (for example, fewer than 3 assets)
- Win rate in the segment where gaps were closed (one company's mid-market win rate rose from 14% to 23%)
- Sales adoption of new assets within 90 days of publication (referenced in more than half of relevant discovery calls in the example)

## VARS Framework (Validate, Acknowledge, Reframe, Specify)

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A four-step conversational technique for handling a buyer's live question about a competitor, presented by Alex McDonnell via the Competitive Intelligence Alliance's CI: Masters programme. The four steps: Validate (learn what is on the buyer's mind before responding), Acknowledge (name the competitor's genuine strength), Reframe (shift attention to a different, more fundamental problem the competitor is not positioned to solve), and Specify (close with a concrete proof point, demo moment, or named customer outcome). The insight is that a rep who jumps straight to rebuttal turns a competitive question into an argument the buyer never asked for.

**When to use it:**
- A buyer asks directly how you compare to a named competitor and the rep's instinct is to launch into a feature list
- Competitive conversations feel like arguments rather than discovery
- A battlecard exists but reps still stumble in live competitive moments
- Win/loss shows deals lost because a rep "got defensive" or "didn't seem to know the competitor"

**Key steps:**
1. Train reps on the full four-step sequence, not just the acronym; role-play all steps together.
2. Pair VARS with the relevant Competitive Battlecard entry as the content source.
3. Practise the Validate step as a genuine question: "What's drawing you to look at [competitor]?"
4. Write the Acknowledge line for each competitor in advance from honest research.
5. Draft the Reframe as a genuine shift in the problem discussed, not a disguised feature comparison.
6. End Specify with something concrete and, where possible, visual (a demo moment or a specific outcome).
7. Review call recordings for VARS adherence, not just deal outcomes.

**Apply it:** Build a one-page cheat sheet per competitor with four boxes (Validate question, Acknowledge line, Reframe angle, Specify proof point), and role-play it once before any rep uses it live.

**Success metrics:**
- Win rate in competitive deals against the named competitor (Rosterlane, a fictional company: 34% to 49% against Shiftwise)
- VARS adherence in call recordings (20% to 71% of competitive conversations in the example)
- Rep confidence in live competitive moments, measured by reduced escalation requests
