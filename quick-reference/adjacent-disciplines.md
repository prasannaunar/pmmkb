## BCG Growth-Share Matrix

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** Plot every product or business unit by market growth rate (vertical) and relative market share against the largest competitor (horizontal), with bubbles sized by revenue. Stars (high share, fast market) need investment; cash cows (high share, slow market) fund the portfolio; question marks (low share, fast market) need an invest-or-exit decision; dogs (low share, slow market) rarely justify new investment.

**PMM's role:** Leadership owns the portfolio decision. PMM supplies market growth estimates, relative share from competitive intelligence and win/loss, and customer evidence on question marks, then aligns launch tiers and PMM capacity with each quadrant.

**Key steps:**
1. Choose one level of analysis and list the units.
2. Write one agreed market definition per unit.
3. Estimate market growth and relative share, with sources.
4. Plot, size bubbles by revenue, and mark thresholds.
5. Write a one-line strategy per quadrant, with a decision date for each question mark.
6. Map PMM effort to the quadrant strategy and flag contradictions.

**Watch for:** market definitions drawn to flatter share; treating a quadrant as destiny; using share as a profit proxy in software.

**Source:** [Bruce Henderson, "The Product Portfolio"](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio) (1970)

## Ansoff Matrix

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** Sort growth options by whether the product and the market are existing or new: market penetration (existing, existing), market development (existing product, new market), product development (new product, existing market) and diversification (new, new). Risk rises with distance from what the company already knows.

**PMM's role:** Leadership owns the growth direction. PMM sizes each target market, supplies customer and competitive evidence, and turns the chosen direction into a go-to-market plan suited to its quadrant.

**Key steps:**
1. List every growth option as one sentence naming product and market.
2. Place each by asking "do we sell this today?" and "do these buyers buy from us today?"
3. Name each option's evidence gap and size its market.
4. Recommend a mix: one low-risk penetration push plus one or two moderate moves.
5. Set a cheap test, with a success threshold, before each risky bet.
6. Match the go-to-market motion to the quadrant.

**Watch for:** calling a stretch an adjacency because code is reused; treating a quadrant as a plan; ignoring the go-to-market cost of moving.

**Source:** [H. Igor Ansoff, "Strategies for Diversification"](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/strategies-for-diversification-ansoff-igor/10001836084), *Harvard Business Review* (1957)

## Porter's Five Forces

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** Judge how much profit a market lets its companies keep through five forces: rivalry among competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power. Strong forces push value to customers, suppliers or newcomers; weak forces let incumbents keep more. It describes industry structure, not one company's merits.

**PMM's role:** Strategy, the CEO or founders own the enter, stay or exit decision. PMM supplies evidence from competitive intelligence, win/loss and customer research, and applies the result to positioning against substitutes, pricing headroom and sales briefings.

**Key steps:**
1. Define one product market and geography precisely.
2. Gather sourced evidence for each force.
3. Rate each force low, medium or high, with two or three pieces of evidence.
4. Mark which forces are strengthening or weakening.
5. Write one paragraph on overall attractiveness.
6. Identify the positioning response to each strong force.

**Watch for:** listing forces without rating them (the same weakness as a SWOT); confusing market attractiveness with your own position; missing substitutes outside the category.

**Source:** [Michael E. Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy"](https://hbr.org/1979/03/how-competitive-forces-shape-strategy), *Harvard Business Review* (1979)

## Business Model Canvas

**Type:** Framework

**Core concept:** A one-page map of how a business makes money in nine blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. The right side shows how value is created for customers; the left side shows what is needed to deliver it.

**PMM's role:** Leadership owns the business model. PMM contributes the customer-facing blocks (segments, value propositions, channels, relationships) and flags which customer assumptions are untested.

**Key steps:**
1. Map one business model per canvas.
2. Start with customer segments, then value propositions per segment.
3. Add channels, relationships and revenue streams.
4. Have product, operations and finance complete the left side.
5. Mark each item proven, partly proven or assumed.
6. Turn assumed customer-side items into PMM's test agenda; update when a block changes.

**Watch for:** canvases written without customer evidence; several models on one canvas; treating the canvas as finished.

**Source:** [Alexander Osterwalder & Yves Pigneur, *Business Model Generation*](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Business+Model+Generation%3A+A+Handbook+for+Visionaries%2C+Game+Changers%2C+and+Challengers-p-9780470876411) (2010)

## Cialdini's Principles of Persuasion

**Type:** Model

**Core concept:** Seven principles that describe why people say yes: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity. A PMM uses them as a lens for auditing messaging, proof and buying journeys, not as a process to run.

**PMM's role:** No single function owns the principles. PMM keeps their use truthful and consistent with positioning, sets the proof strategy, and supplies customer evidence, validation and segment identity.

**Key steps:**
1. Pick one asset and the decision you want the buyer to make.
2. Audit it against all seven principles, checking each claim is true.
3. Match principles to the buyer's main hesitation from win/loss evidence.
4. Source honest proof for the chosen principles.
5. Strengthen two or three principles, then test the change.
6. Record which levers work for each segment.

**Watch for:** fabricated scarcity or proof; stacking every principle on one page; generic proof the buyer does not identify with.

**Source:** [Robert B. Cialdini, *Influence*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3902892W) (1984; expanded edition 2021)
