# Quiz: Porter's Five Forces

1. A project management software company is losing deals, and the sales team blames its main competitor. Win/loss data shows that 55% of lost deals chose to keep using spreadsheets and email. Which force is the analysis missing?
   - **A.** The threat of substitutes, which caps price and wins deals outside the category.
   - **B.** Rivalry among existing competitors, which is strongest when products look alike.
   - **C.** The threat of new entrants, which rises when barriers to entering a market are low.
   - **D.** The bargaining power of buyers, which grows when buyers are large and concentrated.

   **Correct answer: A.** Spreadsheets and email solve the same job by a different means, which makes them substitutes. Teams that list only category rivals miss the alternatives that often take more deals, so win/loss data on what lost deals chose is the right evidence.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: rivalry is a real force here, but the evidence points away from named competitors. Over half the losses went to an alternative outside the category.

   *Why not C:* New entrants are companies about to enter the market. Spreadsheets and email are long-established alternatives, not newcomers.

   *Why not D:* Buyer power concerns buyers' leverage on price and terms. The scenario describes buyers choosing a different solution altogether.

2. A PMM's five forces slide lists the main competitors, the largest customers and the cloud provider the company relies on, but gives no ratings. Leadership says the slide does not help them decide anything. What should the PMM change?
   - **A.** Rate each force with evidence and end with one paragraph on overall attractiveness.
   - **B.** Add a SWOT analysis alongside it, to cover the internal strengths and weaknesses too.
   - **C.** Add more competitors and suppliers, so that no possible force is left off the slide.
   - **D.** Replace the slide with a competitor battlecard that sales teams can use in deals.

   **Correct answer: A.** A five forces analysis only supports a decision when each force is rated low, medium or high with evidence, and the forces are judged together. Listing participants without judging their strength produces no conclusion.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it adds analysis, but a SWOT suffers from the same listing problem and would not supply the missing ratings. The fix is judgement, not another framework.

   *Why not C:* More names make the list longer without making it more useful. The gap is the absence of ratings and a conclusion, not missing participants.

   *Why not D:* A battlecard serves sales in individual deals. Leadership asked about market attractiveness, which needs an industry-level judgement.

3. Market research shows an industry with weak forces and healthy margins, but the company's own product is poorly differentiated and losing share. A board member says "the market is attractive, so we're fine". What should the PMM point out?
   - **A.** Industry attractiveness and the company's own position are separate questions.
   - **B.** The analysis must be wrong, and the forces should be re-rated with more data.
   - **C.** Weak forces guarantee healthy margins for every competitor in the industry.
   - **D.** The company should leave the market and move to one where the forces are weaker.

   **Correct answer: A.** Five forces describes the structure of an industry, not the merits of one company's product. An attractive market can still punish a poorly positioned company, so the analysis needs pairing with positioning and differentiation work.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: checking the evidence is sensible, but nothing suggests the ratings are wrong. The board member's error is confusing two different questions.

   *Why not C:* Weak forces raise the profit potential of the industry, but individual companies still win or lose share. Margins are not guaranteed for a poorly differentiated product.

   *Why not D:* The market is already attractive. The problem is the company's position within it, which repositioning can address.

4. A founder is writing an expansion case for entering the small accounting firm market and asks the PMM to help. Which contribution best fits PMM's role in this model?
   - **A.** Evidence on rivals, substitutes and buyer leverage from CI and win/loss work.
   - **B.** The final decision on whether to enter, signed off by PMM on the founder's behalf.
   - **C.** The supplier contract terms and cloud hosting costs, negotiated with each vendor.
   - **D.** The enter-or-stay decision, made by PMM once the analysis has been reviewed.

   **Correct answer: A.** PMM supplies most of the evidence: competitive intelligence on rivals and entrants, win/loss data on substitutes, and customer research on buyer power. The decision to enter belongs to the founder or leadership.

   *Why not B:* Signing off an entry decision on someone's behalf is not PMM's role. The decision sits with the founder, who owns the investment case.

   *Why not C:* Supplier negotiations belong to operations, procurement or finance. PMM may note supplier power in the analysis but does not negotiate the contracts.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: it correctly places the decision after the analysis, but gives it to the wrong owner. PMM informs the call rather than making it.

5. A strategy team defines its industry as "all business software worldwide" for a five forces analysis. The ratings come out vague and nobody can act on them. What went wrong?
   - **A.** The industry was defined too broadly, which blurs the forces that actually apply.
   - **B.** The team should have rated only two forces, to keep the analysis short and clear.
   - **C.** The team defined the industry correctly, but used analyst data that was too old.
   - **D.** The team should have defined the industry by the features that its product offers.

   **Correct answer: A.** Porter warned that defining the industry too broadly or too narrowly is the commonest error, because it hides or invents forces. The method starts with one product market and geography.

   *Why not B:* Rating only some forces misses the point that the weakest overall structure sets profit potential. All five need assessing for one well-defined market.

   *Why not C:* Fresher data would not fix an industry definition that mixes unrelated markets. The definition is the problem, not the data's age.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: it tries to narrow the definition, but by the wrong criterion. An industry is defined by the product market and the buyers in it, not by one vendor's feature list.
