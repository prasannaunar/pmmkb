# Cialdini's Principles of Persuasion

**What it is:** A set of seven principles that describe why people say yes to a request, drawn from decades of research in social psychology. Robert Cialdini, a psychology professor at Arizona State University, set out six of them in *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984), after years of studying both laboratory experiments and the methods of salespeople, fundraisers and advertisers. He added a seventh, unity, in later work, and the 2021 expanded edition of *Influence* covers all seven. The principles are **reciprocity** (people return favours), **commitment and consistency** (people act in line with what they have already said or done), **social proof** (people follow what similar others do), **authority** (people defer to credible experts), **liking** (people say yes to those they like and who resemble them), **scarcity** (people value what is rare or running out), and **unity** (people favour those who share an identity with them). It is a model, not a method: it describes the levers that shape decisions, and a PMM uses it as a lens for checking messaging, proof and buying journeys rather than as a process to run.

**When to use it:**
- **Reviewing a website, pitch deck or launch message** that is accurate but not persuasive, to see which principles it relies on and which it ignores.
- **Building a proof strategy**, such as choosing between customer logos, analyst endorsements and peer reviews, where social proof, authority and unity each point to a different asset.
- **Designing a trial, freemium or onboarding journey**, where small early commitments and genuine reciprocity shape whether users convert.
- **Message testing produces a winner nobody can explain**, and the team wants a vocabulary for why one variant outperformed another.
- **Sales asks for help with stalled deals**, where buyers agree in principle but do not act, a sign that commitment or scarcity is missing.

**PMM's role:** The principles come from behavioural science, and no single function owns them: copywriters, demand generation, growth teams and sales all apply them. PMM's role is to make sure the principles are used truthfully and consistently with the positioning, and to supply the raw material they need: customer evidence for social proof, credible third-party validation for authority, and the shared identity of the target segment for unity. At a start-up, the founding PMM often applies them directly while writing the website and sales deck. At a scaled company, PMM sets the proof strategy and the messaging hierarchy, and reviews how demand generation and sales apply the principles so the claims stay honest.

**How to read it:**
- **Reciprocity:** give something of real value first, such as a useful benchmark report or a free tool, without strings. It works when the gift is genuinely useful and personal; generic content gated behind a form rarely triggers it.
- **Commitment and consistency:** small, voluntary, public commitments lead to larger ones. A free trial user who configures one workflow is more likely to buy than one who only signs up.
- **Social proof:** people look to similar others when unsure. Proof works best when the buyer recognises the customer as like them: same industry, same size, same role.
- **Authority:** credible expertise reduces perceived risk. Analyst recognition, security certifications and named experts all carry it, provided they are relevant to the buyer's concern.
- **Liking:** buyers favour people and brands they find similar, warm and cooperative. Tone of voice, shared values and genuine praise all contribute.
- **Scarcity:** limited availability raises perceived value. It only persuades ethically when the limit is real, such as a capped beta or a genuine price change date.
- **Unity:** shared identity ("people like us") is stronger than similarity. Communities, user groups and segment-specific language build it.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Pick the asset and the decision.** Choose one asset (a homepage, a deck, a trial journey) and name the decision you want the buyer to make.
2. **Audit it against all seven principles.** For each principle, note where the asset uses it, how strongly, and whether the claim behind it is true and evidenced.
3. **Match principles to the buyer's hesitation.** Use win/loss and sales call evidence to find the main reason buyers hesitate. Risk concerns call for authority and social proof; inertia calls for commitment and scarcity.
4. **Source honest proof.** Gather the customer stories, data and endorsements that each chosen principle needs. Never invent urgency or inflate numbers.
5. **Rewrite and test.** Revise the asset to strengthen two or three principles, not all seven, and test the change with message testing or an A/B test.
6. **Record what works for each segment.** Different segments respond to different levers; log the results so future messaging starts from evidence.

**Example:** Fictional data privacy software company Consentry had a free trial that converted at 4%. The PMM audited the trial journey against the seven principles. The homepage used authority (two certifications) but no social proof from companies of similar size; the trial asked users to commit to nothing; and the pricing page used a countdown timer for a discount that reset every week. Win/loss interviews showed buyers hesitated because they feared choosing a vendor that would not pass their own customers' audits. The PMM added logos and short quotes from 12 mid-sized companies in the same industries, a first-day task asking trial users to scan one website (a small commitment), and a free audit-readiness checklist (reciprocity). The fake countdown timer was removed. Trial conversion rose from 4% to 7% over the following quarter, with no fall in conversion after the timer came out.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Fabricating scarcity or social proof.** Fake countdown timers, invented user numbers and stock-photo testimonials work briefly and then damage trust, and they can breach consumer protection law. Recovery: use only real limits and verifiable proof, and remove any claim you could not show a sceptical customer evidence for.
- **Stacking every principle onto one page.** A page that combines urgency banners, badges, logos, quotes and a free gift reads as manipulative and dilutes the message. Recovery: choose the two or three principles that address the buyer's main hesitation and drop the rest.
- **Using generic proof.** Logos and quotes from companies the buyer does not identify with trigger little social proof or unity. Recovery: segment proof assets by industry, size and role, and show each segment the customers most like them.
