# Quiz: Cialdini's Principles of Persuasion

1. A cybersecurity vendor's homepage shows logos of three global banks, but its target buyers are IT leads at 200-person manufacturing firms. Conversion is low. Which change best applies the principles?
   - **A.** Show proof from mid-sized manufacturers the target buyers recognise as like them.
   - **B.** Add an analyst endorsement beside the bank logos to strengthen the page's authority.
   - **C.** Add a countdown timer for a discount, so buyers feel urgency to act straight away.
   - **D.** Add a long list of every customer logo, so the page shows the most social proof.

   **Correct answer: A.** Social proof works best when buyers recognise the customer as similar to them: same industry, same size, same role. Logos from very different companies trigger little social proof or unity.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: authority is a real principle and can reduce perceived risk, but it does not fix the mismatch the scenario describes. The proof is from the wrong peers.

   *Why not C:* A timer applies scarcity, but it does not address why buyers are unconvinced. Invented urgency also risks damaging trust.

   *Why not D:* More logos from dissimilar companies add volume, not relevance. Social proof depends on similarity, not quantity.

2. A SaaS pricing page shows a "48 hours left" discount timer that resets every week. Trials convert slightly better, but sales hears buyers mention the timer with suspicion. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Remove the timer and use only real limits, such as a genuine price change date.
   - **B.** Keep the timer, but extend it to seven days so it feels more believable to buyers.
   - **C.** Keep the timer and add more social proof, to offset the buyers' suspicion of it.
   - **D.** Replace the timer with a claim that stock is limited, which buyers cannot check.

   **Correct answer: A.** Fabricated scarcity works briefly, then damages trust, and it can breach consumer protection law. Scarcity only persuades ethically when the limit is real.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it notices the credibility problem, but keeps a false limit. A longer fake deadline is still fake.

   *Why not C:* Adding proof does not repair a dishonest claim. Stacking principles on a page that already feels manipulative makes the problem worse.

   *Why not D:* Unverifiable scarcity is still invented scarcity. It shifts the deception rather than removing it.

3. A free trial converts at 3%. Analytics show most users sign up and never configure anything. Which principle does the journey most clearly fail to use?
   - **A.** Commitment and consistency, through a small first task in the trial.
   - **B.** Reciprocity, through a free benchmark report emailed to users on day one.
   - **C.** Authority, through security certifications displayed on the sign-up page.
   - **D.** Liking, through a friendlier tone of voice in every onboarding message.

   **Correct answer: A.** Small, voluntary commitments lead to larger ones. A user who configures one workflow is more likely to buy than one who only signs up, so the trial needs an early, easy first task.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: reciprocity can help trials, but a gift does not get users to act inside the product. The evidence points to missing early commitment.

   *Why not C:* Authority reduces perceived risk before sign-up. These users have already signed up; the drop-off happens after.

   *Why not D:* Tone matters, but users who never configure anything are not put off by tone. They lack a first step that builds commitment.

4. A demand generation team redesigns a landing page with an urgency banner, five trust badges, twelve logos, three testimonials and a free gift. Conversion falls. What does the entry recommend?
   - **A.** Choose the two or three principles that address buyers' main hesitation.
   - **B.** Keep every element, but run an A/B test to find the best page order for each.
   - **C.** Remove all persuasion elements and return to a plain description of features.
   - **D.** Add a reciprocity offer at the top, so the free gift becomes the main message.

   **Correct answer: A.** Stacking every principle onto one page reads as manipulative and dilutes the message. The method is to match principles to the buyer's main hesitation, using win/loss and sales evidence, and drop the rest.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: testing is the right habit, but testing the order of an overloaded page keeps the core problem. The fix is choosing fewer principles first.

   *Why not C:* Removing all persuasion throws away levers that help buyers decide. The problem is excess, not the principles themselves.

   *Why not D:* Promoting one element still leaves the page overloaded. It does not tie the principles to what makes buyers hesitate.

5. At a scaled company, demand generation, sales and growth teams all apply persuasion techniques in their own work. What is PMM's most appropriate role?
   - **A.** Set the proof strategy and review how teams apply it so claims stay honest.
   - **B.** Approve every campaign before launch and own all persuasion copy company-wide.
   - **C.** Leave persuasion to each team, as it falls outside the scope of product marketing.
   - **D.** Write a persuasion playbook once, then hand it to each team to use as they see fit.

   **Correct answer: A.** No single function owns the principles. PMM keeps them consistent with positioning and truthful, and supplies the raw material: customer evidence, credible validation and segment identity.

   *Why not B:* Owning all persuasion copy would make PMM a bottleneck and take on work other functions do. PMM sets direction and reviews; it does not own every asset.

   *Why not C:* Stepping away leaves claims unchecked against positioning and evidence. PMM has a clear role in proof and honesty.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: a playbook helps, but handing it over without review drops the consistency check. PMM's role continues after the guidance is written.
