# Quiz: Business Model Canvas

1. A company sells an enterprise product through a direct sales team and a self-serve product with monthly card payments. The strategy team maps both on one Business Model Canvas, and every block ends up vague. What should the PMM suggest?
   - **A.** Draw one canvas per way of making money and compare them side by side.
   - **B.** Keep one canvas, but colour-code each item by the product it applies to.
   - **C.** Map only the enterprise product, as it brings in most of today's revenue.
   - **D.** Replace the canvas with a Value Proposition Canvas for the two products.

   **Correct answer: A.** A self-serve and an enterprise product usually represent two business models, with different segments, channels, relationships and revenue streams. Blending them produces blocks so broad they say nothing.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it recognises the two models but keeps them on one sheet. Colour-coding still forces shared blocks, which hides how differently each model works.

   *Why not C:* Ignoring the self-serve model leaves half the business unmapped. Its channels and costs differ most from the enterprise model, which is exactly what the canvas should reveal.

   *Why not D:* The Value Proposition Canvas zooms in on two blocks only. It cannot show channels, revenue streams or cost structure, which is what leadership needs here.

2. Leadership fills in a Business Model Canvas for a new product line in a two-hour workshop, without customer research, and asks finance to budget against it. What is the main risk?
   - **A.** The customer-side blocks record beliefs, not evidence, and need testing.
   - **B.** The left-hand blocks were rushed, so cost and resource figures are too rough.
   - **C.** Two hours is too short, and the workshop should be extended to a full day.
   - **D.** Finance should have led the workshop, so the canvas matches the budget model.

   **Correct answer: A.** A canvas written inside the building records assumptions about segments, value propositions and channels. The recovery is to mark every customer-side item with its evidence and treat the rest as hypotheses for PMM to test before funding.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: it spots untested content, but on the wrong side of the canvas. The biggest risk in a new product line is usually whether customers want it, which sits on the right-hand side.

   *Why not C:* A longer workshop without customer evidence produces a longer list of beliefs. Time is not the missing input.

   *Why not D:* Finance contributes cost structure, but leading the workshop would not add customer evidence. The gap is on the customer side.

3. Leadership plans to add a self-serve tier for small businesses to a product sold through direct sales. Which part of the canvas should the PMM contribute most to?
   - **A.** Customer segments, value propositions, channels and customer relationships.
   - **B.** Key resources, key activities and key partnerships for the new tier's operations.
   - **C.** The cost structure, including hosting, support and payment processing costs.
   - **D.** The revenue streams and pricing, set by PMM as the final pricing decision.

   **Correct answer: A.** PMM holds the strongest evidence on the right-hand side of the canvas: segments from ICP work, value propositions from positioning, and channels from go-to-market planning. The business model decision belongs to leadership.

   *Why not B:* These operational blocks are filled in by product, operations and finance. PMM checks them for consistency but does not own them.

   *Why not C:* Cost structure comes from finance. PMM may flag customer-driven costs, such as support load, but does not build the figures.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: PMM often informs pricing and revenue streams, but the final pricing and business model decision sits with leadership.

4. A company moves from annual licences to usage-based pricing. Six months later, sales complains that the commission plan and onboarding process no longer fit. Which practice would most likely have caught this?
   - **A.** Updating the canvas when revenue changed, then checking the linked blocks.
   - **B.** Running a pricing study before the change to set the right usage-based price.
   - **C.** Writing a new positioning statement to explain usage-based pricing to buyers.
   - **D.** Asking sales to review the new pricing page before it went live on the site.

   **Correct answer: A.** The canvas's strength is showing how a change in one block ripples into the others. A revenue model change affects channels, customer relationships and cost structure, so updating the canvas and checking linked blocks would have exposed the commission and onboarding gaps.

   *Why not B:* This is the near miss: a pricing study is valuable, but it answers what to charge, not what else must change. The knock-on effects on sales and onboarding sit outside its scope.

   *Why not C:* Positioning helps buyers understand the change, but it does not reveal internal effects on commission plans or onboarding.

   *Why not D:* A pricing page review catches presentation issues. It would not surface the structural changes the new revenue model requires.

5. A PMM joins a company that sells payroll software and has three months to plan positioning and launches. Nobody can explain in one picture how the business makes money. What is a sensible first step?
   - **A.** Map the business on a canvas with leadership and mark which items are proven.
   - **B.** Write a new positioning statement and message house for the core product line.
   - **C.** Build a launch calendar for the year, based on the product team's roadmap.
   - **D.** Interview customers on their jobs, pains and gains for a value proposition map.

   **Correct answer: A.** Joining a company is one of the entry's stated triggers. A canvas gives a quick, shared picture of segments, channels and revenue, and marking proven versus assumed items shows PMM where its research should start.

   *Why not B:* Positioning is downstream of understanding the business model. Writing it first risks targeting segments or channels the business does not actually rely on.

   *Why not C:* A launch calendar commits effort before the PMM understands how the business works. It is a later step, not a first one.

   *Why not D:* This is the near miss: customer interviews are valuable, but they zoom in on two blocks. The PMM first needs the whole-business view the canvas provides.
