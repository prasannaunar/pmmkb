# Quiz: BCG Growth-Share Matrix

1. A software company plots its portfolio and finds a recently launched analytics product with a relative market share of 0.3 in a market growing 20% a year. The CEO asks the PMM what the matrix says to do next. What is the best recommendation?
   - **A.** Set a dated invest-or-exit decision, backed by evidence it can win share.
   - **B.** Fund it at star level now, so it keeps pace with its fast-growing market this year.
   - **C.** Harvest it for cash and cut new spending while defending its existing customers.
   - **D.** Divest it this quarter and move its customers across to the core product line.

   **Correct answer: A.** Low share in a fast-growing market makes this a question mark, and question marks need a deliberate decision: invest enough to win share, or exit. The entry asks for a named decision date so the product does not drift between the two.

   *Why not B:* This is the right family of response, investment, but it skips the decision. Funding a question mark as if it were already a star commits money before anyone has tested whether it can actually gain share.

   *Why not C:* Harvesting is the strategy for cash cows, which have high share in slow markets. A product with low share in a fast market generates little cash to harvest.

   *Why not D:* Divesting is the usual strategy for dogs in slow markets. In a market growing 20% a year, exiting without assessing the product's chances throws away a growth option.

2. A product team presents its payroll product as a star. Their slide defines the market as "payroll software for UK logistics firms with 200 to 500 staff", where they hold 45% share. What has gone wrong?
   - **A.** The market was defined narrowly enough to inflate the product's relative share.
   - **B.** The growth threshold was set too low, so a slow market was counted as fast-growing.
   - **C.** The bubbles were sized by revenue, when the matrix calls for profit-based sizing.
   - **D.** The matrix was applied to a single product, when it suits whole business units.

   **Correct answer: A.** Market definition is the step most likely to distort the matrix. A narrow slice can turn any product into a share leader; the recovery is one market definition per product, agreed with finance before plotting.

   *Why not B:* This is a near miss: it is also an axis-setting error, but it concerns the growth axis. The scenario's problem sits on the share axis, created by how tightly the market was drawn.

   *Why not C:* Revenue is the conventional bubble size and is what the entry recommends. Changing the bubble size would not fix a share figure that rests on a flattering market definition.

   *Why not D:* The matrix works at product, product line or business unit level, provided you stay at one level. Applying it to a product is not the error here.

3. A growth-stage company's CEO asks the senior PMM to help with a portfolio review across three products. Which contribution best fits PMM's role in this model?
   - **A.** Sourced growth and relative share estimates, plus customer evidence on each product.
   - **B.** The final quadrant strategy for each product, presented to the board as PMM's decision.
   - **C.** The cost-of-goods and margin figures for each product line, drawn from the ledger.
   - **D.** The revenue forecasts finance will use to set next year's budget for each product.

   **Correct answer: A.** PMM holds the market sizing, competitive intelligence and win/loss evidence that the two axes depend on. The portfolio decision itself belongs to the CEO, CFO or strategy team.

   *Why not B:* This is a near miss: PMM may well draft the matrix and recommend strategies, but the decision belongs to leadership. Presenting it as PMM's own decision confuses contribution with ownership.

   *Why not C:* Cost and margin data come from finance. PMM can ask for them to be plotted alongside share, but it is not the source.

   *Why not D:* Budget forecasts are finance's responsibility. The matrix informs where investment should go, but PMM's distinct input is market and customer evidence.

4. A SaaS company's market-leading product has a relative share of 1.5 but thin margins, while a smaller product with strong network effects earns high margins. Leadership wants to use the matrix to set investment. What should the PMM advise?
   - **A.** Add margin or customer lifetime value beside share before deciding on investment.
   - **B.** Re-run the matrix next quarter with refreshed share data, then decide on investment.
   - **C.** Treat the leading product as a star and raise its marketing budget for this year.
   - **D.** Divest the smaller product and fold its customers into the market-leading product.

   **Correct answer: A.** Henderson linked share to cash through experience-curve cost advantages, which matter less in software than network effects and switching costs. Plotting margin or lifetime value alongside share stops the matrix from rewarding the wrong product.

   *Why not B:* This is a near miss: refreshing data is good practice, but new share figures would show the same pattern. The missing evidence is profitability, not more recent share.

   *Why not C:* High share alone does not make a product a star, which also needs a fast-growing market. Raising its budget on share alone repeats the error the scenario describes.

   *Why not D:* Low share does not mean low value when margins are high. Divesting a profitable product on share alone treats the quadrant as destiny.

5. After a portfolio review, leadership classifies a legacy product as a dog and moves it to maintenance. Three months later it still takes a quarter of PMM's launch capacity because two large customers keep asking for releases. What should the PMM do?
   - **A.** Show leadership where PMM effort contradicts the agreed strategy and propose a split.
   - **B.** Cut all launch support for the product at once, without raising it with leadership first.
   - **C.** Keep the current split to protect the two large customers' renewals this coming year.
   - **D.** Commission a full rebrand of the product to lift its market share before changing effort.

   **Correct answer: A.** The final step of the method is translating the quadrant strategy into PMM plans and showing where current effort contradicts it. Leadership made the call; PMM's job is to align its capacity with it and make the trade-off visible.

   *Why not B:* This is a near miss: the direction is right, but acting alone skips the people who own the decision and the customer relationships. A visible, agreed reallocation avoids surprising sales or the two accounts.

   *Why not C:* Protecting two accounts at the cost of the portfolio strategy is exactly the drift the review was meant to stop. Their needs can be met through the migration path, not full launch support.

   *Why not D:* Leadership has already decided on maintenance. A rebrand would spend more effort on a product the business has chosen not to grow.
