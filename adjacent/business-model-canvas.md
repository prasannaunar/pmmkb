# Business Model Canvas

**What it is:** A one-page template that describes how a business makes money, in nine blocks. Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur developed it from Osterwalder's doctoral research and published it in *Business Model Generation* (2010), a book co-created with 470 practitioners. The nine blocks are **customer segments**, **value propositions**, **channels**, **customer relationships**, **revenue streams**, **key resources**, **key activities**, **key partnerships** and **cost structure**. The right-hand side of the canvas describes how the business creates value for customers; the left-hand side describes what the business needs to deliver that value efficiently. Its strength is that it fits a whole business model on one sheet, so a leadership team can see how a change in one block (a new segment, a new pricing model) affects the others. The Value Proposition Canvas, already in this knowledge base, was designed by the same team as a zoom-in on two of these blocks: customer segments and value propositions.

**When to use it:**
- **A new business line or product is proposed** and leadership needs to see the whole model, not just the product, before funding it.
- **The pricing or revenue model is changing**, such as moving from licences to subscriptions or adding usage-based pricing, and the knock-on effects on channels, costs and customer relationships need mapping.
- **A move into a new segment or channel** where the existing model may not carry over, such as adding a partner channel or a self-serve tier.
- **PMM joins a company or a new product line** and needs a quick, shared picture of how the business works before planning positioning and launches.
- **Strategy documents need a common language** between product, marketing, sales and finance about what the business actually depends on.

**PMM's role:** The business model belongs to the CEO and leadership team; changes to it, such as a new revenue stream or a new channel, are their decision. PMM's contribution is concentrated on the right-hand side of the canvas, where it holds the strongest evidence: customer segments (from segmentation and ICP work), value propositions (from positioning), channels (from go-to-market planning) and customer relationships. PMM also flags where the model's assumptions about customers are untested. At a start-up, the founding PMM often fills in the customer-facing blocks with the founders in a single workshop; at a scaled company, PMM reviews a strategy or finance team's canvas and challenges its customer assumptions.

**How to apply it:**
1. **Pick one business model to map.** Map one product line or business unit at a time. A company with a self-serve product and an enterprise product usually has two business models, and blending them hides the differences.
2. **Start with customer segments.** Name the segments the model serves, ideally the ones STP and ICP work already defined. Distinguish between users and paying customers where they differ.
3. **Fill in the value proposition for each segment.** Write the job, pain or gain each segment pays to solve, using your positioning work. Where segments differ, write separate propositions.
4. **Map channels and customer relationships.** Record how each segment discovers, buys and gets support, and the kind of relationship you maintain (self-serve, dedicated account manager, community).
5. **Add revenue streams.** Describe how each segment pays: subscription, usage, licence, services. Note the pricing model and its main metric.
6. **Complete the left side with the relevant teams.** Ask product, operations and finance to fill in key resources, activities, partnerships and costs. PMM's job here is to check consistency, not to own the numbers.
7. **Mark the untested assumptions.** Colour-code each item as proven, partly proven or assumed. The assumed items on the customer side become PMM's research and testing agenda.
8. **Revisit the canvas when a block changes.** When pricing, channels or target segments move, update the canvas and check what else must move with it.

**Example:** Fictional cybersecurity training company Phishwise sold annual licences to mid-market IT teams through a direct sales team. Leadership proposed a self-serve tier for small businesses, priced monthly. The PMM led a canvas workshop with the CEO, head of sales and finance lead. Mapping the new model on its own canvas revealed knock-on changes the proposal had missed: small businesses had no IT team, so the value proposition had to change from "train your users" to "stay compliant without an IT team"; the channel had to be online, with no sales involvement; customer relationships had to move to automated onboarding; and the cost structure needed far lower support cost per account. Three assumptions were marked untested, including whether small firms would pay monthly. PMM ran a concept test and a pricing page experiment over six weeks. Monthly pricing at £3 per user converted, but only with a compliance certificate included. The tier launched with that change and reached 1,400 paying accounts in its first nine months.

**Pitfalls:**
- **Filling in the canvas from inside the building.** A canvas written in a leadership workshop without customer evidence records beliefs, not a business model. Recovery: mark every customer-side item with its evidence, and treat unmarked items as hypotheses for PMM to test before the plan is funded.
- **Mapping several business models on one canvas.** Mixing a self-serve and an enterprise model produces blocks so broad they say nothing. Recovery: draw one canvas per distinct way of making money, and compare them side by side.
- **Treating the canvas as a finished deliverable.** Teams draw it once for a strategy offsite and never update it, so it drifts from reality. Recovery: assign an owner in leadership, and have PMM prompt an update whenever pricing, channels or target segments change.
